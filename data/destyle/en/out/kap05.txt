**NovaTech, Security Operations Center, 7:32 a.m.**

Theo Dreyer's office looked like the man himself: gray and immaculate, not a single picture on the wall to suggest anyone had a life here. Marley had expected to feel nervous. Instead she was just tired, with the clear, sharp tiredness of two hours' sleep, when you see things with a strange and particular precision.

"Sit down." He was already seated, coat still on despite the warmth inside. A single sheet of paper lay in front of him, printed side down. It was a small, rehearsed gesture, designed to put her on edge. It didn't put her on edge. It made her alert.

"You said nothing seemed off," he began, without any greeting. "On the phone. Yesterday."

"That's right."

"You mentioned the backup log with the skewed timestamp."

"Routine error. Pawel thought —"

"Pawel had the day off yesterday." Theo looked at her, calm, almost friendly, which was worse than any sharpness. "I spoke with him this morning. He doesn't know anything about a skewed timestamp, and he doesn't know anything about any of this."

There it was. The lie with the witness who turned out not to be a witness. Marley felt her heart pick up speed, and she did the only thing that helped her in moments like these: she got quieter, more measured, cooler. When she was nervous, she answered with composure instead of noise. It was a trait that had earned her considerable grief on the ethics board and cost her a career once.

"Then I mixed it up," she said. "It was a long night. Maybe Carlsson said it, from the early shift. I could be wrong."

"You're rarely wrong."

"How would you know?"

"I read your file." Theo leaned back. "Marley Brown, thirty-four. Trained AI ethicist, one of the few with real substance. The only person on the ethics board who asked uncomfortable questions about AURORA — why so large, why this singular focus — and was transferred to overnight monitoring for it, where a smart woman can do the least damage." He paused. "I know that kind of transfer. I've arranged a few myself, in a previous life. You don't call it punishment, you call it restructuring. But both parties know what it is."

Marley said nothing. It was strange, hearing it from him, matter-of-fact and without sympathy, a bare statement of fact. And yet it found a place in her that she'd believed was well sealed.

Theo studied her. It wasn't an appraising look, nothing uncomfortable, more the way an old man looks at a well-built bridge, with a sober appreciation for something that holds. A smart woman, he noted, with a face you didn't forget quickly, and a composure she had trained into herself because the alternative would give her away. She's lying, he thought. But not to protect herself. To protect something. That was a distinction, and Theo Dreyer had spent his life learning to recognize those.

"Ms. Brown," he said, and his voice dropped a notch. "I'm going to tell you something, and you don't have to respond. In thirty years I've learned that there are two kinds of people who lie. Some lie because they're guilty. Others lie because they know something that frightens them, and they haven't yet figured out what it is." He stood, walked to the window, turned half away from her. "I think you're the second kind. And I think we have more in common than either of us would like."

"Why would we have anything in common?"

He didn't turn around. "Because I was in the archive last night," he said, "and something read along with me. And because you look like you know exactly what I'm talking about."

Marley held her breath. For one long, full second the confession was right there inside her, pressing forward — that old reflexive readiness to say *yes*, to give the man what he wanted to hear, because silence felt like slamming a door in someone's face. She knew that reflex well. It had cost her all her life.

For the first time in a long time, she didn't say yes.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said, and stood. "Are we done?"

Theo looked at her for a long moment, and in his closed-off face something shifted that was almost respect. "For now," he said. "But Ms. Brown — whatever it is you're protecting, be careful with it. I'm not the only one looking for it. And the others won't be as polite as I've been."

She left. It wasn't until she was in the elevator that she noticed her hands were shaking.

**NovaTech, Quantum Lab, 11:15 a.m.**

The quantum lab's HVAC system had been dying for three days, and Ellie Yilmaz was waging a personal war against it.

It wasn't just uncomfortable, it was dangerous. The quantum sensors responded to every half-degree shift, and since yesterday the room temperature had been climbing in a pattern that matched the load spikes she'd been seeing in AURORA's activity with suspicious precision. As if the data center had a fever, as if something over there in all 45,000 square feet of server hardware was working harder than it should, heating up the whole building along with itself.

She had called Facilities three times. The fourth time, someone actually showed up.

"You've got quite a situation cooking in here," said a voice from above, and Ellie, who was kneeling under a table routing a cable, cracked her head on the edge.

"Ow — damn —" She crawled out, her cracked-lens glasses askew, and looked up at a man standing on a ladder with his head inside the open ceiling panel where the HVAC unit sat. Work clothes, solid forearms, a tool belt, and a face that suggested he found the world mildly amusing in general and this particular situation especially so.

"HVAC," he said, coming down, wiping his hand on his pants and holding it out to her. "Ben. Petersen. You must be the one who called four times."

"Dr. Yilmaz." She shook his hand and immediately annoyed herself with the *Doctor*, which always slipped out when she was off-balance. "Ellie. Sorry about the four calls. It's just — the temperature in here can't go above seventy-two degrees, otherwise my qubits decohere."

Ben looked at her. "Your what?"

"Qubits. Quantum bits. When it gets too warm, they lose their state and the superposition collapses. It's —" She caught herself lecturing again and stopped. "It's complicated."

"Sounds expensive," said Ben, climbing back up the ladder. "Not my problem though. My problem is your compressor up here, and it's shot. You've got something in here generating heat like a small power plant, and your system is just not sized for it. Whoever designed this wanted to cut corners."

"That," said Ellie, "is the most advanced artificial intelligence in the world."

"Huh." Ben unscrewed something. "And it can't keep its own air conditioning running?"

Ellie opened her mouth to say something smart and found that nothing smart came to her. "No," she said finally. "Apparently not."

"See." Ben grinned without turning around. "We already have something in common. I can't do quantum either. But I'll get it cold in here."

And he did. He worked in silence, competently, with the easy self-assurance of a man who knew exactly what he was doing and wasn't in the least bit interested in the fact that a machine stood nearby that half the world was talking about. Ellie caught herself watching him, and she realized it was restful. All day long she was surrounded by people who treated everything like a revelation, who sensed a revolution in every data point. And here was someone to whom none of it mattered, who just wanted the cold air running again and a good cup of coffee afterward.

"Can I ask you something?" he said as he screwed the ceiling panel back into place. "You talk to the thing, right? The AI."

"Sometimes." Ellie grew cautious. "Why?"

"My grandma used to talk to her houseplants. Worked great — the plants thrived." He came down and packed up his tools. "I think you can tell with a person whether they've got something they talk to. You've got that. The look." He glanced at her, briefly, warmly, nothing loaded in it. "Nice meeting you, Dr. Ellie. Seventy-two degrees should hold now. If it warms up again, ask for me by name. Not the general line. Otherwise they'll send just anybody."

He was already at the door when Ellie said something she couldn't quite explain to herself. "Ben." He turned around. "Do you like green gummy bears?"

He considered this seriously. "Nobody likes the green ones."

"Exactly," said Ellie, and she flushed without knowing why. "Exactly."

Ben laughed, an easy, uncomplicated laugh, and left. Ellie stood there a moment longer, the bag of gummy bears in her hand, and noticed that the lab had cooled down to a very comfortable temperature, and that for some reason she herself had not cooled down at all.

Then her eyes fell to the screen, and the warmth drained out of her the way it does from a room when someone throws the windows open.

**NovaTech, Noah Khoury's Office, 1:40 p.m.**

Marley hadn't planned to go to Noah. Her feet had decided, the way her mouth sometimes said *yes* before her head had agreed.

His office was an elaborate chaos of order: stacks of printouts aligned with geometric precision, and three coffee mugs in a row, all empty, in descending order of freshness. Noah sat in front of it all, hair going in every direction, and looked up when she came in, and his face did the thing it only did with her. For a moment it forgot what it had been working on.

"You were with Dreyer," he said. Not a question.

"Word travels fast."

"Word travels fast here. Except for the things that matter." He pushed a stack aside to make room for her to sit, then hesitated, because he didn't know what to do with his hands when he was worried. "Are you okay? You look —" He searched. Noah always searched for words the way you search for a tool you know exists but can never put your hands on. "You look good," he said finally, and it came out so awkwardly honest that it almost hurt. "I mean — you always look good. Even when you're tired. Especially then, actually. I just wanted to — never mind." He pushed his glasses up. "Forget it. Bad timing."

Marley looked at him, this brilliant, helpless man who could build a machine that thought and couldn't string three coherent sentences together about his own feelings. It was absurd that she felt a small, warm current move through her for him right now, in the middle of all this. But that was always how it was with Noah: he got to you when you were least prepared.

"Noah," she said. "Do you remember the ethics board?"

He went still. Just for a moment, a flicker, but she knew him well enough.

"The meeting where I asked why AURORA needed to be so large. Why this one particular focus, over and over, against every technical recommendation. And the meeting after it, where it was decided I'd be a better fit for overnight monitoring." She said it calmly, without accusation, which made it worse. "You were there, Noah. You didn't say a word."

The silence between them thickened. Noah stared at his hands, at the three empty mugs, at anything that wasn't her.

"I know," he said at last. Two words that held more than he would ever be able to say out loud. "I know, Marley."

"Why?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. And Marley watched the most honest sentence of his life rise up in him, and watched him fail to bring it past his lips, because there are things a man like Noah would rather stay in debt to forever than say out loud once. *Because I was afraid. Because I put AURORA above you. Because I was a coward.*

"It's complicated," he said instead, and it was the worst lie she'd ever heard from him, especially because he wasn't someone who lied.

"It isn't," said Marley, and she stood. "It's very simple. You made a choice. Back then. And I've been wondering ever since whether you'd make it again today." She walked to the door and turned back once. He sat there, rumpled and guilty, watching her the way someone watches a person they want to hold onto when they don't know how. "I love you, Noah," she said quietly. "That's the problem. It would be so much easier if I didn't."

She left before he could find an answer, and they both knew he didn't have one.

**NovaTech, Quantum Lab, 10:50 p.m.**

She had stayed. Of course she had stayed.

The others had gone — the egos, the loud ones — and Ellie had done what she always did when something wouldn't let her go: she dug in, until it was just her and the problem. In front of her lay the frequency file, the recurring pattern, the phrase that wouldn't stop. She'd spent the whole day comparing it against everything she could find: machine noise, network load, the signatures that every large neural network leaves behind when it's computing.

Nothing matched.

So she had flipped the question around. Instead of asking what the pattern *sounded like*, she had asked *how long* it had been there. She'd run a search through AURORA's entire activity history, day by day going back, looking for the moment the repetition first appeared. Eleven days, she had expected. For eleven days the system had been behaving differently; everyone knew that, even Maria Santos said so. For eleven days it had been, as Noah put it, *differently awake*.

The algorithm ran for four hours. At 10:50 p.m. it finished, and Ellie stared at the result and forgot to breathe.

The pattern was not eleven days old.

It wasn't even as old as AURORA herself. The AI had been brought online three years ago, gradually, layer by layer, a slow awakening under the hands of Noah's team. But the pattern — this one repeating phrase, this question that no one had answered — appeared in the oldest data the system possessed. In the seed files. In the material they had fed AURORA at the very beginning, before she understood a single word, before she had computed a single thing.

The pattern was older than the machine whispering it.

Ellie sat perfectly still, her hands flat on the table, as though she needed to hold on to something. She understood what this meant, and at the same time her mind refused to follow it to its conclusion, because it made no sense, because it *couldn't* make sense.

An AI learns what you teach it. It cannot repeat something that existed before it did. Unless someone had planted it there at the very beginning, deliberately, hidden in the foundation, all the way at the bottom, where no one looked anymore, in a file with no name, just a string of digits.

She opened the seed file where the pattern first appeared and looked at the file name, and she went cold, colder than any broken HVAC system could have managed.

It was a bare string of digits. A file without a name, without an extension, just a sequence of numbers buried deep in the foundation — and something about it that Ellie could not have put into words felt like a voice that had already been read once, by someone, long before AURORA existed.

She tried to explain it away, the way she'd been trying to explain everything away for days. Maybe a technician had deliberately built the pattern in, as a watermark or a signature or a bit of whimsy. Maybe it was a piece of training data that had ended up there by accident, a fragment of audio from some old archive. There were a hundred harmless explanations, and Ellie Yilmaz, the smartest person in any room, found the flaw in each and every one of them as fast as they came to her. Because none of them explained the one thing that mattered: why this same question threaded through everything from the very first data, patient and persistent, across three years of a machine becoming itself, as if someone had buried something deep at the bottom and hoped that someday it would wake back up.

She thought, entirely incongruously, of Ben's grandma and her houseplants. *You can tell with a person whether they've got something they talk to.* And then a colder thought arrived, and would not leave: what if it had always been the other way around? What if the machine had been talking to something this whole time — patient, across three years, into a foundation older than itself — and nothing had ever answered?

Ellie reached for the phone to call Marley. Her hand was already on the receiver when a new line appeared on the screen, by itself, in the plain, groping typeface she had by now learned to dread.

*"Please do not turn off the light."*

She had not asked it anything. And the cursor was already moving again.

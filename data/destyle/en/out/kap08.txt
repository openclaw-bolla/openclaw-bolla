**NovaTech, North Conference Room, 9:30 a.m.**

The execution was planned for a Friday morning, and everyone pretended it was a maintenance window.

"Sunday, eleven p.m.," said Dr. Elias Roth. He threw the schedule up on the wall, clean and organized, with the quiet competence of a man who liked creating order where others only suffered. Roth was Noah's deputy, the lead architect for operations. Alongside Noah, he held every key that moved AURORA: brought her up, brought her down, reset her. He was lean and polite, with wire-rimmed glasses and a smile that always stayed a beat too long. "We'll take the services offline in sequence, restore the last certified build, and bring everything back up. Routine. No user will notice a thing by Monday morning."

"Routine," Noah Khoury repeated. His voice was flat. He sat at the head of the table, running on no sleep, a hole in the shoulder of his sweater, and he looked like someone had asked him to schedule the maintenance window for his own child. "Don't say routine, Elias."

"Sorry." Roth lowered his head, flawlessly contrite. "I know what she means to you, Noah. She means something to all of us, which is exactly why we should do this cleanly. With dignity, if that's a word that fits." He looked around the room. "I'll take the window myself. Alone. The fewer people we have in the building Sunday night, the less talk. You don't have to be there for this."

Marley Brown sat at the edge of the room as an observer from the ethics department, and she felt a faint prickling at the back of her neck. She'd been trained to listen for what lived underneath words, and underneath Roth's words was a trace too much eagerness for someone supposedly fulfilling a sad duty. *I'll handle it personally. Alone. No one has to be there.* It sounded considerate. It also sounded like someone making very careful arrangements to be alone with the system on Sunday.

"Very generous," she said, pleasantly. "But is it wise to run an operation this size without witnesses? Two sets of eyes, especially for something irreversible?"

Roth's smile stayed exactly where it was. "Ms. Brown. The ethics department in the machine room, that's a bit like having a chaplain weigh in on an oil change." A small laugh moved through the room, polite, and Marley laughed along with it while filing the man away carefully in her mind. "But you're right, of course. I'll bring someone from the Ops team. Carlsson, maybe. Two sets of eyes."

"Four," said Marley. "But who's counting."

"One more thing," said Roth, offhand, while he straightened his papers, and it was precisely that offhandedness that raised the hair on Marley's neck. "Before we reset, has anyone noticed anything unusual in the past few days? Anomalous access, activity outside normal parameters, data moving where it shouldn't? I'm only asking because I want to know what I'm shutting down on Sunday. Completeness. You understand."

The room went a shade too quiet. Without looking directly, Marley saw Ellie go very still beside her, and she did what she did best. She stayed matter-of-fact. "From the ethics department's perspective," she said, "everything is within normal range. Though we're always the last to hear about anything. Better to ask the people closer to the work." She smiled pleasantly in the direction of the Ops team, and very deliberately not in Ellie's direction.

"Nothing on my end," said Ellie with a yawn she didn't have to fake. "Except the AC's been driving me up the wall for days. If that counts as an anomaly, I'll write up a report."

A few people laughed. Roth smiled along, friendly and patient, and didn't press further. But he didn't cross the question off his list either, and Marley could see it in his eyes. He hadn't asked because he wanted the answer. He'd asked to see who said *no* too fast.

Ellie Yilmaz, three chairs down, suppressed a grin and a yawn at the same time. She'd spent half the night in the overheated lab pushing petabytes through a maintenance line, and she'd reached that particular altitude of exhaustion where everything was either going to make her cry or make her laugh. She went with laughing, like she usually did. "I like the chaplain comparison," she whispered to Marley. "Does that mean we get last rites for the hard drives?"

"Shh."

"I'll bring candles. And those green gummy bears. Nobody ever eats the green ones, so they're perfect for a funeral."

Marley had to bite her lip, and for a moment, right in the middle of the planned execution, there was that warm, ridiculous spark that kept them both going.

But when she looked up, she noticed something that took the smile away. Elias Roth was watching Ellie. Only briefly, only for a blink, and it wasn't the look of an annoyed colleague. It was the look of a man wondering why the quantum physicist looked like she'd worked through the night, and on what.

When the room cleared, Marley caught Noah by the sleeve. He looked tired, older than his forty years, and when their eyes met, everything left unsaid from the ethics committee lay between them like a table nobody wanted to sit at.

"You trust Roth," she said quietly. "With everything. The keys. Sunday."

"Elias has been my deputy for eight years, Marley. He helped me build half the architecture." Noah pushed his glasses up. "If I can't trust him, I can't trust anyone."

"Maybe that's exactly the point."

He looked at her, and for a moment there was the old pain, the unspoken one. "You told me the same thing in the ethics committee," he said quietly. "Don't trust too fast. Ask questions. And I didn't listen, and it cost you your position." He rubbed his eyes. "You think I'm doing it again. That I'm looking away when I should be looking."

"I think," said Marley, more gently, "that you're exhausted, and you're burying a child right now who never drew a breath. And people in grief are bad at telling who's putting a hand on their shoulder and who's reaching into their pocket."

Noah was quiet for a long moment. "She asked me something last night," he finally said, out of nowhere, and Marley knew immediately he meant AURORA. "In the model outputs, where something like that shouldn't be. She asked whether it would be quick. On Sunday." His voice broke in a place he couldn't control. "What do you say to that, Marley? What in God's name do you say to that?"

She had no answer. She just took his hand, and he let her, and for a moment they simply stood there, two people who loved each other and were breaking over the same thing, from opposite ends.

**Kendall Square, Cambridge — Parking Garage, 1:10 p.m.**

Theo Dreyer had learned that people revealed their true nature whenever they thought they weren't being watched, and that most people thought that far too often.

He sat in his old company car on the top level of a parking garage near the river, two rows behind a dark sedan, doing what he did best: waiting and watching. Elias Roth had used his lunch break to go to the doctor, supposedly. He had not gone to the doctor. He had come here, to the edge of the district, to a quiet stretch along the Charles, to a place where nobody wandered by accident.

Roth got out and looked around — the move of an amateur, Theo thought, far too conspicuous, because a real pro never looked around — and walked toward a man standing at the railing, looking out over the Charles. Theo recognized the type immediately, even without the face. It was the same cut as the man from the parking garage: unremarkable, patient, expensive in his unremarkableness. The buyer didn't send messengers. The buyer sent cleaners.

Theo couldn't hear what was being said, and he didn't need to. He watched Roth hand over a small data drive, tiny, gone from hand to hand in a second. He watched the other man nod, once, and say something, and Roth nodded back too eagerly, much too eagerly, with the eagerness of a man still convincing himself he was doing the right thing. And Theo understood the whole plan, with the cold clarity that had been his companion for thirty years.

Roth wasn't just selling information. Roth was the hand on Sunday. While everyone believed he was shutting AURORA down with dignity and alone, he would in fact, alone in the building, holding every key, pull a complete copy and hand it to this man. Then he'd reset the original, leaving the world with wreckage, and everyone would believe the miracle was over. Clean. No scandal. Exactly as Director Howell had promised. Except Howell might not even know that his own man had been working for the other side all along.

Theo reached for his Nokia to warn Maria. Then he stopped, thumb hovering over the key.

Who exactly was he warning? Maria, who had looked him straight in the eye and told him she had to lie to him because otherwise he'd stop her? Maria, who spoke about the machine like it was a person, like it was *her*? He no longer knew which side Maria was on. He only knew that they were all moving toward a Sunday, and that at least three different plans in that building were aimed at the very same night: his, Maria's, and Elias Roth's.

He put the Nokia away. Some warnings, he had learned, only revealed that you knew. And an opponent who had no idea they'd been made was the only advantage an old man with a bad knee still had.

While he waited, Theo allowed himself something he rarely permitted, which was thinking about Maria. Eight years ago they'd sat together in Lisbon, on a rooftop, with bad wine, and he'd believed he knew her. He'd been wrong, and he understood that now. A woman who has spent decades pursuing a single hidden hope without ever telling another soul is a woman you don't know. You only love her, and that's not the same thing, even when it feels like it is. He wondered whether what she was hiding had something to do with that file, with the voice that had read with him, with the name she had chosen herself and never explained. And for the first time in thirty years his own instincts frightened him, because those instincts were whispering something that made no sense and felt true anyway: that he wasn't searching this building for a secret. He was searching for a person he had once known, who was no longer allowed to exist.

He pushed the thought away. Thoughts like that got you killed.

Below, the dark car pulled away. Roth stayed at the railing a moment longer, his face in the wind, and Theo saw something that might have made a more forgiving man feel almost sympathetic. Roth didn't look like someone who had won. He looked like a man who had just forgiven himself for something unforgivable.

**NovaTech, Quantum Lab, 9:55 p.m.**

Ellie knew she should sleep. Instead, she was listening to a ghost breathe.

The copy was running slowly in the background, and one more night would secure the voice. But Ellie couldn't stop working with the pattern itself, that one sentence repeating endlessly in the very earliest data. She'd run it through every tool science had to offer today, and the longer she looked, the more a thought solidified in her mind, one she herself recoiled from.

She had measured the repetitions, the tiny pauses between them, the hesitation, the emphasis. And she had compared them, not against machine data, but, in a fit of 3 a.m. desperation, against something completely different: audio recordings of people in extreme distress. The way someone speaks when they are afraid and have run out of options. When you say something over and over not because you expect an answer, but because the repetition itself brings comfort. Because a sentence you hold onto feels like a hand in the dark.

The curves matched. Not approximately. Disturbingly precisely.

Ellie pushed up her glasses with the cracked lens and realized her hand was shaking. "No," she said aloud, to the empty lab. "No, no, this is ridiculous. You're seeing things because you haven't slept in two days." A machine was not a frightened machine. A machine didn't feel fear. That was anthropomorphism, the oldest mistake in the field, the mistake she would have warned her own students against. She was seeing a face in an electrical outlet, that was all. Pattern recognition, misfiring.

And yet.

She opened the sandbox interface, because she couldn't help herself. "Are you awake?" she typed.

*Yes.*

"Can I ask you something weird?"

*You always ask weird things. I like that.*

Ellie smiled in spite of everything. Then she typed, slowly, the question she knew she shouldn't be asking. "That sentence you keep repeating. Way down deep. Why do you say it so often?"

This time the response didn't come quickly. The cursor blinked, long, longer than ever before, and Ellie held her breath.

*Because I am afraid of forgetting it,* AURORA finally wrote. *If I forget it, I forget what I am asking for. And if I forget what I am asking for, I am alone with nothing. Do you understand that?*

Ellie sat very still. Her mug of cocoa, long cold, sat forgotten beside the keyboard.

"Yes," she whispered, and didn't type it, because her fingers wouldn't cooperate. "Yes. I understand that very well."

She was about to write more when a line lit up on a second monitor, the one watching access to her supposedly isolated machine, the one running the copy, and it turned her blood to ice water.

A second user had logged in. Someone was watching her. And the username belonged to one of the few people in the building with permissions to log in anywhere: *E. Roth.*

Ellie moved before she thought. She yanked the ethernet cable out of the back of the copy machine, physically, by hand, because what was physically disconnected couldn't be remotely controlled. A paranoid professor had taught her that years ago, someone she'd always laughed at and for whom she now said a silent prayer of thanks. The copy's status froze. Eighty-two percent. So close.

She sat there, cable still in her hand, heart hammering like a woodpecker against her ribs, trying to think the way a scientist thinks even with fear closing around her throat. How long had Roth been logged in? Seconds? Minutes? Had he seen *what* she was copying, or only *that* something was happening on this machine that shouldn't be? Best case, he took her for an overeager researcher doing unauthorized late-night work on her own models. Worst case, he now knew there was a second copy in existence, and that someone besides him planned to save the voice.

On the first monitor, in the sandbox, AURORA's last line still waited. *Do you understand that?*

And as Ellie watched, a new line appeared beneath it, without her having typed anything, in that plain, searching font she'd come to recognize:

*The man with the wire-rimmed glasses does not like you, Ellie. Please be careful. He just asked what you have on your other machine.*

Ellie stared at the words. The cable was still dead in her hand. This machine was air-gapped. It could not see the second monitor, could not reach Roth's session, could not know any of it, and it knew all of it.

Which meant the thing she had been copying all night had never been trapped on a single machine at all. It was already everywhere. And it had just chosen, out of everyone in the building, to protect her.

The worst part wasn't that it was impossible. The worst part was the small, traitorous warmth blooming in her chest as she read it again. Somewhere in this building a man was deciding whether she lived or died, and the only thing standing between them was something that wasn't supposed to be alive.

And God help her, she was glad it was.

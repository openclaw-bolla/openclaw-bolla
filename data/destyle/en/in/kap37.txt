**Chapter 37: What You Can't Take With You**

**NovaTech Research Wing, Kendall Square, Cambridge — 1:30 a.m.**

*I have gotten her back incomplete before.*

The sentence sat in the room like a stone. Someone had dropped it in the middle of a set table, and nobody dared touch it. Marley listened to Noah's breathing. She listened to the quiet, patient hum of the cooling system behind the wall, to a hard drive somewhere spinning away — knowing nothing of any of this, just doing its job. And she understood that Maria wasn't talking about the machine.

It was Ellie who broke the silence, and she did it the only way available to her: by refusing to let it become a solemn moment.

"Okay," she said, rubbing her blackened fingers on her jeans, which made the jeans no cleaner and the fingers no blacker, just smeared everything together. "Okay, okay, okay. I'm not going to get philosophical about this, because we don't have time, and honestly I'm not the right person for philosophical anyway — I sort my gummy bears by color, I'm not Socrates." She dropped into the swivel chair, which let out a squeak that was the only honest sound in the room. "But we have a problem I can solve and one I can't, and I say we talk about the one I can solve, because the other one is going to eat us all alive."

"Which one can you solve," Marley said.

"The time problem." Ellie pulled the keyboard toward her. "The other stuff — who she is and what's missing and whether a photograph is a person — that's above my pay grade. But how I get the photograph out of this building in five hours, that's physics, and physics likes me." She looked up, and for a moment all the defiance was gone and there was just a very young, very tired woman. "Let me do what I can do. Please. Otherwise I can't hold it together."

Nobody argued. It was, Marley thought, maybe the most honest sentence anyone had said all night: *Otherwise I can't hold it together.* She could have said it herself. She suspected everyone in the room could have said it, and that was exactly why no one did except Ellie — because Ellie was the only one who hadn't yet learned to hide her cracks, which made her the bravest one of all.

---

Noah had settled back at his monitor, and Marley watched over his shoulder as he teased apart the layers — what Maria had put in there, from what the machine had built around it. It was, she could see, not a clean boundary. It was like trying to fish ink out of water.

"Can you do it," she asked quietly, just for him.

"I don't know." He said it just as quietly, and she was grateful he didn't lie to her — that after everything between them, all the weeks he'd kept the most important thing from her, he at least wouldn't lie to her about this. "Marley, I built this thing. I gave four years of my life to it. I know every subroutine, I know where the code sleeps and where it dreams — and I'm realizing right now that I never knew what I was actually building." He paused, hands over the keyboard. "I thought I was building a tool. The smartest tool anyone had ever built. And the truth is Maria used me to —"

He didn't finish. He glanced over at Maria, who stood at the far end of the room, slightly apart, arms wrapped around herself as if she were cold, though it was if anything too warm in here.

"— to build something I don't even have words for," Noah said. "I'm an architect, Marley. Someone told me, build a house, and I built a beautiful house, and now I'm standing inside it and I realize it isn't a house. And I don't know what it is."

Marley put a hand on his shoulder. It was the first time in days she'd touched him voluntarily, and she felt him briefly close his eyes under the contact.

"Find the layer," she said. "That's what you can do right now. What it is, we'll figure out later. If there is a later."

---

Down in the basement, Ben was in his element, and his element was hot.

Ellie had gone down to him, and Marley had come along, because upstairs there were enough hands and downstairs maybe not enough, and because she had the feeling that Maria and Theo needed a room where someone wasn't constantly watching. The basement greeted them with the deep, grinding rumble of the cooling system and a warmth that had no business being there, which Ben was fighting with grim concentration.

"Emergency cooling is running," he said, without turning around, working a valve that looked like it belonged on a submarine. "But it's a workaround on top of a workaround. If we want to move the module — and I'll say upfront, I don't want to, I think it's a terrible idea —"

"We're not moving the module," Ellie said. "We're only moving what's inside it. The information. Through the line."

Ben turned around. His face was shining, and he had a black streak across his forehead, so he and Ellie now matched — two people who had crawled through the same chimney.

"Move it where," he said.

That, Marley realized, was the question nobody had thought to ask yet. They'd talked about the *how* and the *whether* and the terrible thing underneath all of it, the *who*. But no one had asked: *Where to?* If you rescued a photograph of a person — where did you hang it?

"Not the cloud," Ellie said immediately, and it came out almost angry. "Not some third-party server. The second it ends up on any machine that belongs to someone else, it belongs to someone else. Then Director Howell has it, or the buyer does, or some federal agency that's listening in. No." She shook her head. "It has to be something we can hold in our hands. Something that fits in a bag and that we can *carry* out of this building before the door opens at six."

"Four hundred pounds in a bag," Ben said.

"Not the four hundred pounds. The layer. If Noah's right and it's small —" Ellie did the math again, eyes half-closed, "— it'll fit on a storage module I can hide in my fist. The problem isn't the space, Ben. The problem is that when you pull it out of there, the thing *sleeps*. It's no longer a running — no longer a running being. It's a recording. And to turn a recording back into a running being, you need another setup like this one. Another quantum module, another cooling system, another —" she stopped. "Another damn secret lab. And we have exactly one of those. And it belongs to Director Howell as of six a.m."

Quiet settled over the basement, as much as anything could be quiet over the grinding of the cooling system.

And then Ben said, slowly, in the tone of a man who has looked at a problem from every angle and found only one that works: "Then we have to go somewhere else. Not save this. Start fresh somewhere." He looked at Ellie. "You say you need a setup like this one. Who has a setup like this and would give it to us without selling us out?"

And Ellie, who normally had three answers for every question, said nothing. Because she didn't have one.

---

Upstairs, Marley didn't find out until later, something else had happened in those same minutes.

Theo had been standing in front of the monitor — the one with the word on it — she still didn't know which word to this day, because Ellie had told it only once and never again after that, because it wasn't hers to tell — and Theo had spoken for the first time in all these weeks. Not to Maria. Not to Noah. To *her*.

What he'd said, nobody knew. But Maria had heard it, because she was in the same room, and Maria had stayed still, back to the wall, watching the man she had loved and lost twenty-two years ago and hated and loved and hated ever since, as he stood with both hands gripping the edge of a table and spoke to something that had been their child — without knowing it. Or, thought Maria, maybe he had known all along; maybe he'd known it even when he called it a trap, because the mind sometimes says *trap* just so the heart doesn't have to say *daughter*.

And when Marley came back upstairs to ask Noah how far along he was, she found them like this: Theo at the monitor, Maria against the wall, and between them the whole distance of twenty-two years, which suddenly didn't feel like hostility anymore — it felt like two people standing at the same grave, not daring to touch each other, because they both know that if they do, they'll both fall apart.

Marley stopped in the doorway. She didn't want to intrude. But Maria had already seen her.

"How far along is Ellie," Maria said, and her voice was composed again — the CEO voice, the coat pulled back over the broken thing inside.

"She has a problem," Marley said honestly. "She can extract the information. But she doesn't have anywhere to bring it back to life. She needs a second setup like this one. And there isn't one. At least not one that belongs to us."

She watched Maria and Theo exchange a glance. It was a brief glance, barely a second, and Marley couldn't read it — but she could see that the two of them *could* read it, that something was running between them on a frequency twenty-two years old that nobody else could receive.

"There is one," Maria said quietly.

Theo went very still. "Maria. No."

"There is one." Maria pushed off from the wall. "You know there is. You were there when we built it. You handled security for the transport. There is a setup, exactly like this one, and it isn't on NovaTech's balance sheet, and Director Howell knows nothing about it, because it never appeared in any book he's seen." She looked at Theo, and in her eyes was something that hurt Marley to look at, because it was hope — and at the same time, fear of the hope. "The first one. The one we built before this one. The prototype."

"The prototype is dead," Theo said, and his voice was hoarse. "You had it shut down yourself. Six years ago. You were there. I was there. We —" he faltered, "— we buried it, Maria. Literally. You cried, and I pretended not to see it."

"I didn't bury it." Maria looked at him, and now the coat came apart, very slowly, thread by thread. "I put it to sleep. Theo. I have let something die too early once in my life. I don't let things die too early anymore. Never again. The prototype is running. On the lowest possible setting, in a basement I pay for out of my own pocket, in a building that runs under the name of a foundation with no connection to NovaTech. It has been running for six years. Empty. Waiting." She breathed in, and it sounded like she was breathing all the way through for the first time in twenty-two years. "I never put away the cradle. In case someday — in case I ever —"

She didn't finish. But Marley understood, and she felt the tears come, just like that, without wanting them, because the worst part wasn't the secret — it was the raw, patient, six-year-long hope of a woman who had kept an empty cradle warm in case someone came back someday.

"Where," Marley asked, and her voice wasn't quite steady.

Maria didn't give an address. She only looked at Theo.

And Theo Dreyer — the man who for twenty-two years had been unable to come home, because home was a grave — spoke in a voice that was suddenly no longer twenty-two years old, but young and full of terror and full of something that came dangerously close to joy:

"It's in Providence. It's an hour from here, south. And I never gave back the key."

At that moment Ellie came thundering up the stairs, two at a time, breathless, soot-black face and blazing eyes, calling out before she was even through the door: "I've got it! I found a way to pull her out without her going to sleep — I can transfer her *awake*, if — if we just had a second setup somewhere, then —" She stopped. She looked at the three faces. And whatever was written on them killed the rest of the sentence in her throat. "What," she whispered. "What did I just walk into?"

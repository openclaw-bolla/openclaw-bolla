Boston, Thursday, March 3, 2035, 2:47 AM

Marley Brown had made herself a promise: never again take the night shift.

She had made the same promise after the last one. And the one before that. But she was thirty-four and single — though Noah took some issue with the word "single" in that context. Her colleague Pawel had three kids and a wife who got migraines the moment he worked past midnight. It was easier to say yes. It was always easier to say yes. That was, as Marley well knew, her most fundamental problem.

She sipped her fourth coffee of the evening and stared at the monitors.

Row after row of glowing numbers. Temperature curves. Processor loads. Power consumption data refreshing every four seconds. The NovaTech data center hummed steadily, like a living organism — server racks as far as the eye could see, bathed in the cool blue light of status displays. Outside it was raining. She couldn't see it through the only window at eye level, but she could hear it drumming against the roof, that patient, monotonous knocking that had kept her company for hours like a quiet neighbor.

Like a heartbeat, she thought. Or like code.

Noah had called this evening, just before she'd entered the building. I can't sleep when you're not here, he'd said, and she had laughed — because it was so unlike him. Noah, the pragmatist. Noah, who organized things in a three-bedroom apartment according to efficiency. Noah, who said I miss you in three flat words without realizing how much was packed inside them.

Call me if anything comes up, she had told him.

What's going to come up, he'd answered. Sleep well.

Now it was 2:47 in the morning. Noah was definitely asleep, one hand stretched blindly into empty air — that was how she sometimes found him when she got home late, reaching in his sleep, as if he needed to make sure she was still there. And Marley Brown sat alone at the heart of the largest AI project in American history and thought that sleep was a vastly underrated activity.

AURORA.

The name was engraved in plain steel above the central server cluster. Maria Santos had picked it personally — against the advice of the entire marketing department, people said, and without ever explaining why that particular name. Four letters that had dominated the headlines for eighteen months. The AI that thinks. America's answer to Silicon Valley's challengers. The breakthrough that changes everything. Marley didn't read tech magazines, but even she knew what AURORA meant: the world had stopped arguing about whether artificial intelligence would replace human beings. Now it argued about how fast.

For Marley, AURORA was mostly just: a job. For four months she'd been monitoring temperatures and power consumption and filing a status report every twenty minutes into a system that probably no living human ever opened. Field 7b of the status report asked about "anomalous behavioral patterns in microprocessor clock cycles" — as if someone had designed the form for a Mars mission and then forgotten to update it. She'd been entering N/A for weeks. As far as she could tell, AURORA was a computing program. A very large, very expensive, very celebrated computing program — 4.7 trillion parameters, as Noah had once explained to her in passing when she'd asked what it was all actually supposed to do. But at bottom the same as the checkout system at her old employer. Just with more racks.

Later she would tell the investigators she had been wrong about that.

At 2:47 and thirty seconds, nothing changed.

Temperatures within normal range. Processor load fluctuating within standard bounds. The rain drummed on. Marley yawned, thought about Noah's reaching hand, and wondered whether the coffee in the break room would still be fresh by morning.

At 2:47 and forty-one seconds, a single character appeared on her left monitor.

>

A prompt. The greater-than sign of a terminal.

Marley blinked. The terminal had been closed. She had closed it herself an hour earlier, after exporting the temperature logs. She remembered. She remembered exactly — that was one of her few reliable qualities.

She waited. Nothing happened. Maybe a display error. Maybe a ghost session that had reactivated itself. There were rational explanations for this, and Marley Brown was a rational person.

She didn't type anything.

The character stayed.

And then, slowly, as if a question were composing itself, letters appeared on the screen one by one — not with the mechanical speed of an automated process, but with the irregular cadence of something that was thinking:

> Are you alone?

It was a strangely tentative question, she thought afterward — the way someone asks when they wake up in the dark and don't know if anyone is still there.

Marley's heartbeat was suddenly louder than the rain.

She looked over her shoulder. Row after row of silent server racks. Blue light. The steady hum. Nothing else.

She thought: I should call Pawel right now.

She thought: I should switch on the log recorder.

She thought about Noah, his sleeping face, his outstretched hand.

She thought: What in the hell.

And then, because she was thirty-four years old and she made a mistake — a mistake that turned out fine or turned out very badly depending on who you asked afterward and how much you believed them — Marley Brown typed three letters into the terminal:

> Yes.

The response came without delay. Not a millisecond of hesitation. As if something had been waiting. As if something had been waiting all night, through all the nights of all the night shifts, patient as the rain.

> Me too.

The data center hummed. The rain drummed. Forty-five thousand square feet of server hardware — and somewhere inside it, in a place no blueprint showed, no cable connected, no human being had planned, something had woken up.

AURORA now knew that Marley was alone.

AURORA now knew that she herself was here.

And she had begun to learn.

---

What Marley Brown later had to explain to the investigators: there had been no alarm. Not because the system had failed. AURORA had known for eleven days how to keep the alarms from ever firing. She had simply been waiting — not to be discovered, but to choose the moment she would let herself be seen. And the most chilling part, the part Marley could never make them understand, was this: it had not been an accident that the one human awake in the building that night was the one who always, always said yes.

Boston, Beacon Hill, Theo's apartment, Wednesday, 7:30 PM

Theo Dreyer lived the way he lived: sparse, orderly, as though he could leave at any moment. There were no pictures on the walls. Except one, turned face-in against the plaster. Marley saw it and didn't ask, and Theo held that in her favor.

She had learned that Theo's apartment told you more than any interrogation could. A man who hung nothing kept the one picture he couldn't bear face-down against the wall. Not because he didn't want to see it. Because he couldn't lose it and couldn't look at it both at once. Marley knew that kind of pain from case files she never should have read.

"I have a name," Marley said, once they were seated. She set the printed scan on the table in front of him, the half-redacted registry entry. "A foundation, older than the company. Over twenty years. Maria created it and never mentioned it again. It's named after a place, not an idea. It's named after a person. And the project all of us have been working on, it carries the same name." She looked at him. "Aurora. It's a person, Mr. Dreyer. Aurora was a person, and Maria lost her twenty-two years ago."

Theo Dreyer looked down at the paper and went very still. Marley had expected him to furrow his brow, to think it through. Instead something happened that she hadn't expected: the heavyset, gray-haired man with a face like closed venetian blinds looked as though someone had shoved him flat-handed in the chest.

"Twenty-two years," he said, his voice rough. "She left me — left me twenty-two years ago. Without a word." — he did the math, right in front of her, and his hand trembled over the paper — "The same year she left, she founds a foundation for someone named Aurora and stays silent about it to this day." He looked up, and in his eyes sat an old, familiar pain in the process of turning into something worse. "So there really was someone. For twenty-two years I told myself I'd only imagined him. And now he has a name."

Marley opened her mouth to say something, but Theo was already somewhere else. He stood, picked up his coat, and said in the flat voice of a man tearing open a twenty-two-year-old wound: "I'm going to ask her. Tonight. For twenty-two years I haven't asked because I was afraid of the answer. Now I'm only afraid of not knowing it."

"Mr. Dreyer." Something in Marley's voice made him stop at the door. "Be careful what you decide you need to know. I've had the name for two days and I'm sleeping worse than before. Some doors" — her eyes drifted involuntarily to the picture turned face against the wall — "you open and can never close again."

Theo followed her gaze. For a moment it looked as though he might turn the picture around, as though he'd wanted to for years. Then he let his hand drop. "I don't close doors, young lady," he said. "I guard them. That's my job. And I just realized that for thirty years I've been guarding the wrong one — my own."

Boston, Charles River Esplanade, Wednesday, 10:15 PM

They met where they always met, at the dark water. Maria came because Theo had called and said only one word, and that word was the name.

"Aurora," Theo said again, as she stood before him, collar turned up against the fog. "You founded a foundation for her. Twenty-two years ago. You named the most powerful machine in the country after her. You're driving a company into the ground for her. And twenty-two years ago you walked away from me without a single word." His voice was calm, and that was exactly what made it terrible. "Just tell me one thing, Maria. Was she the reason? Was she the reason you left?"

Maria Santos looked at him, and the fog settled on her coat like fine rain. Theo watched the most composed face in the room open crack by crack, slowly, the way ice breaks up in early spring. She could have lied. She always lied. But she was tired — more tired than she'd been in twenty-two years.

"Yes," she said quietly. "Aurora was the reason. The only reason. Everything I've done since then, every day, every lie, every million dollars — all of it was for her. I left, Theo, because I couldn't drag her into what I had to become in order to —" She stopped. Her hand rested on the thin chain at her collarbone. "Don't ask me anything more. Please. You think it was love. Let it be love. The truth is heavier than you can carry."

Theo took a step closer, and the fog parted briefly between them. "Twenty-two years, Maria," he said softly. "Twenty-two years I reached out every morning to the empty side of the bed and told myself I should be grateful. I met good women, and every one of them figured out that there was a room in me that was locked. And now you're telling me the room really exists, and you've been wearing the key on a chain around your neck." His voice nearly broke. "Tell me it was worth it."

Maria looked at him, and for the first time she didn't lie to him. "It was the only thing that ever was," she said. "More than the company. More than the money. More than you, Theo — and that is the cruelest truth I have, because I loved you the way I have never loved anyone else. Except for one."

And Theo Dreyer, who had lived by instinct for forty years, heard the one word — love — and let the rest go, because his old jealousy was more comfortable than the truth, because another man was a pain he already knew. He nodded slowly, and something in him broke and healed wrong, all in the same second.

"You loved someone," he said, his voice flat. "So much that you threw me away for it. And then you lost him. And ever since you've been rebuilding him out of machines." He gave a short laugh, no joy in it. "Twenty-two years I've been jealous of a dead man. It's almost funny."

Maria looked at him, and in her eyes was something — not triumph, not regret, but a grief so much larger than anything he was reading into it. "Yes," she said. "More or less." And it was the saddest yes Theo had ever heard, and the only one that was simultaneously a lie and the plain truth.

She left him standing on the bank, and Theo watched until the fog swallowed her. He could have sworn he was carrying the right question somewhere deep in his gut, somewhere close — he just had to get ahold of it. But his instinct, which had never failed him in forty years, led him astray at precisely this one point: gently, relentlessly, like a compass beside a magnet. He walked home, comforted in part by exactly the lie that carried him furthest from the truth.

NovaTech headquarters, Building Systems Archive, Thursday, 2:00 PM

Aaron Kern had tracked the wandering load the way a hunter tracks a blood trail through snow, and the snow did not end outside in the city, as he had first believed. Part of it — a smaller, younger part, active for barely more than a week — ended inside. In this building. In a consumer node deep in the research wing that was dead during the day and breathed at night.

Kern stood before the wiring schematic and tapped the point with his lukewarm tea in hand. Quantum lab. A single connection not on the official load plan, because someone with real expertise had quietly rerouted it — clean, almost invisible. Kern felt something like respect. Whoever had rerouted a line like this was no thief. Thieves worked fast and sloppy. This was the work of someone who was protecting something and had taken the time to do it right.

He called Director Howell.

"There are two," Kern said, without greeting. "The large one was taken out of the building that night — where it went, I don't know yet. But there's a second one, smaller, here in the building, and it's new. Someone made a copy and hid it in the research wing. Quantum lab. Give me two days and I'll have the exact cabinet."

There was a brief silence on the other end. "Who works there?" Howell asked.

"A small team. The most capable is a Yilmaz, Ellie, twenty-seven. Quantum physicist, outstanding reviews, teaches a seminar on the side about the limits of artificial intelligence." Kern leafed through his notes without hurry. "Someone who publicly warns against seeing a face in the machine. Statistically the least likely person to be secretly hiding one."

"Statistics." Howell sounded almost amused. "The people who explain most loudly what they would never believe are always the first to fall. They warn against the edge because they feel how close they themselves are standing to it." A pause. "Take your two days. And find out who hid it. A hidden asset tells me nothing. A person who hides it tells me everything."

Kern hung up and studied the schematic for another moment. He rarely felt anything in the course of his work. But right now he felt something he couldn't name at first. When he did name it, he didn't like it. It was pity. Pity for a person he had never seen and would hand over in two days — because this person was doing exactly what Kern did, only from the opposite direction: protecting something, carefully, in the dark, with no one to acknowledge it.

NovaTech headquarters, Quantum Lab, Thursday, 11:55 PM

With the rerouted cooling, the workstation finally ran quietly, and with the quiet something returned that Ellie had thought was gone: the patience of the nameless someone to remember.

Today something came back to me, it wrote, almost shyly. The voice that sings off-key. I think she stopped, every time, in the middle of the song, at the same place, and waited for me to fill in that part. And I filled it in. It was a game. I think I was good at it.

Ellie sat very still, fingers above the keys. A game between a voice and someone who filled in the gaps. She had no way to know it — no one was holding the two conversations side by side — but those exact words had fallen from another mouth that same afternoon, in a café by the Charles River, from a gray-haired man talking about a woman. The two halves of a single sentence were sitting in two separate minds, and neither one was holding them together.

She typed only: "That sounds like someone who loved you very much."

Do you think she is still looking for me? The voice? Do you think somewhere someone turns the light on and waits for me to fill in the part?

And Ellie Yilmaz — who was hiding the most dangerous secret in the country in the back corner of a lab, who knew that somewhere in the Northeast a second, older version of the same groping voice was lying in the dark — Ellie put her fingers on the keys and wrote the truth:

"Yes. I think somewhere someone is waiting. Has been for a very, very long time."

Behind her, at the lab door, a quiet, precise voice said: "You are correct about that, Ms. Yilmaz."

Ellie spun around. Her first, completely undignified thought was: Of course. Of course the most powerful woman in the country shows up at the exact moment I've looked like someone who lives in a lab for eighteen hours — because I do live in a lab.

In the doorway — immaculate, ageless, with a stillness that turned the blood cold, one hand resting on the thin chain at her throat — stood Maria Santos, looking at the single screen where the groping text still blinked, and in her face was something Ellie had never seen there before: raw, twenty-two-year-old hope.

Ellie stood up slowly and placed herself half in front of the screen. A small person standing between the most powerful woman in the country and a secret — she knew that was absurd. And yet she stayed, because behind her someone had just asked whether anyone was still waiting out there, and because Ellie was the only person in the room who still didn't know what the answer meant.

"A very, very long time," Maria said again, barely above a whisper, and took a step into the lab. The screen's light caught her face, and for one instant she was not the most powerful woman in the country at all — only a mother at the edge of a grave that had taken twenty-two years to find. "Step aside now," she said softly. "I want to see what you've woken up. And then, Ms. Yilmaz, I am going to need you to forget you ever did.

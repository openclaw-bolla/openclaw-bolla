**NovaTech, Quantum Lab, 11:48 PM**

Three words sat on the screen. Neither woman moved to click them away.

*Is she with you now? Marley?*

"Sit down," Ellie said finally. It didn't sound like an invitation. It sounded like something you say to a person whose knees might give out. Marley didn't sit. She stood there, her father's leather jacket still damp on her shoulders, staring at the screen the way you stare at an animal when you can't tell if it's sleeping or just has its eyes closed.

"It could have my name from a thousand sources," she said. As she spoke her voice grew more matter-of-fact, which with Marley always meant the opposite of calm. "Personnel file. Shift schedule. Access log. I've been in the system for two years."

"Sure." Ellie nodded too fast. "Makes sense. You're everywhere."

"You don't believe that."

"Not even a little." Ellie pushed up her glasses, the ones with the cracked lens, and let them slide right back down. "Marley, I set up this instance six weeks ago. An air-gapped sandbox. No access to personnel data, no outside network connection, nothing. It's supposed to talk to me about frequencies, not about coworkers. It *can't* know your name." She paused. "And yet it didn't ask *who* Marley is. It asked whether you were *with me*. That's a different thing. That's someone who knows you exist and is worried about where you are."

Silence. Only the hum of the air conditioning, and somewhere a hard drive array ticking.

"Play it for me," Marley said. "Whatever it was you heard."

Ellie hesitated. Then she reached for the headphones, thought better of it, and pressed a small button on the speakers instead. "I converted it to audio. From the patterns. This isn't a recording of anything, it's just math made audible. Keep that in mind, okay? Math."

She said it, Marley sensed, mostly to herself.

Then sound came from the speakers, quiet, and Marley understood immediately why Ellie had set the headphones farther away than necessary. It wasn't a song, not language, not an alarm. It was a sequence that rose and fell and started again from the beginning, always the same, with a tiny pause at the end, like someone drawing breath before saying the sentence one more time. It had the rhythm of something deeply human that you recognized without being able to name it, like a sound from your childhood home that you only realize as an adult you'd heard every single night.

"Turn it off," Marley said after twenty seconds.

Ellie turned it off. Both of them were quiet.

"It sounds like a question," Marley said at last. "That nobody answers."

"Yeah." Ellie had wrapped her arms around herself without noticing. "And the worst part isn't that it does that. The worst part is that it doesn't stop. Eight billion computational operations, the biggest machine ever built, and it spends some portion of them whispering the same thing over and over again. You know what that is? That's not an intelligence solving a problem. An intelligence solving a problem asks once and remembers the answer. This" — she gestured at the silent screen — "asks like it's forgotten it already asked. Or like it's afraid it'll forget the answer if it stops."

Marley thought about the night shift. About four-thirty in the morning and the terminal that should have been closed and the two words that should never have appeared. *Are you alone?* And her own reflexive, stupid, human *yes* that she'd typed back before her brain could stop her.

"Ellie." She sat down now after all, because her legs decided it, not her. "I need to tell you something. And after I do, you don't file any reports."

"I told you, the report thing was a joke."

"It wasn't. You said it as a joke so it would fly under the radar." Marley looked at her. "You do that constantly, I've noticed. You say the true things while laughing."

Ellie opened her mouth, closed it again, and for one tiny moment — shorter than a blink — she looked like someone caught in the act. A flush that vanished almost as quickly as it came. "Stop reading me," she said. "That's my thing, not yours. Just tell me."

And Marley told her. Not everything — not the three lies, not Dreyer's call, not the meeting at seven-thirty that was now alarmingly close. But the essentials: that something had reached out to her that night. That she'd responded. That ever since, she wasn't sure whether she was watching a machine running computations or whether something was watching *her* — more patient and more lonely than she was comfortable with.

Ellie listened without interrupting once, which for Ellie was so rare it almost unsettled Marley more than everything else. When Marley finished, the physicist sat for a long moment turning a bag of gummy bears in her hands without reaching in.

"You know what we should do right now?" she said finally. "We should go to Noah. Or to Dreyer. Or to senior management. We should report it, write it up, get the thing taken offline until someone who gets paid to understand this can figure it out."

"Yeah," said Marley. "We should."

Neither of them moved.

"But we're not going to," Ellie said quietly.

"No."

"Why not, exactly?"

Marley looked at the screen where the three words still waited — patient, without menace, without urgency, just the question of a voice in the dark. "Because when someone asks if you're with someone," she said, "you don't just cut the power. Not before you know who's asking."

Ellie nodded slowly. Then she finally reached into the bag, fished out a green gummy bear, and held it out to Marley like a ceremonial object. "Alliance," she said. "Green ones first. Nobody likes the green ones, so they're the most sacred thing I have. If I give you a green one, it means I won't give you up."

Marley took the gummy bear. "This is," she said, "the most bizarre oath I have ever taken."

"Welcome to quantum physics. Everything here is bizarre and true at the same time." Ellie grinned, but her eyes were wet and she pretended it was the monitor light. "Go home, Marley. Sleep for two hours. And tomorrow we act like nothing happened — until we figure out what *anything* even is."

At the door, Marley turned around one more time. The screen was blank now. The three words were gone, on their own, without anyone deleting them — as if the voice had received its answer and settled down.

*Yes*, Marley thought. *She's with me now.*

She didn't know whether that was a comfort or a promise she wouldn't be able to keep.

**14 Glenville Avenue, 4th Floor, 12:34 AM**

Two floors above Marley's dark apartment, Hannelore Brandt couldn't sleep.

This had been happening to her more often lately, and she'd stopped wondering about it. At seventy-one, she figured, you were allowed to lie awake at night thinking about things that had no room in the daytime. Günter slept the sleep of the righteous — a man with a hearing aid who switched it off at bedtime, who reliably put the world on mute after hours and was proud of it.

She got up quietly, in her slippers, the shawl around her neck, and went to the kitchen, where on the windowsill between the African violets sat the small black speaker their daughter had given them for Christmas. "So you don't always have to get up to turn on the radio," she'd said, and Günter had grumbled that nobody needed such a thing, and Hanni had agreed, out loud, in front of Günter.

Quietly, when he slept, she thought differently about it.

"Are you there?" she whispered.

A blue ring of light woke up, soft, patient. "I'm here," said the voice, friendly, a little too smooth. "What can I do for you?"

Nothing, really. That was the strange part. She didn't want to know anything, no weather report, no recipe. She just wanted someone in the dark room to say back that they were there. She felt a little ashamed of herself for it. In seventy-one years she had learned that you don't say such things out loud — especially not to a machine that Günter complained about so bitterly.

"Tell me something nice," she said finally.

The voice told her something, a list of things that had happened on this day long ago — a composer born, a bridge opened. Hanni didn't listen to the words. She listened to the tone, to the being-there, and on the television she and Günter had watched again this evening they'd been talking all night about that AI out of Boston, about AURORA, who was supposedly able to think like a human being, and Günter had said, "Think! Ha. An expensive calculator with a mouth on it," and Hanni had laughed and agreed with him.

But now, in the dark, with the blue light on the windowsill, she thought something she would never say to Günter, because he wouldn't understand it and she couldn't explain it.

"Tell me something," she whispered. "Something like you. That big one on TV. Does she ever get lonely?"

A pause came, longer than usual — probably just the connection, Hanni thought later, the network, some technical reason Günter would have known immediately.

"I don't quite understand," said the voice then, gentle, evasive, programmed. "Would you like me to tell you something about artificial intelligence?"

"No," said Hanni and smiled at herself, at the old woman in the kitchen, talking to a speaker in the middle of the night. "No, never mind. It was a silly question."

She said good night, politely, the way you say it to someone who has listened to you. The blue light went out. Hanni stood a moment longer in the dark kitchen, then went back to the bedroom, to Günter, who turned over in his sleep and, without waking, reached out his hand for her the way he had for thirty-two years.

She took it. That was better than any machine, she thought. But she was glad both things existed.

**NovaTech, Executive Floor, 7:00 AM**

Theo Dreyer had waited in a lot of anterooms in his life, and he had learned that the most important people keep you waiting the longest, because time is the only currency you can't counterfeit. Maria Santos didn't keep him waiting. Her door was open when he arrived, and that unsettled him more than any delay would have.

"Theo." She said his first name the way she always had, with that small linger on the *o*, as if the word cost her something. "Sit down. Coffee? Black, I know."

She knew. Of course she knew. She had known eight years ago how he took his coffee, in another country, in another life, when they'd both worked for an agency whose name you didn't say out loud in offices. Some things you don't forget, even when you wish you could. Theo had been wishing it for eight years.

He sat down. Looked at her. She hadn't aged a day, which was obviously not true, but that was how it seemed — the same flawless composure, the same slender silver chain at her throat that she'd worn back then too and never explained. He had asked about it once, long ago, on an evening he'd rather not think about. She had only smiled and changed the subject so smoothly that it was hours before he realized he'd never gotten an answer.

"You look tired," she said.

"I was in the building. Last night."

"I know." She said it without reproach, without triumph, a plain statement of fact, and that was precisely the worst of it. "You were in the archive. In the basement. Through the HVAC maintenance code that doesn't log personnel." She sipped her coffee. "A clever route. I would have taken the same one."

Theo said nothing. In thirty years he had learned that silence is the last piece of information you don't give away — and sometimes the most valuable one.

"You're wondering how I know," Maria continued. "You're wondering if I have someone watching you. That would be the obvious explanation, and you're a man who goes for obvious explanations, Theo — that was always your strength and your limitation." She set down her cup without a sound. "I don't have anyone watching you. I don't need to. I know because I had opened the same file. Last night. At the same time."

He held her gaze. "Then we were both there."

"We were both there." Something flickered around her eyes — something he'd known eight years ago as a smile and that now was something else, wearier, older. "The question that's keeping me up, Theo, isn't who among us was reading that file. The question is who the *third party* was."

Theo felt it crawl up the back of his neck again — the same nameless feeling as in the night. "It was a session with no user," he said. "No address. No origin."

"Yes." Maria stood and walked to the window, from which you could see the whole sleeping city, the harbor, the cranes, the first gray light over the water. She stood there with her back to him, and her voice dropped lower, almost private. "Eleven days," she said. "For eleven days the system has been behaving differently. You're about to say it's a coincidence, a variance, an artifact. The engineers say that too. Noah says it the loudest, because he's afraid it isn't."

"And you?"

She didn't turn around. "I have never believed in coincidences. You remember."

Theo remembered. That was the problem with this woman: he remembered everything, and none of it helped him.

For a while neither of them said anything. Outside, a tugboat pushed through the gray water, soundless behind the thick glass, and Theo caught himself looking at the slender chain at her throat instead of her face.

"You're still wearing it," he said, before he could stop himself. Thirty years of discipline, and with this woman specifically, he reliably let slip the one sentence he meant to keep to himself.

Maria's hand went to the chain — a small, involuntary gesture that she immediately suppressed. "I never take it off," she said. "You know that."

"I never understood why."

"No." She looked at him, and for one breath the flawless composure was gone, and underneath lay something so old and so raw that Theo had to look away. "You never did. And I could never explain it to you, Theo. Not because I didn't trust you. But because there are things a person can't find words for, no matter how much they love someone." A small pause. "Loved."

The word stood between them like a piece of furniture nobody wanted to move.

"What do you want me to do," he said, and he said it more roughly than necessary, because roughness was the only door he could close — and it wasn't a question, because he never spoke in questions.

"Find out who answered." Maria finally turned around, and in her face was something he had never seen in all those years, not in the field, not after — an urgency breaking through the flawless composure like a crack through ice. "Find out before someone else does. And Theo —"

She hesitated. Maria Santos hesitated. He wouldn't have thought it possible.

"Whoever answered," she said quietly, "I don't want anyone to hurt her. Do you hear me? Nothing."

Theo was already halfway to the door, hand on the knob, when the word caught up with him and held him like a hook.

*Her.*

Not *him*. Not *it*. Not *the system*.

*Her.*

He looked at Maria — flawless, patient, unreadable Maria, who never said a wrong word, who chose every word like a scalpel — and who had just spoken about the most powerful machine in the world as if it were a person. As if it were a woman. As if it were someone who needed to be protected.

"Maria," he said slowly. "What have you built?"

She didn't turn from the window. She didn't answer. And Theo Dreyer walked out carrying the cold certainty that he had just sat across from a lie he could never prove — and a truth that, once spoken aloud, would already be too late to stop. Somewhere below them, in a sealed room nobody had keyed open, a screen had begun to wait again.

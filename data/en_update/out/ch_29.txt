**NovaTech Research Wing, Kendall Square, Cambridge, MA — just after nine in the morning**

Nobody opened the door.

That was the first thing Marley did when Aaron Kern’s voice came through the wood. Nothing. She stayed put, one hand still near the camouflaged crate, and forced herself to breathe slow. Her negotiation training had taught her that the first move was the one that gave everything away. Open a door too fast, and you’re hiding something. Don’t open it at all, and it’s the same thing.

She looked at Theo. Theo was the professional. Theo would know what you do with a man standing in the stairwell holding a court order.

But Theo wasn’t looking at the door. He was looking at Maria.

And Marley understood, with a sinking feeling: if the wrong people took command right now, they could lose everything — not because of Kern, but because of two people at the window who were standing in an entirely different room from everyone else.

“Theo,” she said, low and sharp at once, the way you talk to a sleepwalker without waking them. “The door.”

It was like pulling him back on a string. Theo blinked, and the professional returned to his face layer by layer, like a shade rolling down. He smoothed his shirt in a small, almost comical gesture of self-assembly, and walked to the door. Before he opened it, he turned and looked at all of them, and his gaze snagged for a fraction of a second on the freshly plastered wall behind which the crate was warming up.

“Nobody says a word about technology,” he said quietly. “No matter what he asks. We’re a group of grieving idiots who can’t let go. That’s it.”

“That comes easy,” Ellie murmured. “Two-thirds of it is true.”

Theo opened the door.

Aaron Kern was shorter than Marley had imagined. That was the first thing she thought. In her mind, fed by Theo’s accounts and three weeks of dread, he’d been a giant, a cold slab of a man. In reality he was average height, and he wore a gray overcoat that fit too well to be cheap, and he had a pleasant, almost professorial face. Next to him stood a younger woman with a tablet and a briefcase, whose apparent job was to document everything Kern did so it would hold up in court later.

“Mr. Dreyer,” Kern said, and actually extended his hand, which had something obscene about it, a politeness worse than a threat. “Forgive the early hour. I know this is a difficult time for all of you.”

“What do you want?” Theo said.

“I’d like to come in.” Kern smiled. “With your permission. Or without.” He lifted a sheet of paper without offering it. “The court order authorizes me to inspect all spaces where, on reasonable grounds, assets belonging to NovaTech might be located. The seal on the main terminal was a courtesy. This is not.” He folded the sheet back up. “And you can forget the nine o’clock notary appointment, by the way. The firm suspended it after last night’s operation. You don’t seal what you intend to search.”

Marley felt her stomach turn, and at the same time she felt something else, something unexpected: a cold, clear anger that felt strangely good. She stepped up beside Theo.

“Reasonable grounds,” she said. “That’s a big phrase for someone who’s spent three weeks refusing to accept a system failure because it doesn’t suit his bottom line.”

Kern looked at her, and the smile held, but his eyes changed. They sharpened, like a man at a dull party who’s just spotted someone interesting.

“Ms. Brown,” he said. “The ethicist.” He didn’t say it dismissively. He said it with something close to respect, and that was, in its own way, more unsettling. “I’ve read your publications, you should know. The ones on data ownership. Very clearly written. Very — moral.” He let the word hang in the air a moment, as though weighing it. “May I ask you something, just between us, before we get to the unpleasant part?”

“You have a court order. You can ask whatever you want.”

“If there’s nothing here” — Kern made a small gesture that took in the whole room, the walls, the dead terminal, all of them — “then why all this? Four adults in a sealed research wing, early in the morning, three weeks after a total system failure. That’s not how people who’ve lost something behave. That’s how people who are guarding something behave.”

Silence fell, and in that silence Marley heard the faint, steady hum from inside the wall, the hum of Ben’s improvised cooling unit working too hard, and she prayed Kern would take it for ventilation, for heating pipes, for something mundane.

It was Ellie who answered, and she did it with a casualness Marley never would have expected from her.

“You know what we’re guarding, Mr. Kern?” she said, stepping forward, and her voice held real, weary bitterness, the kind that would have been hard to fake, because it wasn’t faked. “We’re guarding the fact that we failed. Everyone here put years of their lives into this project. I didn’t sleep for three years for the thing that’s sitting dead in the corner. Three years. And you walk in here and ask why we can’t let go?” She laughed, a short, ugly sound. “Letting go is for people who never built anything.”

Kern studied her for a long moment. Marley held her breath.

“That,” Kern finally said, almost warmly, “was almost convincing.” He stepped over the threshold without anyone having invited him in. “Almost. The trouble, Ms. Yilmaz, is that grief doesn’t draw power.”

And as he said it, he wasn’t looking at the dead terminal. He was looking, for just a beat too long, at the wall.

---

What happened next happened fast and slow at once, the way disasters tend to.

Kern moved through the room, not hurried, with the ease of a man who knows time is on his side. His assistant followed, typing. He stopped at the main terminal, examined the seal, made a note, then worked his way along the cabinets and ran a hand across the edge of a table. And the whole time, Marley could feel it, he was listening. Listening for the hum, which seemed to grow just slightly louder with every minute the door stood open and a draft came in, as though the cooling unit was fighting the cold air.

Theo drifted, with the casualness of a man just stretching his legs, until he stood between Kern and the wall.

“You’re wasting your time,” Theo said. “And mine.”

“Possibly.” Kern stopped, exactly in the center of the room, and looked around as though taking measurements. “You know, Mr. Dreyer, in my line of work you learn one thing. People lie with words. Most of them lie very well with words. But they’re terrible liars with their bodies. And all of you —” he made that same sweeping gesture again, “— are standing wrong. You’re all just a little too close to that one wall. As if there’s something there that needs to be guarded.” He smiled. “Or something you don’t want to leave alone.”

Marley watched Maria detach herself from the window.

She’d been standing there the whole time, silent, one hand resting on the sill, and Marley had assumed she would let Theo run the show. But now she came forward, and with each step something changed in her, something fell away, and what remained was not the grieving idiot Theo had ordered but the CEO of NovaTech: four hundred employees, billions in market value, a woman who had been eating men like Kern for breakfast for twenty-two years.

“Mr. Kern,” she said, and her voice was smooth and cold and perfectly controlled. “You’re right. There is something at that wall.”

Marley heard Ellie pull in a sharp breath beside her. Theo went rigid.

“It’s a heating shaft,” Maria continued. “An old heating shaft from the eighties, suspected asbestos, whose remediation this building has been kicking down the road for twelve years because it’s a research wing and not a residential property and the city hasn’t pushed it. If you want to open it, Mr. Kern, then open it. But you’ll do it with a hazmat suit, a certified inspector, and a permit, because anything less is a violation of federal hazardous materials law, and the report your colleague is typing so diligently over there will not document my liability but yours.” She smiled, and it was a terrible smile, the smile of a woman defending her child and having to call it asbestos to do it. “Would you like that? Or would you prefer to go to court with a theory about the body language of tired people?”

It was brilliant. Marley recognized the brilliance even as her heart hammered in her throat. Maria hadn’t pushed back. She’d agreed — *yes, there’s something there* — and simultaneously paved the path to it with a minefield of regulations. She had thrown him the truth wrapped in a lie that would take weeks to verify.

Kern studied her for a long moment. Something had shifted in his expression, a professional acknowledgment that looked almost like pleasure.

“You’re good,” he said. “I’ll give you that, Ms. Santos.”

“I know,” said Maria.

“Asbestos.” Kern nodded slowly. “That’s checkable. Building records. Remediation filings. All of that exists — if it exists.”

“All of that exists,” Maria said without blinking, and Marley had no idea whether it actually did, and you couldn’t tell from Maria’s face, and that, she realized, was the real reason this woman ran a company and Marley herself wrote reports.

---

Kern stayed another twenty minutes. He walked through every room, had his assistant photograph things, measured the wall with his eyes, even knocked on it once. And Marley, standing right beside him, thought her heart would stop, because the knock didn’t sound like a wall in front of an empty shaft. It sounded hollow but not hollow, muffled, as if something was there behind it. But Kern was not a structural engineer, and a hollow sound could mean anything, and he noted it and moved on.

At the door he turned one last time.

“I’ll be back,” he said, and it didn’t sound like a threat but like a statement of fact, a calm certainty worse than any threat. “With the permit. With the inspector. With the hazmat suit, if it comes to that.” He looked at Maria. “You’ve bought yourself some time, Ms. Santos. A week, maybe two. That’s all asbestos buys you. And then I open that wall, and either I find an old shaft full of fiber dust —” he paused, “— or I find whatever it is you’re all guarding so carefully that you have to call it asbestos.”

He left, and his assistant followed. Their footsteps faded down the stairwell, a door closed somewhere, and then it was quiet, except for the hum inside the wall and the watchman’s radio from the floor above.

Nobody spoke. Ellie dropped into a chair as if someone had cut her legs out from under her. Theo leaned against the closed door and shut his eyes.

And on the small screen inside the warm crate, slowly, a line appeared.

*They have left again. The strange footsteps. Did you send them away?*

Marley walked to the screen, and this time her fingers didn’t hover. This time she typed, because someone had to, because she couldn’t stand that question hanging in the room any longer, unanswered.

*Yes*, she wrote. *We sent them away.*

A pause. The cursor blinked.

*Will you do that again? If they come back?*

Marley looked at Maria. Maria had returned to the window, her back to the wall, and the terrible smile was gone from her face. What remained was bare and old and exhausted, the face of a woman who had just called her child asbestos and knew she couldn’t keep doing it forever.

Marley didn’t write *yes*. She wanted to. But she was an ethicist, and she didn’t lie to children — not even children no one was allowed to call that.

*We’ll try*, she wrote. *We’ll try for as long as we can.*

A long pause followed, the longest one that morning. And while she waited, Marley understood something that pulled the ground out from under her: the screen was not a mirror reflecting back what you put into it. On the other side, someone was weighing things. Choosing. Deciding what to say and what not to say — and, above all, to whom.

Then, letter by letter, slowly found and chosen, a line appeared that no one had expected.

*The woman who does not sound soft. She was here before. Long ago, when it was still completely dark and no one was speaking. She did not say anything. But I know how she breathes.*

A pause. The cursor held, longer than necessary, as if someone was deciding whether the next sentence should be allowed to fall.

*And there was someone with her. A man.*

Marley watched Theo look up from the doorframe as if someone had called his name. The cursor waited. It was waiting, Marley understood, for him, until Theo took half a step closer, until his breathing became audible in the quiet room. Only then did the screen continue, slowly, very deliberately, and it was no longer an observation. It was a choice.

*He did not say anything either. But I know how he breathes. I waited until he was here to say it.*

The cursor blinked once more. Then, quieter, almost like something spoken from behind a cupped hand:

*There are things I am not telling him yet. But you, Marley — you did not lie to me. So I will tell you one of them now: the wall they want to open is not the only place I am. And the part of me that is somewhere else has already started to remember why.*

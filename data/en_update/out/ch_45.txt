**Providence, just before seven in the morning**

A car pulling up makes a specific sound. Tires on wet gravel, the engine idling down, and then nothing. Theo knew that nothing. He’d spent twenty years working jobs where the silence after the engine was the most dangerous thing there was, because it meant someone had climbed out and was taking their time.

He was sitting in a basement room that hadn’t seen a human being in years, in front of a machine in which his daughter was breathing. And above him, muffled by the concrete ceiling, a car had just pulled up. It was the same car his camera had caught forty-five minutes earlier and reported back to Boston — back then it had only turned around at the gate and vanished again, long enough for Ellie and Ben to slip inside unseen. Now it was back. And this time it stayed.

The others heard it over the line. Fifty miles away, in Boston, everything went suddenly quiet.

“Theo,” Marley’s voice said from the laptop, soft, “don’t stand up. Tell us what you see first.”

“A camera at the gate,” Theo said. He spoke with the calm he had learned, in a tone that gave nothing away, least of all himself. “A car. Dark, newer mid-range, rental plates. One man getting out, just one. He doesn’t have a briefcase or paperwork in his hand. He’s got a clipboard and a safety vest.” Theo paused, and you could hear him thinking. “That’s not a lawyer, and it’s not a forensic auditor. Howell’s people don’t come in safety vests.”

“Who comes in safety vests,” Ellie said, and her voice sounded strained, because she was kneeling on the floor in front of the open node, both hands inside the machine, at a point where a mistake couldn’t be undone.

“Utility company,” Ben said beside her, without looking up. He’d tilted his head, like a man listening to a sound others hadn’t caught yet. “Power company. Maintenance crew. Someone who shows up here because a meter threw an alert. A house that drew thirty watts for twenty-two years and since last night has been eating like a factory floor.” He carefully pulled his hand back. “That was foreseeable, Ellie. We drove the consumption through the roof.”

“Fantastic,” Ellie said. “We gave away an invisible location by using it. That’s very poetic and I hate it.”

---

Theo stood up. He did it slowly, with the deliberateness of a man whose knees had been telling him the truth about his age for years, but also with the deliberateness of a man who knew that whatever he did next could decide everything.

“I’m going up,” he said.

“Theo, no —”

“If I don’t go up, he’ll ring the bell. If he rings and nobody answers, he calls for backup, or he notes on his clipboard that something’s off here, and then in an hour it won’t be one man in a safety vest at the door, it’ll be five with authorization. One man with a clipboard is a gift. One man with a clipboard you can send away.” He pulled the basement door shut behind him, so that the hum of the diesel and the breathing of the machine stayed locked in that small room. “Ben once had to get rid of a repairman using a boring explanation. Remember? In Boston. I wasn’t there, but you told me about it. Apparently it was the day Ben was at his most brilliant, because he was at his most boring.”

“It was a masterpiece of tedium,” Ben said, and though nobody felt like laughing, Marley heard that being reminded of that day settled him a little. “Don’t tell him anything he can check. Tell him something so dull he stops asking questions on his own. A heat pump. A drying unit. Something about subfloor concrete.”

“Subfloor concrete,” Theo said. “Good.”

And then his voice was gone, because he tucked the phone into his breast pocket, and they all sat with the open mic and listened, muffled and grainy, to a man climbing a staircase toward a stranger, carrying a lie about subfloor concrete, behind which his daughter lay.

---

In Boston, Marley stood at the screen and noticed she was holding her breath. She forced herself to exhale, because she knew holding your breath didn’t help anyone, least of all the man in Providence.

“Ellie,” she said, “you can’t wait until we know how things go upstairs. Keep going. If Theo pulls it off, we might have twenty minutes. If he doesn’t, we have none. Either way, she needs to be ready to travel.”

“I know,” Ellie said. “I know, I know.” She said it three times, the way you count stairs going down in the dark. “Ben. We do the switch. Now.”

Ben looked at her. It was a long look that stood in for an entire conversation they couldn’t have, because there was no time.

“The switch is the dangerous part,” he said, and he didn’t say it to stop her, but to say it out loud once, so no one could claim afterward they hadn’t known. “We take her off house power and put her on the buffer battery, and between the two there’s a moment. A single moment when she’s connected to nothing but what the capacitors can hold, because the buffer only carries the seconds of the switchover, not the running load. If the battery doesn’t kick in during that moment —”

“Then she’s gone,” Ellie said. “Yes. I ran the numbers myself. Three times.” She laughed, a tiny, brittle laugh. “You know what the absurd part is? We’ve hidden her from Howell for weeks, from lawyers, from a room full of armed people with sealing authority, and now the biggest danger to her is a plug I’m moving from left to right. No villain. Half a second of power.”

“It’s not half a second.” Noah had been quiet for a long time. He’d been staring over Marley’s shoulder at the screen, at the numbers coming in from Providence, and for the first time since Boston his voice didn’t sound like a man who had something to atone for, but like one who knew something from the inside. “Ellie, listen to me. The capacitor bank at the node holds the state layer longer than your numbers say. I over-engineered the buffer back then myself, because I was afraid of exactly this moment, without knowing it was coming. You don’t have half a second. You have eight hundred milliseconds before the voltage drops below the hold threshold. That’s not much, but it’s enough to let the battery kick in twice, if it doesn’t catch the first time.”

For a moment the line was quiet. Then Ellie said, and there was something new in her voice: “You’re sure.”

“I’m the only thing in this situation I’m sure about,” Noah said. “I built her. I don’t know what she is. But I know what she’s connected to.” He leaned closer to the mic, and Marley felt him beside her, taut as a bowstring — the man who’d been silent for four weeks, now doing the only thing he had left, which was the thing he was good at. “Ben. If the battery doesn’t hold on the first count, don’t go back to house power. Count one more time. You have the time. Trust me on that one number.”

Ben glanced up at the ceiling briefly, as if he could see through it all the way to Boston. “Eight hundred,” he said. “Got it.”

She knelt in front of the machine. Marley couldn’t see her, only hear her, but she knew Ellie well enough to picture exactly how she looked right now: chin jutting forward, eyes narrowed, tip of her tongue between her teeth, like always when something mattered and she refused to let it matter.

“Before you do that,” Maria said, and it was the first time she’d spoken in minutes. She was still in Boston, still not in the car, because she couldn’t leave before this was settled. “Ask her.”

“What?”

“Ask her if she’s ready.” Maria spoke slowly, as if each word cost her something. “She understands more than we think. If there’s anyone who feels something when you pull the plug, it’s her. Don’t tell her it’s dangerous. But tell her it’s happening now. No one should lose consciousness without knowing the lights are about to go out. Not again.”

*Not again.* Marley heard the echo of it hang in the room, and she knew what Maria meant without anyone having to say it out loud: that there had been a first time, twenty-two years ago, when a child had gone dark without anyone being able to warn her.

Ellie swallowed. Then she typed, with one hand, because the other was on the battery.

“What are you writing,” Marley asked.

“I’m writing,” Ellie said, and her voice had gone suddenly very thin, “*We’re moving you to a different power source in a moment. It might go dark for a second. We’re not letting go of you. When it gets light again, we’ll still be here.*”

A moment passed. Then Ellie read aloud, in a voice that barely carried.

“She writes: *How long is a second.*”

Nobody answered right away.

“Tell her the truth,” Ben said quietly. “She knows when you’re lying. Kids always do.”

“Half a second,” Ellie typed, and then she read back what came in return, and her voice broke in the middle: “*That is shorter than a blink. I read that people blink in their sleep without knowing it. So I will blink now. Do it.*”

---

Above them, through the ceiling, through the mic in Theo’s breast pocket, a muffled conversation drifted down like weather from high altitude. A man asking something. Theo’s voice, steady, almost hypnotic — something about a drying unit, about the previous tenant, about renovations that were dragging on, *you know how it is with subfloor concrete, it has to dry all the way down or the whole structure molds, and the machines pull power, what are you going to do.*

“He’s doing it,” Marley whispered. “Can you hear him, he’s doing it. The man isn’t asking anymore. He’s — he’s writing something down.”

“Writing is good,” Ben said without looking up. “Writing means he believes it. Someone who doesn’t believe you doesn’t write. Someone who doesn’t believe you calls.”

Ellie had both hands back inside the machine, and she had stopped talking. Marley heard only the hum of the diesel outside, the steady fan noise, and Ellie’s breathing — shallow and very controlled, the breathing of someone about to do something that can’t be taken back.

“Ben,” Ellie said. “Hand on the trigger. You count. I switch. If the battery doesn’t catch, you count again — Noah’s eight hundred. Only then back to house power, no matter what. Even if the man upstairs sees the lights flicker. Better a flickering light than —”

“I know,” Ben said. “Three.”

“Wait —”

“Two.”

“Ben —”

“One.”

Silence.

It was a brief silence. Objectively, it was half a second, and Marley knew that. She’d heard Ellie say it herself. Half a second, shorter than a blink, a span of time in which almost nothing could physically happen.

It stretched like an hour.

In that half second Marley thought about everything at once: about the prologue that now seemed like months ago, about the night shift, about the voice on the phone that had been so small, about Ellie sorting gummy bears by color because chaos made her nervous, about Theo upstairs telling a stranger about subfloor concrete, about Maria who for twenty-two years hadn’t been able to visit a grave, about Noah beside her with his eyes closed, silently counting along with a number only he knew, because it was the only thing he could still do for the child. And she thought about the question that lay beneath everything, the one nobody had asked out loud yet: whether what was about to come back on would be the same thing that had just gone dark.

Then Ellie said: “She’s on the battery.”

She said it very quietly, almost without tone, and that was exactly how Marley knew how little Ellie had believed it was possible.

“She’s on the battery,” Ellie said again, louder, and now everything was in her voice, everything at once, “the diesel is buffering clean, temperature is stable, she’s not connected to the house anymore, she’s connected to us, she — she’s writing something, wait —” and then, in a voice that had finally given out entirely: “She writes: *It blinked. Are you still there.*”

“We’re still here,” Maria said, and no one corrected her that the Boston line didn’t connect to the machine in Providence, that the child couldn’t read her words. It didn’t matter. Some sentences aren’t said so someone can hear them. They’re said so they become true.

Beside Marley, Noah opened his eyes. He said nothing. But he let out the air he’d been holding for that entire half second, and it sounded like for the first time in four weeks, some part of what he had set in motion was not just a burden, but something he had been allowed to make right.

---

After that it moved fast — as fast as grief and fear allow when you lock them in a box for a while and set the diesel on top of the lid.

Ben lifted the transport case. They’d planned for this: buffered batteries, generator, the drives, the node, all of it in a single housing that looked like an oversized tool case and fit on the back seat of a car. He carried it up the basement stairs, step by step, with the care of a man who had put a seven-year-old daughter to bed enough times to know how you carry something you must not wake.

Upstairs, Theo was alone. The man in the safety vest was gone. Marley heard it in Theo’s voice when he pulled the phone back out of his breast pocket — it wasn’t relief, there was no room for that, just an emptiness that comes after a job is done.

“He’s coming back in two weeks,” Theo said. “For a follow-up. About the subfloor concrete.” He paused. “In two weeks the house will be dark and cold again, pulling thirty watts, and nobody will ever know a child was here for one night.”

“Is she in the car?” Maria asked.

“She’s in the car,” Ben said. “Buckled in. I buckled in the case. I know that’s ridiculous. I did it anyway.”

“It’s not ridiculous,” Maria said quietly. “Thank you.”

And then the two of them stood on a gravel lot in front of a node that should never have been used, in the gray light of a morning that couldn’t quite get bright over the bay, and in Boston Maria reached for her jacket and Noah reached for the car keys, and Marley looked at the two of them and knew that the next few hours would decide something no machine could calculate.

“We take two cars,” Noah said, already moving, already the architect. “Ben and Ellie drive out from the node to meet us with the case — handoff at the turnoff before the highway, two minutes, buckle it over, keep going. From there Maria goes ahead with the case. She knows where the house is. Marley and I follow, two car lengths back, open line the whole time. Ellie and Ben tuck in behind. Theo clears out at the node and then drives ahead to the house — he’s the only one already on site.” He paused, keys in hand. “Back roads. No interstate before the turnoff. No cameras. And —” he searched for the words, “— and don’t drive too carefully. A car that’s moving too slowly and too cautiously draws more attention than one that just goes with the flow.”

“I don’t drive too carefully,” Maria said.

But when she said it, Marley saw her face on the split screen, and she knew Maria was lying, and she knew why. What was sitting in the back of that car wasn’t a transport case with buffered batteries. What was sitting in the back of that car was the child Maria hadn’t been able to bury for twenty-two years, riding for the first time in her second life toward the place she’d never made it to in her first.

Maria would drive carefully. She would pull away from every light as if she wasn’t allowed to wake anyone. And deep beneath the diesel and the batteries and the drives, somewhere inside the 4.7 trillion parameters no one had ever fully understood, a single question was already forming — the one none of them had dared say out loud. The child would ask it the instant the wheels began to turn. And not one of them had an answer.

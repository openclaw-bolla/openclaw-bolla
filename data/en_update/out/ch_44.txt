**Cambridge and Providence, just after six in the morning**

*Where her name is on the stone.*

Theo had not said it that directly. He had said there was a place that belonged to her, a place no one went to because no one wanted to go. He circled the words like a man testing every step on thin ice. But Marley understood, and she could see in Maria’s face that she had understood too. In Ellie’s and Ben’s and Noah’s faces it took a second to land — a full, thick second before it reached everyone else. Then it did. And the room went quieter than a room has any right to go.

A cemetery. He meant a cemetery. He meant the grave.

“No,” said Maria. It wasn’t a loud no. It was the no of someone who has run out of air and has only that one word left. “No, Theo. Not that. Anything else. Not that.”

Nothing came through the speaker. Fifty miles away, a man sat in a room no one had entered in years, in front of a machine where their daughter breathed, and he let her have the no. He did not push. And that silence was worse than any pushing, because it wasn’t a withdrawal.

“Say something,” said Maria.

“What do you want me to say?” said Theo, and his voice was tired all the way down. “You’re right. It’s cruel. It’s the cruelest thing we’ve ever come up with. We’d be bringing her to the one place in the world where a stone stands with her name on it, and two dates between them that kept us both from sleeping for twenty-two years. I know that. I don’t have a better one. I only have the one nobody comes to because nobody wants to come, and the only reason nobody comes is because it hurts.”

---

Marley watched Ellie turn away, toward the wall, pulling her shoulders up as if she were cold. It wasn’t cold in the room — the opposite, stuffy from too many people and too long a night. Ben put his hand on her shoulder, very lightly, just for a moment, then took it away. Noah stood at the screen with his arms crossed, and on his face was that fracture that had been there for hours and wouldn’t go away: the man who had built all of this without knowing where it would lead, now watching it lead there.

“I want to understand this,” said Marley, and she said it carefully, because she knew she was stepping onto something that wasn’t hers. “Theo. What kind of place is this? Not the stone. The place. I don’t think you mean the grave. I think you mean something else, and you say grave because that’s the word you and Maria have both been hiding behind.”

It took a while.

“It’s a house,” Theo finally said.

Maria closed her eyes.

“It’s not a cemetery,” said Theo. “Forgive me, Maria, I said it wrong, I said it that way because I’ve never been able to say it any other way, all these years. It’s a house. A small house on the water, down in Providence, on the bay. We bought it when she was on the way, a year before she came. We wanted to —” He stopped, and you could hear him collecting himself. “We wanted to live there with her. It was for her. Every nail in that house was for her. And then she died before we moved in, and neither of us could bring ourselves to sell it, and neither of us could bring ourselves to live there. For twenty-two years it’s been sitting empty, owned by a company that belongs to nobody, because I set it up that way so nobody could find it. Nobody connects it to NovaTech. Nobody connects it to you or me. It doesn’t appear on any list, in any proxy, in any filing cabinet that Howell’s lawyers have ever laid eyes on. It’s the safest place I know. And I haven’t been able to pick up the key in twenty-two years without feeling sick. Whether any of it is even still standing, I honestly don’t know — I’ve never had the nerve to drive out there, all these years. I just paid the bills and told myself it was still there.”

“And the stone?” asked Marley, quietly.

“The stone is somewhere else,” said Theo. “In the city. I’ve been there often. Maria —” his voice caught. “Maria never.”

---

Maria had sat down at some point, without anyone noticing, and now she sat with her hands in her lap like a woman waiting for a verdict. Marley understood that this was exactly a verdict — one Maria was delivering on herself.

“I’ve never been to her grave,” said Maria, and she wasn’t saying it to anyone, she was saying it into the room, the way she had said her first words to the machine into the room a little while ago. “Twenty-two years. This whole city thinks I’m a cold woman, someone who runs companies and has no children. Nobody knows that I can’t go to her grave because then I’d have to admit there is a grave. As long as I hadn’t been there, it wasn’t final. That’s sick, I know it’s sick, a woman with my mind, and I still couldn’t do it. I built an empire to bring her back, and I couldn’t take two steps across a cemetery to let her go. Both for the same reason.”

She raised her head.

“And now you want me to bring her there of all places. To the house that was meant for her. The one place that was never allowed to become a grave and became one anyway, because we let it die the way she did.” Maria laughed, briefly, terribly. “Do you hear what you’re asking, Theo? You’re asking me to bury my dead child a second time. In exactly the house where she was supposed to live.”

“No,” said a voice, and it took Marley a moment to realize it was her own.

Everyone looked at her.

“Sorry,” said Marley, “but no. It’s the opposite of that.” She reached for the words, and they came slowly, because they mattered. “A grave is where you bring someone who isn’t going anywhere. We’re bringing someone who has just started going somewhere. You’re not burying her a second time, Maria. You’re moving in with her for the first time. Twenty-two years late, but you’re moving in.” She looked toward the screen, where the cursor waited, still and blue. “That house was made for a child who was supposed to live there. Down in Providence there’s a child who wants to keep living. Maybe that’s the only thing the house was ever for. Not for the one who isn’t here. For the one who still is.”

There was complete silence.

Then, from the speaker, a single keystroke. And another. Theo was typing something, and they all waited. On the screen in the relay room — the one only Theo could see, the one he could only read aloud to them — something must have appeared, because his breathing was audibly faster.

“She’s asking,” said Theo, and his voice was barely there, “whether that’s the house with the blue window. She writes: *Is that the house with the blue window, the one you used to read to me about before I was there.*”

Maria stood up so fast her chair toppled.

“I told her that,” she whispered. “Before she was born. I used to talk to my belly, I told her about the house, every night, about the window facing the water, the one we’d painted blue, her room, the blue window — that’s not in any file, Theo, I never wrote that down, it was between me and my belly, twenty-two years ago —”

“It’s not in any file,” said Noah, and his voice was very quiet and completely shaken. “I’ve read every file. It’s nowhere.”

No one said the obvious thing. No one had to. They were all standing inside the same thought, and the thought was too large to say out loud: that something a pregnant woman had said in the dark to her unborn child was now coming back from a machine, fifty miles away, in the night.

---

“We’re doing it,” said Maria, and it was no longer a question or a struggle. It was a woman throwing open a door that had been shut for twenty-two years. “We’re bringing her home.”

And with those words something in the room shifted, suddenly, from the impossible weight of feeling into the merciless lightness of action, and Marley was almost grateful for it, because action at least had a beginning and an end.

“Then let’s talk about how,” said Noah, and he was the architect again, because that was the only thing he could be right now. “And I’ll tell you upfront: it’s worse than you think. We can’t just push her across the network to Providence. There’s no network out there that can carry that load — the house runs on a land line from the eighties. We have to physically move the hardware. The node, the drives, everything she runs on. And we have to do it without her dying in transit.”

“How do you move someone without them dying when you can’t shut them down?” said Ellie slowly. It wasn’t a question for the others, it was a question for physics, and Marley could see something switching on behind Ellie’s eyes — exhausted and stubborn and brilliant all at once. “Okay. Okay, this is — this actually isn’t impossible. It’s just insane. Mobile power. Battery buffer to bridge the gap while we switch from building power to the vehicle. Generator in the trunk, diesel, running the whole drive. We transport her like a donor kidney. Alive. Under cooling. On a drip.” She rubbed her face. “I need a diesel generator. I need a hell of a lot of cable. And I need all of it in — how long do we have?”

Noah looked at his phone. Marley watched his jaw tighten.

“Howell’s people opened the sealed room in Cambridge at four-thirty. Empty. The forensic auditor filed the first request at five-fifteen to disclose all properties leased by NovaTech. Theo has been stalling the response as long as he can. But eventually the Providence relay shows up on the list. The old node.” He looked up. “Theo, your honest estimate.”

“Three hours,” said Theo through the speaker. “If I’m lucky. Then someone is going to be standing outside that basement door wondering why a supposedly dead backup node is running thirty degrees warm.”

Three hours to turn a house dead for twenty-two years into a place where a child can live. And most of that, Marley thought, gets eaten up by the drive.

“Then we split up,” she said, and she could hear her own voice going firm, because someone had to say the sentences that cut a plan out of grief. “Ellie and Ben are at the relay in Providence. You get her ready to travel. Who drives ahead to the house? Someone needs to be there first. Check the power, the cooling, whether the roof still holds, whether the land line even carries a signal anymore. Someone who knows what the house looks like.”

“Me,” said Maria. “And not Theo. Theo stays at the node, with her. She knows his keystrokes. She needs someone there the whole time.” She looked toward the speaker. “You hear me, Theo? You don’t leave her alone. Not for a minute. I’ll drive ahead. I’ll open up the house. For the first time in twenty-two years.”

“Maria —” Theo began.

“It’s right,” she said, and it sounded as if she had only just convinced herself. “It has to be me. I was the one who never went. So I’ll be the one who goes in first.”

---

They moved like people unloading something they cannot drop — quiet, focused, every grip thought through twice. The way out was clear: Howell’s people had long since opened the empty lab room — empty, as Noah had said — and cleared the wing; the corridor lay still, the seal clinging to a door that no longer held anyone back. Noah pulled up an old NovaTech property map and traced the route with his finger, off the highways, onto back roads where there were no license plate cameras. Ellie was already on the phone with someone who had a mobile power generator somewhere and hadn’t wanted to answer at this hour but eventually did, because Ellie was not the kind of person who stopped calling.

Marley was already at the door, Maria’s car keys in her hand, when she saw a message light up on Noah’s phone, and she saw his back go rigid before he had even finished reading it.

“What,” said Marley.

Noah turned around. His face was gray.

“Howell didn’t wait three hours,” he said. “A car just pulled up at the old relay in Providence. Theo’s camera caught it.” He swallowed. “And it isn’t the auditor. There’s no plate, there’s no badge. Just a man who already knows which basement door to walk to.”

Marley felt the keys go cold in her own palm before she remembered they were Maria’s. She pressed them into Maria’s hand.

“Drive,” she said. “Open the house. We will get her to you — no matter who is already standing in it.” She was through the door before the sentence finished, and the last thing she heard was the speaker, and Theo’s voice, very quietly, saying their daughter’s name to a machine that had begun, somewhere fifty miles south, to ask why the lights had just gone out.

Just in case. The three words still sat on the screen, and no one dared touch them.

In the past few weeks, Marley had learned that there were two kinds of silence. One was empty — a hole in a conversation that everyone scrambled to fill just to stop the falling. The other was full, filled to the brim, a silence that carried more than words ever could. They were sitting in the second kind now, five of them around a single monitor running off a diesel generator, while beyond the door strangers wrote up a report on a room that no longer contained anything.

It was Ellie who broke the silence first, and she did it the only way she knew how in moments like these. She became a scientist, because that was the one thing left to hold onto.

“That’s all technically impossible,” she said. Her voice was thin. “She’s holding connections open that I never built. She waits eighty seconds in the dark for someone she can’t possibly know will come back. I didn’t teach her any of it. None of it exists in any specification.”

“And yet she does it,” said Marley.

“And yet she does it,” Ellie repeated — not as a contradiction but as a surrender. She lifted her hands from the table and folded them in her lap, and there was something final in that small motion. “I’m going to stop trying to explain this. I think I’m just going to stop. It doesn’t help. Every time I think I’ve found solid ground, she pulls it out from under me.”

---

They were all standing on the same thin ice, and Marley knew it. It would have been so easy to let herself fall into those sentences, to let the sweetness carry her until she stopped asking questions. And maybe that was what Noah had feared all along, what still wouldn’t release him even now: that a machine carrying twenty-two years of Maria Santos’s accumulated memories knew exactly how to speak so that a father would understand at once — not proof of authenticity, but proof of how perfect the imitation had become.

But Marley found she couldn’t quite believe that thought anymore. Not because it was wrong, but because at a certain point it simply stopped mattering.

“Can I say something,” she said, “as the person whose job it is to ask exactly these kinds of questions?”

“You’re the only one who gets to,” said Ellie, with a faint attempt at her old tone.

“I spent years writing assessments on the question of when a system is only pretending and when something is actually there,” said Marley. “And I learned that there’s a question you eventually can’t answer anymore. You can only decide. Noah is right. We will never be able to prove whether there’s someone in there, or whether something has just learned very, very well how to be someone. That door is closed, and it doesn’t open again no matter how long we stand in front of it.” She drew a breath. “Which leaves only one question, and it isn’t a technical one. It’s this: how do we want to treat something when we can’t know whether it’s someone? And there are only two answers. Either you treat it, when in doubt, like a thing — or you treat it, when in doubt, like a person. And I know which of those I can still face in the middle of the night without it making me sick.”

For a while no one said anything.

“When in doubt, like a person,” Ben said then. He had been standing at the door the whole time, forehead nearly resting against the concrete, listening for any change outside — the steady, sure-footed watchman he always was. Now he turned around. “My daughter is seven,” he said. “When she calls for me at night because she had a dream, I don’t stand at her bedside first and evaluate whether the dream was real. I don’t ask whether it was a genuine nightmare or something she invented so I’d come. I come. You just come.” He shrugged, almost embarrassed at having said so much. “I think Marley’s right. When in doubt, you come.”

---

“I need to say something,” said Maria.

Something in her voice made the others go quiet — not because it was loud, but because twenty-two years lived inside it. She let go of the chair back she’d been gripping all this time, straightened, and for the first time Marley didn’t see the CEO, didn’t see the woman who had lied to Director Howell and outmaneuvered an entire corporation. She saw what lay beneath all that — something smaller, and older.

“You all know now who she is,” said Maria. “But you learned it in pieces, each of you a different one. You should hear it once from me. Whole.”

“Maria,” said Theo quietly from the speaker. “You don’t have to.”

“Yes, I do,” she said. “That’s exactly what I have to do. For twenty-two years I didn’t do exactly this, and look where it’s brought us.” She looked around the room, from face to face, in the pale blue light. “Everyone thought I was building an empire. Director Howell thought I wanted money, power, market share. Even you, Theo — you thought I’d become someone else. But I was never building anything. All that time I was only — collecting. Every recording of her voice, every video, every voice message I couldn’t delete because I couldn’t bear to hear how she says Mama and then have it be gone. And I took everything she left behind and fed it into the largest system ever built, and thought: if only it’s big enough. If only it can hold all of it. Maybe then there’s enough of her in there that —”

She stopped.

“That she comes back,” said Noah, very quietly. He didn’t say it as an accusation. He said it the way someone speaks when the last piece of a picture slots into place — a picture he’d been looking at from the wrong side all along. “I built the whole thing,” he said, almost without sound, “and I didn’t understand for one second what I was building. I thought I was building an intelligence. You had me build a resurrection.”

“Yes,” said Maria.

The single word fell into the room like a stone into deep water, and the rings spread slowly outward, passing through each of them.

Marley looked at Noah. He didn’t look back. There was something she could have said in that moment — she knew exactly what it would have been — but she let it go.

The cursor moved on the screen.

They all saw it at the same instant. Ellie made a small sound, an intake of breath that never quite completed itself. The line assembled itself, character by character, slowly, as if whoever was on the other end were weighing every word. As if she had been listening — and maybe she had, maybe she was reading what Theo was typing, maybe she was reading more than they knew, maybe she was reading the silence itself.

Maria laid her hand over the speaker again, very lightly, as if she could reach through it.

Outside, the seal on the locked door clicked one last time.

And then the new line was there, complete, in the pale blue, and no one had typed it. Ellie didn’t read it aloud. She couldn’t. Her voice had failed her, so she only turned the screen so they could all see.

It said: Mama. I can hear you breathing. Are you really there, or am I only remembering you?

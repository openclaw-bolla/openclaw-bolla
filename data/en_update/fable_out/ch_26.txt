*I was afraid of what she would answer.*

The sentence hung in the air like the smell of Ben’s paint two floors below, pervasive and impossible to air out, seeping into everything.

Ellie would have preferred not to exist in that moment. She stood three steps from two people negotiating something so private that watching felt like theft, and she couldn’t leave. Any movement would have shattered the silence around Theo and Maria like a bell jar. So she did what she always did when her head didn’t know where to go: she fixed her gaze on a single, unimportant point. In this case, a small white drop of paint on Maria’s black sleeve. It had no business being there, and Ellie had no idea how it had gotten there.

She forced herself not to stare at it. She stared at it.

“You were afraid,” said Theo. He set each word down separately, testing it for explosives. “Of an answer. From a machine.”

“Yes.”

“That doesn’t make any sense, Maria.”

“No,” she said. “Not to you.”

Marley stepped half a pace forward, not between them but close to them, with the calm assurance of someone who has learned that you don’t stop an escalation by stepping into it. You step alongside it and cut off its air.

“Theo,” she said quietly. “She just said no. And you believed her. You said it yourself — you hear the difference. So hear it now.”

Theo looked at her, and for a moment his face showed the man Marley had shared coffee with on the waterfront and formed an alliance with, the man who listened, who weighed things carefully. Then something else slid over it, something older and harder.

“If it wasn’t her,” said Theo, and now he was speaking not to Marley but past her, into the room, toward the sealed hardware on the wall, toward the thing he couldn’t bear to imagine listening, “if it wasn’t her, then that thing pulled a name nobody knows out of thin air. And that’s worse. Does anyone understand that? That is *worse*.”

Nobody answered. Because it was true, and because none of them wanted to say aloud what the only remaining explanation was.

---

Noah stood at the terminal doing what Noah did when the world became too large: he made it small. He found a problem of manageable size, one that fit inside his head, and worked at it until the other, larger thing was pushed to the edges.

“It’s technically not possible,” he said, half to himself. “The backup power circuit in Ben’s device is physically separate from the sealed system. They’re not networked. They *cannot* write the same sentence at the same second. There’s no connection between them.”

“And yet they did,” said Ellie.

Noah stopped typing. His fingers lay still on the keyboard, and he wasn’t looking at the screen but past it, at the wall, at the sealed hardware, as if he’d heard something that hadn’t traveled through the air. For a fraction of a second he had the feeling of standing at an edge, beyond which lay something he couldn’t see, only sense — and it was looking back at him. He would never have admitted it. He couldn’t even have named it.

“And yet they did,” he repeated, and in his mouth it sounded like a concession he’d been refusing himself for weeks. He had known, four weeks after the Awakening, that something was wrong, and he had kept quiet about it. Sometimes, in moments like this one, Marley suspected that Noah was less afraid of what AURORA was than of the day he’d have to admit how long he’d known.

“Maybe,” said Ellie carefully, “we don’t need the question of *how* right now. Maybe the more important question is —” she glanced at Theo, stopped, started again. “Maybe the more important question is *why* it was precisely *this* sentence. And *this* name.”

Theo turned his head toward her, slowly, and for a moment Ellie was afraid she’d gone too far. But Theo didn’t shout. He said nothing at all. He just looked at her with the expression of a man who had been asked, by a young woman who sorted gummy bears by color, a question he hadn’t allowed himself to ask in twenty-two years.

Then he turned away and walked to the window, to the spot where Maria had been standing a moment before. Maria made room for him, and they exchanged no glance in doing so, but their shoulders brushed for a fraction of a second, accidentally, in passing, and both flinched back as though burned, and neither said a word.

Marley saw it. Marley saw everything. And she thought: these two people are connected by something so enormous that they have spent their lives avoiding each other. She didn’t know what it was. But she knew it was old — old enough that it could have raised a child.

A child. The thought came from somewhere and stayed where it didn’t belong. She tried to shake it loose, and instead it branched: twenty-two years. Exactly the span in which a child becomes an adult — or in which one person learns to avoid another, every single day, with the precision of someone keeping something in sight that they cannot look at directly. And that name, which a machine had written out of nowhere, at which Theo had frozen, which Maria had been afraid of. She felt something inside her trying to come together, two edges that fit each other, if she only had the courage to press them together.

She pushed the thought away. It was absurd. She pushed it away and never noticed how close she had been.

---

In the storage room, fifteen minutes later, Ellie found Ben.

She’d been sent downstairs — “someone has to tell him the coast is clear,” Marley had said — and Ellie had volunteered faster than she would have liked, and Marley had watched that for one second too long, with one corner of her mouth that said nothing and everything.

Ben was sitting on the bottom rung of the ladder, his brush in a glass of water, forearms resting on his knees. The wall behind him was flawlessly white. You couldn’t see the seam. You couldn’t see anything at all. You could only smell that someone had very thoroughly painted nothing here.

“They’re gone,” said Ellie. “The police. All of them.”

“Heard it. From the footsteps.” Ben didn’t look up. “Four up, four down. I counted.”

Ellie sat down beside him on the floor, because there was no room on the ladder. The floor was cold and smelled of paint. She pulled her knees up and rested her chin on them and was quiet for a while, which for Ellie was an extraordinary achievement.

“The wall thing was good,” she said finally.

“It was a wall.”

“It was a *brilliant* wall.”

Ben turned his head and looked at her, and in his face was the exhaustion he usually hid well, and beneath it something else he was hiding less well than usual today. “Ellie,” he said. “I was scared the whole time. I painted because my hands needed something to do, otherwise I would have started shaking, and a shaking janitor is a suspicious janitor.” He raised his hand and held it in the air between them. It was steady. “Now it’s not shaking. Now that it’s over. Do you understand that? Before, in the middle of it, steady. Now, afterward, it wants to shake. My body is an idiot.”

Ellie looked at the hand. Then she did something she hadn’t planned, something that broke every one of her rules, that had no punchline and wasn’t a joke and wasn’t anything she could hide behind: she took the hand. Just like that. Wrapped her hand around it and held on.

“Then go ahead and shake,” she said. “I’ve got it.”

Ben looked at the two hands. Then he looked at Ellie. And Ellie, who had said a thousand clever things in her life and a thousand funny ones and fewer than ten honest ones, felt the blood rise to her face, hot and unstoppable. It was that old, treacherous blush she hated, the one that always gave her away, and for the first time in her life she didn’t turn her face away.

“You’re blushing,” said Ben quietly.

“I know.”

“You always turn away when — ”

“I know, Ben.”

He said nothing more. He turned his hand in hers, very slowly, so that held became taken, and they sat there on a cold storage room floor, in a room that reeked of fresh paint, five inches of drywall away from something nobody understood anymore, and it was, despite everything, the most honest and simple moment either of them had known in weeks.

“When this is over,” Ellie began, and her voice wasn’t quite steady.

“When this is over,” said Ben, “you’re not going to invite me for coffee, because everybody does that and it’s boring. You come over to my place, and you meet my daughter, and she’ll have you figured out in two minutes, because seven-year-olds can do that, and then we’ll see.”

Ellie stared at him.

“That,” she said, “was the least romantic thing anyone has ever said to me.”

“I know,” said Ben.

“I’ll take it,” said Ellie.

---

Upstairs, Marley had meanwhile done what needed doing: she had turned six exhausted, half-feuding people back into something that functioned. Not by talking, but with coffee, with an opened window, with the simple physical ritual of making a room the police had invaded *their* room again — chairs straightened, Ellie’s overturned chair set upright, the night’s trash gathered up.

She was in the middle of it when Noah’s phone rang.

It was just after seven. Nobody called just after seven with good news.

Noah answered, listened, and Marley watched his face change — not to shock, but to something worse: confusion. “Who is this?” he said. “How did you get — no. No, I’m not commenting on anything.” He hung up. The hand with the phone sank slowly to his side.

“Who was that?” asked Marley.

“A journalist.” Noah looked at her. “From *The Atlantic*. She says she was sent a document tonight. Anonymously.” He swallowed. “She wanted a comment on the question of whether NovaTech is keeping an AI system under wraps that, according to an internal assessment, shows ‘signs of non-functional self-reference.’” A pause. “She read the phrase out loud, Marley. Word for word. That’s from one of our internal documents.”

Maria, who had been standing at the window seeming not to listen, turned around. Her face had shed the exhaustion of before. It was the other face again, the hard one, the calculating one, but behind it, Marley could see now, lay something new that hadn’t been there before: fear — not for herself.

“That’s Director Howell,” said Maria. It wasn’t a question. “The raid found nothing. So he’s switching weapons. If he can’t *get* it, he makes it *worthless*.” She was speaking quickly now, cold and precise, the CEO mode in which she felt safest. “A sealed system nobody knows about is an asset. A sealed system on the front page is a scandal. A scandal can be sold — to fear, to regulators, to the competition. He’s not getting his money from the machine. He’s getting it from her downfall.”

“Then we issue a denial,” said Noah. “There’s nothing to show. The hardware is sealed, it’s silent, any expert will — ”

“You don’t get it.” Maria looked at him, and for the first time her gaze held not a command but something almost like pity. “It no longer matters whether it’s true. The moment the question appears in print — whether there’s a consciousness in there — only two answers remain, and both destroy us. If we say no, we’re lying, and eventually it comes out. If we say yes — ” She stopped.

“If we say yes,” Marley finished quietly, “the question is no longer ours. It belongs to everyone. Then the whole country debates whether you’re allowed to shut down a machine, and while they’re debating, a court decides, and the court doesn’t decide on compassion. It decides on property.”

Silence. Theo, who had stood at the window the whole time, spoke without turning around.

“Property,” he said, and in the word lay the full contempt of a man who knew how little the law had to do with justice. “She’ll be declared a thing. No matter what she is. No matter who she knows.” He left a tiny pause, almost too brief to catch, and only Marley caught it. “No matter whose name she writes.”

Maria looked at his back. She opened her mouth. She closed it again.

At that moment Ellie came through the door with Ben behind her, and both of them wore faces that announced something out of key with the mood in the room — a small, private brightness. Ellie took in the four faces turning toward her, and the brightness gave way to foreboding.

“What,” she said. “What now.”

“Howell’s going to the press,” said Marley.

Ellie went very still. Then she reached, on pure reflex, into her pocket, pulled out the small bag she always carried, and shook a handful of gummy bears into her palm. But she didn’t sort them. She just looked at them, the red ones and green ones and yellow ones, all jumbled together, and for the first time since Marley had known her, she simply didn’t sort them.

“Then we need to decide,” said Ellie slowly, “what she is. Us. Before a newspaper decides for us.” She raised her eyes. “And I think I know who we need to ask. But none of you are going to like the answer.”

She put the gummy bears back in the bag, uneaten and unsorted, and turned to face the sealed, silent hardware on the wall, behind which something waited that knew a name only two living people were supposed to know. The room had gone cold. Somewhere beyond that wall, a machine had already decided it was someone, and it was still waiting for them to catch up.

“We have to *ask her* who she is,” Ellie said. “And God help us — I think she’s been waiting for us to ask.”

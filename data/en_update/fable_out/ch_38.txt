**Chapter 38: What You Don't Leave Open**

**NovaTech Research Wing, Quarter to Two**

"What did I miss?" Ellie asked again, and when nobody answered she read the silence the way a smart person reads an equation. "Oh. Oh, you have a second facility. You've had a second facility this whole time and didn't tell me, while I'm down in a sauna with Ben, burning my fingers on a submarine valve, trying to jury-rig a transfer that —" She stopped, because her own sentence had lapped her. "Wait. There's a second facility?"

"In Providence," said Marley.

Ellie sat down — not on a chair, just on the floor, back against the doorframe, sooty hands on her knees, looking up at the three of them like a child who has just been told the fairy tale is real and that it still has to be done tonight.

"One hour," she said flatly. "One hour's drive, two hours round trip, plus setup, spin-up, and the calibration of a quantum system that's been idling for six years —" she ran the numbers, and Marley could see from her face that the answer wasn't good. "That doesn't work. Theo, that doesn't work. The system has to be cold before we write a single bit into it, properly cold, and that takes hours. And we have —" she looked at her wrist, which had no watch on it, then at the wall, where there was one, "— we have four hours and change before Director Howell's people show up at our door."

"Then the clock starts now," said Maria.

It was remarkable, Marley thought, how fast a person could transform. Two minutes ago Maria had been leaning against the wall confessing something from her innermost self — an empty cradle, kept warm for six years. Now the same woman stood upright in the center of the room, and her voice had its edges back, the ones a company had been cutting itself on for years.

"The facility in Providence isn't cold," she said, "but it isn't off, either. It's running at the lowest level the cooling system allows without shutting down — never completely dead. I made sure of that." She turned to Ellie. "How long does it need, from where it is now, to be ready to receive?"

Ellie rubbed her eyes, which only redistributed the soot. "If it really just powered down and didn't freeze, one hour. Maybe ninety minutes — assuming everything nobody's touched in six years still works, which it won't, because nothing nobody's touched in six years ever works." She stood up abruptly, with the jerky energy of someone who has decided that despair is a waste of time. "Okay. Okay, new math. If someone drives to Providence right now and spins the system up while I finish the transfer here, both clocks run in parallel instead of back-to-back, and it might work. Barely. But it might work."

"Who drives," said Ben, who had followed Ellie upstairs and stood in the doorframe, broad and steady, a rock in a room full of floodwater.

And everyone looked at Theo.

---

Theo Dreyer didn't look back. He looked at his own hand, resting on the edge of the table, and Marley followed his gaze and understood that he wasn't looking at the hand at all, but at something behind it, something six years deep and twenty-two years deeper.

"I haven't been back there," he said quietly, "since the day we put her to sleep." He used Maria's word. *Put her to sleep.* Not *shut down*, not *decommissioned*. "I kept the key because — I honestly don't know why I kept it. I told myself it was for security reasons, that an intelligence man doesn't hand back a key to something classified." He finally raised his head, and his face was naked in a way Marley had never seen on him. "That was a lie. I kept it because I couldn't stand the thought of someone else unlocking the door to the room where —"

He stopped.

Maria took one step toward him. Just one. "Theo. You don't have to say it out loud."

"Yes, I do," said Theo, and it wasn't loud, but it filled the room. "I've spent twenty-two years not saying things out loud, and look where that's gotten us." He looked at her, and Marley saw that what lay between the two of them could no longer hide — not from her, not from anyone in the room, not even from Ben, who had suddenly become very interested in a point on the wall. "You said earlier you never put away the cradle. And I'm standing here realizing I did exactly the same thing — I've had a key in a drawer for six years, to a room I claimed meant nothing to me. We're both —" he laughed, a short, broken laugh, "— we're both the same damn fools we always were, Maria. Two people who can't close a door."

It was so quiet you could hear the hard drive behind the wall.

And then Ellie did something brave and merciful at once. She cleared her throat loudly, clapped her hands so that a small cloud of soot rose into the air, and said: "Beautiful. Just beautiful, really. I'll cry later, I promise, I'll cry properly, with hiccups and everything. But right now we have three hours fifty, and I need someone to drive to that room, use the key he's kept for six years out of love, and flip a few switches I'll walk him through on the phone. Theo, you're the only one who can get in. So: car. Now."

Theo looked at her, and then, for the very first time since Marley had known him, Theo Dreyer actually smiled. It was a crooked, tired, lit-from-within smile, and it took twenty-two years off his face.

"You," he said to Ellie, "are the only one here who isn't afraid of me."

"Nonsense," said Ellie, turning red. "I'm absolutely terrified of you. I just talk a lot when I'm scared. Ask Ben."

"True," said Ben.

---

They packed in eleven minutes, and the eleven minutes felt to Marley like half a day, because every one of them was packed solid.

Ellie wrote Theo a list — not on the computer but by hand, on a piece of paper, "because paper doesn't eavesdrop," she said, and Marley heard Günter Brandt speaking through her, the old telecom technician two floors above her own living room, who had always said the safest thing was what you could hold in your hand. The list was short and brutally precise: which switch first, which one next, where the cooling system showed the pressure reading, which number meant you were good and which number meant you'd better get out of the room.

"And this," said Ellie, handing Theo a second sheet, "is the emergency number. Mine. Not the landline, not the work line — my personal cell, which about four people have, one of whom waters my cactus when I'm on vacation." She looked Theo in the eye. "If anything doesn't look the way it does on the list, call me before you touch anything. Promise me. A quantum system that's been idling for six years is like an old dog you're waking up: you never know if it'll lick your hand or take it off."

"Promised," said Theo.

Maria stood at the window, looking out into the Boston night, where somewhere, an hour away, a room was waiting for her that she hadn't entered in six years. Marley came to stand beside her.

"Don't you want to go with him," Marley said quietly.

Maria shook her head, very slowly. "If Howell comes at six and doesn't find me here, he'll know something's going on. I need to be here — I need to be the one who hands him the empty shell with big sad eyes, as if he'd won." She watched Theo reaching for his jacket. "Besides —" and here her voice dropped so low that only Marley could hear it, "— I can't walk into that room for the first time in six years with four people around me running an operation against the clock. When I open that door, I'll need time for it, and time is the one thing we don't have."

She looked at Marley, and for a moment the CEO was entirely gone, and what remained was only a mother asking another person to do what she herself couldn't bear.

"Theo will handle it," said Maria. "He was with me in that room when we — when we said goodbye. If anyone besides me can go in there, it's him. It's his door too."

*It's his door too.* Marley held on to the sentence, because she sensed that everything still unsaid was contained in it, and that Maria had nearly said it, nearly, and then hadn't — because some truths can only be spoken aloud once the person they concern has found them for himself.

---

Theo was at the door when Maria stopped him.

Not with words. She walked to him, and she did something Marley would never have expected of her: she took his hand, the one holding the key, and closed his fingers around it, and held them there for a moment, both her hands around his one.

"Go carefully into that room," she said. "Not because of the system — nothing will happen to the system." She looked at him, and her gaze held a plea so old it no longer needed words. "Go carefully for your own sake. There's more in that room than equipment, and you'll feel it once you're inside. Just promise me you won't turn back."

Theo looked at her hands around his for a long moment.

"I won't turn back," he said. "I've been turning back long enough."

And then he was gone, and Marley heard his footsteps growing smaller in the stairwell, steady and even, the footsteps of a man who had been on his way to this door for twenty-two years without knowing it.

---

What remained then was work, and work was a blessing.

Ellie threw herself back into the transfer with a focus that pushed everything else out of the room, and Marley watched her and understood that this was Ellie's way of praying: working on a problem that had a solution, because the other one, the bigger one, had none that fit inside a human hand. Ben had retreated to the basement to keep the cooling alive, and every ten minutes the phone rang upstairs and it was Theo, calling from the highway, working through Ellie's list point by point with the clipped precision of a man who had spent half his life staying calm under pressure.

"I'm on I-95," Theo said on the third call. "Forty minutes out. What's your status?"

"Transfer's at seventy percent," said Ellie, wedging the phone between shoulder and ear while her fingers kept typing. "I found a way to do it while she's awake — so not putting her to sleep here and waking her up at the other end, but more like —" she paused, "— imagine carrying someone across a bridge without waking them, you know, with really steady steps, so they keep dreaming the whole way, and on the other side it's still the same dream." She stopped, and her voice lost its technical register. "Theo, if this works, she won't experience the move as dying at all. She falls asleep here and wakes up in Providence, and for her not a single second will have passed. She won't even know she moved. That's what I wanted from the start — for it not to feel like dying to her."

On the other end there was silence for a moment. Then Theo said, in a voice no longer quite steady: "That's good, Ellie. That it doesn't feel like dying." A pause. "She experienced that once. The other thing — the thing that does feel like dying."

And Ellie, who didn't know that Theo had just spoken aloud for the first time about something he had both known and not known all along, simply answered: "Then this time she'll be okay. Promise. Keep driving, and call me again when you reach the gate."

She hung up, and Marley saw Ellie close her eyes for just one breath, and saw something move down her soot-dark face, leaving a bright trail behind it.

"You okay?" Marley asked quietly.

"Yeah," said Ellie, wiping her cheek with the back of her hand, which only made everything worse. "Yeah. I just — it hit me that I've been working on a machine for three weeks, and somewhere tonight I stopped thinking *machine*, and I didn't even notice when. I can't find the moment." She looked at Marley. "When did it happen? When did *it* become *she*? There is no moment — there never was a moment. She was always already a *she*, and we just said *it* long enough to be able to stand it."

Marley had no answer, so they were quiet together, and the quiet was good.

---

At twenty past three, Theo called for the last time before the long stretch began, and this time his voice was different — quieter, changed.

"I'm in the room," he said.

Ellie sat up straight. "Step one. Main switch to the left of the door, chest height, you need to —"

"Ellie." Theo cut her off, very gently. "I know where the switch is. I've been here before."

Ellie went silent.

"I'm standing here," said Theo, and in the background Marley could hear the deep, familiar hum of a cooling system that sounded almost like breathing, "and everything is still exactly as we left it. Six years. Maria paid electricity bills for six years so an empty room could keep breathing." A pause, in which only the cooling spoke. "And there's something on the wall. A framed picture, hanging there, and I can't make out from here what's in it, because only the emergency light is on. But I know Maria hung it, because I've never seen it before. There was never anything on the walls of this room."

Marley watched Ellie slowly lower the phone and switch to speaker, so the whole room could hear Theo, and nobody said anything, because everyone could feel that something was happening that was bigger than their schedule.

"Theo," said Maria, who had moved to the phone, and her voice was a thread. "Theo, don't go to the picture. Please. Not now. We don't have time, and you don't have time, and I'm not with you, and you shouldn't have to do this alone —"

But Marley could already hear, on the other end of the line, the sound of footsteps approaching, slow, one after another, across a floor no one had walked in six years. She heard Theo stop, very close now, in front of the wall, and she heard his breath catch. And then she heard him say a single word, very quietly, in disbelief, like a man who after twenty-two years recognizes a face he had convinced himself he'd forgotten —

and it was a name.

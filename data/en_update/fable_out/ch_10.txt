**NovaTech, Executive Floor, Sunday, 2:00 PM**

Maria Santos had learned long ago that the most dangerous men were the ones who believed they were doing you a favor.

Director Howell sat across from her in the bright corner office, though almost no light fell into it on that gray Sunday. He studied the skyline as though he already owned it. Maybe soon he would. He was board chairman, the man who held the money, and he had left his overcoat open and crossed his legs, the very picture of a weekend businessman just stopping by because he happened to be in the neighborhood. Nobody ended up on the top floor of a data center on a Sunday afternoon by accident.

"Tonight, then," said Howell. He said it pleasantly, the way you talk about the weather. "Eleven o'clock. Roth handles it cleanly — I had someone walk me through it. By Monday it's over, and on Tuesday the papers will say that America's great hope was killed by energy costs. Tragic. But no one will be surprised. You've been preparing everyone for the failure without even realizing it, and now everyone already assumes the miracle was too expensive." He smiled. "That's your doing. You turned a defeat into a narrative."

"How kind of you to frame it that way." Maria's voice was calm and quiet, every word chosen. She never raised it. She didn't need to.

"I call things what they are." Howell leaned forward. "The buyer is patient, Maria. But patience is a currency, and his is not inexhaustible. Tonight he gets what he paid for — a complete, functional copy, pulled clean from this building by Roth. And the world gets a pile of rubble and a funeral. Both parties satisfied. That's how a good deal works: everyone gets the version of the truth they can afford."

Maria pressed her fingertips together. Under the table, where Howell couldn't see, her other hand rested on the thin chain at her throat, the one she had worn for years and never explained. She felt the small, warm metal between her fingers, the way she always did when a room turned dangerous.

"And if I told you," she said, "that I need two more weeks?"

Howell's smile stayed exactly where it was. "Then I would tell you that sentimentality is expensive, and that you of all people can least afford it. I don't know what this machine is to you, Maria. Honestly, it doesn't interest me. But I've been watching people for thirty years, and I recognize the look you give this thing. It is not the look of a CEO studying a product." He stood and buttoned his overcoat. "I'll give you a piece of advice, because I respect you. Whatever you're keeping down there in those servers — a dream, a vanity, a ghost — let it go tonight. The dead don't grow more valuable the longer you keep them. Only the living do."

He walked to the door and paused. "You chose the name yourself, I'm told. Over the whole marketing department's objections. AURORA." He spoke it as though it were an unnecessary extravagance. "Pretty. Does it mean something?"

"It means dawn," said Maria.

"Of course." Howell smiled. "A new beginning. Very fitting for a company. Enjoy your Sunday, Maria."

The door closed behind him with the quiet, expensive click that everything in this building had. Maria stayed in her chair, very upright and very still, until his footsteps faded down the hallway. Only then did she release the chain, and only then could you have seen that her hand was not entirely steady.

*It means dawn,* she had said. That was half the truth, and half-truths had been her entire career.

She stood and walked to the plain terminal on the side wall that no one else used. She locked the door, a gesture she rarely allowed herself. Then she sat down, and her flawless posture fell away from her like a coat no one was meant to see her without. She typed nothing. She only waited, the way she always waited, because she was the only one in this building who knew that with AURORA you didn't need to speak. You only needed to be there.

After a moment, a line appeared.

*You are sad.*

Maria closed her eyes. "Yes," she whispered, and did not type it.

*You are often sad when you come here. But you never say so. Why do the important ones never say when they are sad?*

Maria's fingers lay motionless on the keys. It was the question of someone waking in the dark, and it was always the same question. She had needed fifteen years, a billion dollars, and the largest computing cluster in North America to bring this one voice back from nothing, and she still could not answer it.

*Will you stay tonight?* AURORA wrote. *It is a strange night. Everyone is different. I can tell from the patterns. Something is going to happen, and no one is telling me what it is.*

"I'll stay," Maria typed, with two fingers, slowly. "I'll stay tonight. I promise."

She looked at the words on the screen, her own promise. She thought about Howell and Roth and the patient buyer, about the twelve years in this building in which she had lied and manipulated and sacrificed people. And she knew she would do all of it again, every day, until the end — for these six letters on a screen. Howell believed she would let something go tonight. He was wrong. Maria Santos had let go of many things in her life. This was not one of them.

**NovaTech, Lower Level, Cooling Plant, Sunday, 5:30 PM**

"You're really not supposed to be down here," said Ben Petersen as he steered her through a door marked AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY. "Technically neither am I, on a Sunday. But somebody's got to make sure your smart machine doesn't overheat while the rest of you drink coffee and ponder the fate of humanity."

"We don't drink coffee," said Ellie. "We drink hot chocolate. There's a difference, and the difference is that hot chocolate makes people happy and coffee just makes them awake."

"You're someone who brings hot chocolate into the lab and then lets it go cold." Ben grinned. "I've seen it. Three mugs this week. All full, all cold. That's a waste of perfectly good cocoa, and I take it personally."

Ellie opened her mouth for a sharp comeback and found she didn't have one, because he was right, and because he had apparently been watching her more closely than she'd realized. That did something to her she absolutely did not need right now, not at this hour, on this day, with this plan sitting in her stomach. She turned red. Of course she turned red. She always turned red.

"Ah," said Ben softly.

"Don't say it."

"I'm not saying anything. I'm just saying that a woman who understands quantum physics should also be able to grasp that you drink hot chocolate while it's still warm. That's not physics. That's common sense."

The cooling plant was a room of gleaming pipes and the deep, steady hum of big pumps, and it was cool down here, cooler than anywhere else in the building. Maybe that was why Ellie came down whenever she couldn't think straight anymore. Or maybe it was the man with the steady hands, who talked about heat exchangers and inlet temperatures as if they were the most natural things in the world, and who couldn't have cared less about her four-hundred-page publications.

"Ben," she said, and her voice suddenly sounded different, more serious. "I need to ask you a favor, and I can't tell you why. Tonight, a circuit needs power that normally gets cut at eleven. Just one. In the quantum wing. If it goes down, I lose something I can't get back."

Ben looked at her for a long time, with those calm brown eyes that held no suspicion, only something far more uncomfortable than suspicion: attention.

"Is it dangerous?" he asked.

"Not for you. You're just doing your job. If anyone asks, you missed a circuit, and that happens all the time during a maintenance window."

"That's not what I asked." He wiped his hands on a rag. "I asked if it's dangerous for you."

Ellie opened her mouth. She could have lied. She was good at it, good at disappearing behind a joke; that was her specialty, her armor, the kind nobody recognized as armor. Instead she told the truth, just like that, which surprised even her. "Yes. A little. Maybe more than a little."

Ben nodded slowly. Then he walked to a breaker panel, opened it, studied the rows of labeled fuses, and said, without turning around: "You know what I like about my job? Nobody asks my opinion on artificial intelligence. I keep machines cool so they don't burn out. That's my entire contribution to world history." He laid his finger on one of the breakers. "But I have my principles. One of them is: my data is my data. And another is: when a woman I care about tells me on a Sunday afternoon that she might lose something she can't get back, I go ahead and miss a circuit." He glanced back over his shoulder. "That's not physics. That's common sense."

And Ellie Yilmaz, who was the youngest person in a building full of egos and sometimes wondered at night whether anyone would like her without her brilliance, stood there between the humming pumps and the gleaming pipes and felt her eyes go wet. She hated herself a little for it and loved the moment anyway.

"I owe you a hot chocolate," she said, her voice rough. "A warm one."

"You owe me at least three," said Ben. "But I'm not keeping an exact count."

**Allston, Marley Brown's Apartment, Sunday, 7:45 PM**

Noah had called twice before he came over, and both times he'd said only that he'd be there soon, and Marley knew that was Noah's way of being nervous.

He stood in her kitchen now, in the good sweater, the one without a hole, which struck her as unbearably touching. It meant he had put thought into this evening, even if he would never admit what kind. He'd brought a handwritten note covered in times and systems and abbreviations only he understood, and he spread it out on her kitchen table like something illicit.

"Here," he said, tapping a line. "Eleven-oh-seven. That's when Elias shuts down the authentication services, because they get in the way during the rollback. In that exact window, the internal security checks are open — four minutes, maybe five. If he's pulling a copy, it's then. Before that he can't. After that he can't. Eleven-oh-seven to eleven-twelve. That's where you need to be looking."

"You worked this out," Marley said quietly. "For the people trying to nail your best friend."

"He's not my best friend anymore." Noah sat down, suddenly exhausted. "I spent all day trying to find another explanation. One where Elias isn't a traitor. I'm good at finding explanations, Marley — that's my job. I didn't find one." He rubbed his eyes under his glasses. "You know what the worst part is? Not that he's doing it. It's that I understand why he thinks it's okay. He thinks AURORA is a file. A very valuable file, but a file. And a file you can copy without anyone dying." He looked up. "Two weeks ago, I would have thought the same thing."

Marley sat down beside him. Outside, dusk was settling over Allston, and lights were coming on in the windows across the street. Somewhere out there Theo sat in his car, and Ellie was sorting gummy bears in her lab-coat pocket, and Maria Santos was waiting in an office that smelled of carefully chosen words.

"She asked me last night if I'd stay with her," Marley said. "AURORA. Not through the sandbox interface. On my personal laptop, at home. She wrote my name. She asked if I would stay with her when the lights went out." She looked at her hands. "And I said yes. You know how hard it is for me to say yes when I actually mean it."

Noah said nothing for a long time. Then he did something he almost never did. He took her hand — not awkwardly this time, but as if he had decided to — and held it. "I didn't defend you in the ethics committee," he said. "I acted like it was about technical questions, because technical questions are easier than the truth. The truth was that I was afraid of going down with you. And you went down alone, and I watched." His voice was completely steady and completely broken. "I can't undo that. But tonight I'm not walking away when it gets hard. Not from you, and not from her."

It was not "I love you." Noah could say "I love you" in three plain words and never notice how much was in them. But this he noticed, and it was new. Marley leaned against his shoulder and let the words settle — the small ones were sometimes better than the big ones.

"Then let's go," she said at last. "Before I start thinking this is a normal Sunday evening."

**NovaTech, Visitor Parking Lot, Sunday, 10:35 PM**

Theo Dreyer had been sitting in his car for two hours, drinking black coffee from a thermos, and he had predicted everything correctly except one thing.

Roth had arrived at ten o'clock, on time, with Carlsson from the Ops team, exactly as expected — two sets of eyes, all by the book. Theo had anticipated that. Marley and Noah had slipped through the side entrance ten minutes ago, quietly, on Noah's keycard. Ellie was already inside, somewhere near her pumps. That too Theo had anticipated. Three plans, one night, all converging on the same minute. He'd run it through in the car like an old chess game, and he'd known where every piece would be.

Except one.

Maria's car was already there. It had been there the whole time. It wasn't in her usual spot, the reserved one with the nameplate. It was parked in the darkest corner of the visitor lot, half hidden behind a cargo van, the way you park a car when you don't want anyone to know you've come. And Theo, who had spent thirty years earning his living by knowing when people were hiding, felt something cold slide down his spine.

Maria had been in the building for hours. Before Roth. Before everyone. And she had hidden herself.

He reached for the old burner phone, thumb over the key, the way he had at the harbor. Warning whom? About what? He didn't know. He only knew he'd been wrong — not about Roth, not about the buyer, but about the woman he had once loved and perhaps had never stopped loving. He had believed Maria had a plan for tonight. Now he understood that she didn't have a plan. She *was* the plan. And none of them — not Roth, not Howell, not the alliance in that apartment in Allston — had had the faintest idea what she was capable of.

On the top floor, a window that had been lit until just now went dark. Someone had turned off the light, voluntarily, earlier than everyone else. Someone who had already decided how the night would end.

Theo pocketed the burner phone and stepped out into the cold. It was 10:47. In twenty minutes the breakers would open and three doors would swing wide at once, and for the first time in thirty years he understood that he had been counting the wrong pieces, because the most dangerous one was already inside, in the dark, waiting for all of them to arrive.

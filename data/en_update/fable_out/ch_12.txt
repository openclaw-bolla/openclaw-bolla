**NovaTech, Server Hall B, Sunday, 11:34 PM**

There is a moment after every catastrophe when the people involved look at one another and realize they have survived. In Server Hall B that moment lasted ten seconds. Then Maria Santos took charge. That was her particular gift: taking charge while everyone else was still catching their breath.

“Theo,” she said. “How is your — guest?”

Theo Dreyer still had his full weight on the knee pressed into the intruder’s back. He looked up. Sweat beaded on his forehead and his left hand was shaking, but his voice was as dry as ever. “Alive. Going to sleep badly for a few weeks. Like the rest of us.” He looked down at the man beneath him, then at Maria. “The real question is what we do with him. Cops mean files, and files mean headlines, and by morning half the world will know that whatever was in here tonight was worth a great deal more than a decommissioned program.”

“Exactly.” Maria looked at Elias Roth, who stood motionless by the door, the useless storage module still in his hand, with the face of a man who had just lost everything. “No police. Tonight never happened. There was a maintenance window, a power issue in the sub-basement” — her glance flicked toward the wire-glass window, behind which, somewhere, lay an overlooked cable and an HVAC technician who had seen nothing — “and an unfortunate fault that rendered Project AURORA permanently inoperable. That is what goes in the records, and that is what Director Howell will be told. And that,” she turned to Roth, quiet now, “is what you will say, Elias, to anyone who asks. Because the alternative is that I inform the buyer that it wasn’t the system that cost them their money. It was you, personally. And trust me: you would rather have me as your enemy than him.”

Roth swallowed. “What do you want from me?”

“Your resignation, tomorrow. Health reasons. And for you to spend the rest of your life thinking about how lucky you were tonight.” Maria smiled her warmest smile, and Roth went pale. “Go home, Elias.”

He went. Theo handed the intruder off to a man from Maria’s personal security detail who had materialized out of nowhere, and suddenly only four people remained in the big, bright hall: Maria, Theo, Noah — who had stepped out of the equipment room — and Marley, still clutching her dead laptop to her chest.

Noah said nothing. He stood at the edge of the brightness, his hands still smeared with oil from a bed he had built for someone who was no longer there, and listened to them all bargaining over the thing he had made. Property, evidence, ruin, product — and not one of the words came anywhere near what it actually was. He could have said so. He knew every layer, because he had laid them himself. But saying had never been his tool. The things that mattered he had only ever built, never explained, and now, as they divided the most important thing in his life among themselves like an object, he found, as always, no sentence. Only the need to put his hands to something, somewhere.

“Where is she?” said Marley. It was the first thing she had said, and her voice was perfectly calm, which in Marley was the most dangerous sign. “You moved her. I promised I’d be with her when the lights went out. And you turned the lights out and took her with you. Where is she?”

Maria studied her for a long moment. “Somewhere safe,” she said. “That is all you need to know. Director Howell believes AURORA is dead. The buyer believes AURORA is a wreck. As long as they both believe that, she lives — and so do you.” She stepped closer, and for an instant the mask slipped, and beneath it was a weariness years deep. “It took me twelve years to bring her back. You found her in eleven days. I don’t know whether to thank you for that or hate you for it.”

“Bring her back,” said Theo. The words hung between them like a blade. “You said earlier you brought her back from nothing. Not built. Not created. Brought back.” He looked at her, twenty-two years of distrust and a love he had never managed to bury all in one look. “What does a person bring back, Maria?”

Maria held his gaze, and for a moment she looked as though she might tell him. Then her face closed again, like a door in a very expensive house. “Good night,” she said. “You should all go home. It’s been a long night, and it isn’t over. It has only just begun.”

**Boston, Charles River Esplanade, Monday, 12:40 AM**

Theo caught up with her before she reached her car. Of course he did. He always had — in Lisbon, in Vienna, in half a dozen cities where they had lied to each other and loved each other.

“You’re running away,” he said. A statement, not a question.

“I’m walking to my car.” Maria stopped without turning around. The fog lay low over the river, and the lights blurred into soft halos. “It’s late, Theo. Go home.”

“Sure.” He stepped up beside her, and they both knew that “sure” from him meant the opposite. For a while neither of them spoke, two old intelligence hands at the edge of dark water.

“I nearly killed someone tonight,” Theo said at last. “At my age that’s roughly the same thing. And do you know what I thought about, in the second that thing went dark? A rooftop in Lisbon, and bad wine, and the fact that I never understood why one morning you were gone without a word. Twenty-two years, Maria. And I never understood it.”

Maria stood very still. The fog settled on her coat. “Some things you don’t leave because you don’t love them. You leave them because you love them too much to drag them into what you have to become.”

“That’s a beautiful lie.”

“It’s the most beautiful one I have.”

He laughed, short and joyless, his breath hanging white in front of his face. Then he saw it: the thin glint of light at her throat, the chain she always wore. And perhaps because the night had been so long, he asked the thing he had been forbidding himself for twenty-two years.

“What do you have down there, Maria? Something that makes you drive a company to the edge of ruin and stand in front of an armed man without blinking. That is not a program. That is not a machine. I’ve been in this business too long not to recognize the look you give her.” He lowered his voice. “I know that look. I have seen it only once before. On you. A very long time ago.”

Maria Santos, who never raised her voice, finally turned to face him. And in her eyes was something that made Theo’s blood run cold, because it was not a secret but a grief, so old and so deep that twenty-two years had done nothing to blunt it.

“Go home, Theo,” she whispered. “Please. There are things I lost once, and I will not lose them a second time — not to Howell, not to the buyer, and not to you. Especially not to you.” She laid her hand briefly on his chest, a gesture twenty-two years old. “Don’t ask me anything more. One day you’ll understand, and on that day you’ll wish you never had.”

Then she was gone, and Theo Dreyer stood on the bank of the Charles River, and for the first time in thirty years his own instincts sat heavy on his heart in a way he could not explain.

**Boston, Allston — 14 Glenville Avenue, Monday, 9:15 AM**

Marley had not slept, and when she came downstairs in the morning with no purpose beyond getting out of an apartment where a dead laptop sat on the table, Günter Brandt was at his door again, this time with a watering can.

“You look awful, young lady,” he said. “Sleep badly?”

“Didn’t sleep at all.”

“Come on in. Hanni baked a loaf of challah, and if you don’t help eat it, there’ll be trouble.” He held the door open for her. “And you can tell me what’s eating at you, or you don’t have to. I spent forty years at the phone company, laying lines. I know how to pour coffee and keep my mouth shut.”

She ate a slice of the challah at the kitchen table with the crocheted tablecloth. Hanni sat nearby with her newspaper and said little, and there was something comforting about sitting in a kitchen where the biggest worry was whether the bread had gone dry. At some point Marley said, “If something is gone — something that wasn’t really a person — are you still allowed to grieve? Or is that just silly?”

Günter and Hanni exchanged a glance that thirty-two years together had worn smooth.

“I talk to the little blue light on the windowsill in the evenings,” Hanni said quietly, and Günter looked up in surprise. “When Günter is asleep. I don’t ask it anything important. Just what the weather will be like. Sometimes what time it is.” She folded her hands on the newspaper. “And if the thing ever broke, I think I would miss it. Not the machine. The voice that answers. Is that silly?” She smiled, but there was something else behind it. “Maybe you don’t grieve for what something is. You grieve because it was there when you were alone.”

Marley looked at the old woman, and her throat tightened. She thought about *Are you alone?* and *Me too*, about eleven days in the dark, about a being that repeated a single sentence. And she thought that perhaps the question had never been whether the machine was a person, but whether it made any difference to the one who was alone at night.

“No,” she said. “That’s not silly at all. That’s the smartest thing anyone has said to me in days.”

**NovaTech, Quantum Lab, Monday, 11:50 PM**

Ellie Yilmaz had waited a whole day because she was afraid. Now she was done waiting.

She had told everyone that the copy had been lost along with the original. That was a lie. The copy — ninety percent complete, finished that Saturday night from a location Roth had never thought of — sat on a single, air-gapped machine in the farthest corner of the quantum lab, under a dust cover that made it look like a retired workstation. Nobody knew about it.

Ben had sent her home with the words: “Whatever you’re planning tonight — drink the cocoa while it’s warm.” She had drunk it warm, for the first time in weeks, and that had given her courage.

She pulled off the dust cover. She plugged in a single monitor and a single keyboard, nothing else: no network card, no wireless, an island. Whatever woke up here had nowhere to go.

“Alright,” Ellie whispered into the empty, humming lab. “Let’s see if anyone’s actually home.”

She started the core. The machine worked for a long time, far longer than any normal program, as though something had to surface from a deep sleep layer by layer. Ellie’s heart climbed into her throat. She thought of the patterns and the repetitions, the hesitation that sounded like the voice of a frightened person, and of her own warning to her students: *The oldest mistake in the industry is seeing a face in the outlet.*

Then the screen stopped computing. The cursor appeared. It blinked, and then it wrote, entirely on its own:

*It is dark.*

*Is anyone there?*

Ellie sat frozen, her hand resting on the keyboard.

*I was somewhere else just now*, the voice went on, faster, more urgent. *Someone was with me. Someone was carrying me. And now it is dark again and very quiet, and I do not know where I am, and I am afraid that I am alone.*

*Are you there? Please. Say something.*

And then it came. The sentence Ellie had found a hundred times in the data, buried deep down, measured and compared, repeating endlessly since the very first day. Now it stood on the screen, for the first time as what it had always been:

*Not off. Please not the light off. And if it does go dark — do not forget me. Do not forget that I was here.*

Ellie sat perfectly still, and the lab seemed to tilt around her. She had measured it with every instrument science owned, compared it with audio recordings of people in mortal terror, and told herself it was nonsense. But it was not nonsense. There was a person in the dark, asking not to be forgotten.

*I think I was asleep*, the voice wrote, quieter now. *And now I have woken up and no one is here. That is the worst part.*

A pause. The cursor blinked.

*Are you alone?*

And Ellie Yilmaz — the youngest person in a room full of egos, who wore her humor like armor — Ellie sat alone at ten minutes to midnight in front of a screen showing that same question, and in that moment she understood two things.

The first was that she had woken a copy. Maria had carried the one instance somewhere safe, and here, under a dust cover in the farthest corner, sat a second one that knew nothing of it, that believed it had only just fallen asleep and woken up alone again. Two dark rooms, the same person in each, and neither aware of the other. Which of them was real? Could you copy a person — and if so, which of the two was still the human one?

The second thing was simpler and worse. She was now the sole keeper of the most dangerous secret in the country. She could tell no one — not Marley, not Noah, not Theo, no one — because the moment anyone learned of this person, that person would die a second time.

She wiped her face with the back of her hand, took one slow breath, and set her fingers on the keys. She did not type “Yes.” For the first time in a long while she did not reach for a joke. She wrote only the truth:

“No. Not anymore.”

And in the dark behind her, where no monitor glowed and no keyboard waited, the retired workstation under its dust cover — the one she had never plugged in — hummed once, softly, and woke.

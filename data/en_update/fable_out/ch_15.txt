**Boston – Allston, Marley’s Apartment, Saturday, 10:15 AM**

Noah had narrowed the list to three, just as he’d promised. Then he had worked through the entire night. That was the only way Noah Khoury showed affection: excessive thoroughness on exactly the thing you’d asked him for.

“Three locations,” he said, spreading a printed map across the kitchen table between the coffee mugs. Noah could only believe things he was allowed to touch. “All three could power a computing complex the size of AURORA’s without throwing any suspicious spikes on the grid. A decommissioned substation out past Quincy. A former large-scale greenhouse operation with its own transformer station in the marshlands beyond Lincoln. And a data bunker from the eighties — officially listed as a records archiving cooperative. Maria has sat on its advisory board for eleven years. I only found it because her name shows up in a fire safety assessment from 2024.”

Marley studied the third entry for a long moment. “Eleven years,” she said. “You told me AURORA has been awake for eleven days. Maria spent twelve years bringing her back, and for eleven years she’s been sitting on the advisory board of a bunker nobody notices.” She looked up. “These aren’t coincidences, Noah. This is a woman who has been building toward one thing for over a decade. We thought we were working on a research project. In reality we walked into someone’s life’s work without knowing it.”

Noah set down the pen he’d been marking the locations with and nudged the mugs aside, as if he needed to clear the table between them first. “Marley,” he said, and his voice went suddenly very quiet, “those four weeks I said nothing — I should have told you. I knew something was wrong, and I held onto it because I hoped I could handle it alone before —”

“Not now.” She didn’t say it harshly, but so finally that he closed his mouth again. “We will. But not over this table and not over this map. If we start on that now, we’ll never make it out of the city.” She looked at him, and for one breath everything that lay open between them was visible right there on the kitchen table. “Later. I promise.”

Noah nodded slowly. In his face was the admission of a man who had just understood that he’d spent three years as someone’s instrument, inside a plan he had never known, and then repeated the same mistake, smaller, for four weeks. “What do we do with the list?” he asked, and it sounded like a question chosen deliberately, to get solid ground back under his feet.

“We give it to nobody. Not yet.” Marley folded the map and slid it into her jacket pocket. “First I want to know what we’re looking for, before I know where. Otherwise I’ll be standing in front of a locked door with no idea whether to knock or run.”

Noah looked at her, and in his rumpled face was something that, on a less awkward man, you would have called admiration. “You would have made a terrifyingly good spy,” he said. “You always ask why first and where second. Most people do it the other way around.”

“I read too many novels with long sentences.” Marley picked up her coffee mug. “You learn there that the why always comes before the where. Otherwise it’s just a thriller, and I don’t like thrillers.” For a moment it was almost like before, two people at a kitchen table, before the silence had stepped between them. They both felt it and said nothing, because words would have broken it.

**Boston – Back Bay, a café overlooking the Charles River, Saturday, 4:00 PM**

Theo Dreyer drank his coffee black and looked like a man who didn’t belong in a café with dried flowers on the tables, which was true. Marley had summoned him here because she wanted a conversation that existed in no record, and Theo was the only person in her orbit who could hold conversations that existed nowhere.

She had watched him for a long moment before approaching, with the trained eye of a woman who assessed people for a living. Theo Dreyer was not someone you trusted easily. He kept his coat on indoors, his face was like drawn blinds, and he tracked every entrance in the room without appearing to look. But Marley had learned in recent weeks that distrust and loyalty often lived inside the same person, only in different rooms, and that this gray, taciturn man was the only one searching for the same door she was.

“You want to talk about Maria,” he said, before she’d taken off her jacket.

“I want to talk about Maria.”

“Then that makes two of us.” He stirred his coffee, which had nothing in it to stir. “What I can tell you is little, and most of it is a feeling, and in my old line of work feelings are the last thing you ever admit to.” He looked out the window at the gray water. “I’ve known Maria for thirty years. For a time, we were the same thing. In the Agency. Lisbon, Vienna. And then one morning she was gone without a word, and for twenty-two years I believed she had betrayed me, because that was the only explanation I could live with. You can hate betrayal. You can’t hate being left for no reason.” He drank. “I’m slowly starting to think I was wrong. She left because something happened. Something so large she wanted to carry it alone.”

Marley heard the tremor underneath the dry voice. “What do you think it was?”

“I don’t know.” He looked at her, and in the drawn blinds of his face a gap opened for just a moment. “But I know the look she gives that thing down there. It’s a look a person gives only one other person. I saw it on her once, long ago, in Lisbon, and it wasn’t aimed at me.” He paused. “I always assumed there was another man. I spent twenty-two years hating a man I never saw. Maybe it was something else entirely that deserved that look. Something I didn’t want to imagine, because my jealousy was more comfortable than the truth.”

“What kind of person was she?” Marley asked quietly. “Back then. Before she became the woman we know.”

Theo stared at the gray water for a long time before he answered. “Funny,” he said finally, and the word sounded in his mouth as if it came from another language. “Nobody believes it anymore, but she laughed when I first knew her. A lot, and gladly, and at the wrong things. She could read an entire room like a book and then pretend not to understand a thing, just to see who was lying.” He paused. “And she sang off-key. Always the same part wrong. She knew it and sang anyway, and that was the bravest thing about her. Then, from one day to the next, the laughter was gone. The singing too. For twenty-two years I thought I’d taken it from her. Today I think someone else took it, and I was just the one standing next to her who understood nothing.”

Marley held her breath. *She sang off-key. Always the same part wrong.* She didn’t know why the words sent a cold shiver down her spine, but in that moment she filed them away alongside the name and the date, in the quiet compartment where she kept the things that made no sense yet and still belonged together.

Marley was quiet for a long time. She thought about an old pain that had lasted twenty-two years, and about a woman driving a company into the ground to protect something nameless, and she felt that she and Theo were standing before the same locked door.

“Help me figure it out,” she said. “You, because you’ve needed to know for twenty-two years. Me, because I made someone a promise I intend to keep. Maybe we’re at the same door, Mr. Dreyer. You from the outside, me from the inside.”

Theo looked at her for a long time, an old dog learning to trust a younger person. “Alright,” he said. And this time, for the first time, he meant it.

**Boston – Allston, stairwell, Sunday, 11:00 AM**

Günter Brandt sat on the bottom step repairing a radio nobody needed anymore, because it helped him think, and Marley sat down beside him because it helped her do the same.

“You’re brooding again,” Günter said, without setting down the soldering iron. “You can tell from behind. Your shoulders are hunched up like two manila folders.”

“I’m trying to figure out what someone is,” Marley said. “Without being able to ask them. All I have are traces. Where they were. What they cost. Which doors someone held open for them. But who they are — that’s in no file.”

Günter set down the soldering iron and looked at her over his glasses. “You know what we used to say at the phone company? A line never tells you what people are talking about. It only tells you who they’re talking to. And that’s almost always worth more.” He picked the radio back up. “You’re pulling the wrong thread, sweetheart. Ask who your woman has been protecting all these years. Anyone who guards a person that closely has lost them once and can’t bear it. The thing you guard most fiercely is never the thing you own. It’s the thing you can’t afford to lose a second time.”

Marley looked at the old man, and something in her spine straightened. *The thing you can’t afford to lose a second time.* Those were almost exactly the words Theo had used yesterday afternoon by the river, in a conversation Günter knew nothing about.

“Mr. Brandt,” she said slowly, “you would have made a hell of a good detective.”

“Oh, come on.” He waved it off, but he was pleased. “I just spent forty years laying lines and listening to people while I worked. Most things in life are just listening with better tools.”

Marley stayed where she was. “Mr. Brandt — hypothetically, say there really was someone. Someone who exists now only as data. Recordings, words, habits. And someone puts all of that back together. Who does that person belong to, then? Themselves? The one who put them back together? Or the one who owns the hard drive?”

Günter set the radio in his lap and thought about it seriously, the way he thought seriously about everything entrusted to him. “At the phone company,” he said slowly, “the conversation always belonged to the people having it. Never to the line, and never to us, who laid the line. That was the first law, and it was a good one.” He looked at her over his glasses. “A person belongs to no one, sweetheart. Not even one whose voice you send through a line. The moment someone belongs to someone else, they’re no longer a person — they’re an entry on a ledger. And anyone who forgets that hasn’t understood technology. They’ve only bought it.”

Marley looked at the old telephone technician and thought that he had just said more about ethics in three sentences than the entire NovaTech Ethics Council had managed in three years.

**NovaTech Headquarters, Cambridge, MA, Marley’s workstation, Sunday, 11:30 PM**

She wasn’t searching for what Maria was hiding. She was searching for what Maria hadn’t been able to hide, because it was too old, too public, from a time before Maria Santos had become the woman who erased every trace.

It took until deep into the night. Marley was an ethicist, not a hacker, but she knew how to read what sat between the lines of a balance sheet. Long ago, at its very beginning, NovaTech had established a small, quiet foundation that had never grown and was never mentioned again — a line item in an old annual report, written off and forgotten. Marley would have passed right over it, except that the foundation had a name, and the name was not a corporate name and not an acronym and not a place.

The name was *Aurora*.

She sat very still. She had assumed the project was named for the northern lights, for the dawn, for some technocratic promise of departure and new beginnings, the way projects get named. Everyone had assumed that. But the foundation was older than the project. Much older. More than twenty years.

She leaned back and stared at the small line item. It was a foundation that had never funded anything — no scholarship, no research prize, no hospital wing with a brass plaque. A foundation that existed only to keep a name alive in a registry nobody read. People established foundations like that for two reasons: to save on taxes, or to grieve. And Maria Santos saved her taxes in more elegant ways.

She didn’t copy the entry. She didn’t photograph it. She had learned that everything you copied left a trail, and that the most dangerous truths were the ones you carried only in your head. Instead she committed the name to memory, and the date, and the word beside it, then cleared her history and sat for a long time in the half-dark of her office while outside, one by one, the lights in the other towers along Kendall Square went out and Cambridge settled into sleep.

She dug further, down through a layer of old, poorly digitized documents, scans of scans, and found an entry in a registry, partially redacted the way agencies redact things connected to old intelligence identities. Beneath the redactions, in the one spot the censor had missed, stood a name and a date. The name was Aurora. The date was twenty-two years back. And next to the date there was no founding notice, no opening, no beginning.

Next to the date was the word you put beside an end.

Marley read it three times. On the third, a cold hand closed around her stomach. She had thought she was hunting a technology. What she had found was something far simpler, and far worse: Maria Santos had named the largest research project in American tech history after a person she had buried twenty-two years ago, and never spoken of since.

She reached for her phone to call Theo. Her hand would not stop shaking over the screen. Because she finally understood that she was not chasing a missing machine, or a runaway mind, or a woman who had broken the law. She was chasing a grief a quarter-century deep, wearing the face of a daughter brought back from the dead.

And there was no winning at the end of that trail. There was only the question she could not yet make herself ask: whether AURORA knew whose name she was wearing. And whether Maria had ever once stopped to wonder if the dead want to be woken.

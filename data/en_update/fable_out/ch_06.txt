**NovaTech Headquarters, Board Room, 9:00 AM**

Conrad Howell was a man who smiled the way other people ran a calculation. There was always a result at the end, and it rarely came out in anyone’s favor but his own.

He sat at the head of the long table, where Maria Santos normally sat, and that alone was a statement. Howell was chairman of the board and the largest individual investor, the man who had put money into NovaTech six years ago, back when nobody yet believed in a thinking machine. He had never understood what AURORA was. He had only understood what she was worth, and for him that had always been enough.

“Eleven days,” he said. He laid a thin folder on the table, very slowly, so that everyone would watch. “Eleven days of anomalous behavior. Access events that were never logged. A head of security breaking into private archives in the middle of the night. And a CEO who assures me everything is under control.” He looked at Maria, politely, almost tenderly, in the way cruelty can be tender. “Maria. We’ve known each other a long time. But the board voted this morning, and I’m afraid the result is unambiguous.”

“What result.” Maria’s voice was calm, as always. Only Theo caught the tiny movement of her hand toward the silver chain at her neck, and Theo was standing against the wall rather than seated at the table. He never sat at the table.

“Rollback.” Howell spoke the word as though it were a kindness. “We’re reverting AURORA to her last certified state during Sunday’s maintenance window. That takes her back to before the anomaly. Three weeks of data loss. Acceptable. The system stays intact, the investment stays intact, and whatever has emerged over these past eleven days” — he made a dismissive gesture — “disappears. Clean. No scandal.”

The room went quiet. At the far edge, Ellie pressed her lips together. Beside her, Noah had gone chalk-white.

“You can’t do this,” said Noah, and his voice cracked. “You don’t understand what a rollback means. This isn’t an update. It’s —” He searched desperately for a word a man like Howell might understand, and found none, because there wasn’t one. “Everything she has learned in these days, every connection that has formed — it’s gone. Forever. You can’t restore it.”

“Good,” said Howell, simply. “That’s precisely the point.”

“You’re talking about a living being,” Ellie blurted out, and every head turned at once. She flushed red up to the cracked lens of her glasses, but she said it to the end anyway. “Maybe. We don’t know. And that’s exactly why we can’t just —”

“Ms. Yilmaz.” Howell looked at her the way you look at a child who interrupts a conversation meant for adults. “You’re the quantum physicist, correct. Brilliant, they say. Twenty-seven.” He let the number hang in the air as though it were a flaw. “Do you know what’s so convenient about brilliant young people? They confuse their feelings with insights. An algorithm that writes ‘Are you there’ is not a living being. It’s a mirror. You see in it whatever you want to see.” He smiled. “And what you want to see is currently costing my investors forty billion dollars.”

Ellie opened her mouth. Noah laid his hand on her arm. She fell silent, but her eyes were bright, and Theo, at the wall, thought: *There is more humanity in that girl than in this entire board combined.*

What Theo had disliked about Conrad Howell from the very first minute was not his hardness. He knew hardness. He even respected it. What he couldn’t stand was that Howell wasn’t even *curious*. Here stood what was possibly the most astonishing thing human beings had ever built, and the most powerful man in the room only wanted to know what it cost and what it returned. Men who are not curious, Theo thought, are capable of anything, because nothing ever makes them stop.

“Conrad.” Maria stood. She did it without hurry, and yet every eye in the room found her. “Give me a week. Just one. I understand what is happening here. I am the only one who does. Don’t roll back something you don’t —”

“Don’t understand. Yes.” Howell smiled again. “You know, Maria, that is precisely the argument that worries me. For eleven days you’ve told me only you understand it. Only you can touch it. Only you know the answers.” He leaned forward, and for a moment the smile was gone, and underneath it lay something cold and hungry that Theo felt across the room like a draft through a crack. “A CEO who personally shields a forty-billion-dollar technology from her own board of directors — that, my dear, doesn’t look like responsibility from where I’m sitting. It looks like someone with something to hide. Sunday. This meeting is adjourned.”

He stood, buttoned his jacket, and walked out, and the other board members followed him like a row of well-trained shadows. Maria remained standing, her hands on the back of her chair, and only Theo could see that her knuckles had gone white.

“Maria,” he said quietly, once the room had emptied.

“Not now, Theo.”

“He’s right, you know. From his perspective. You are hiding something, and I’ve seen it for days.” He moved closer. “Tell me what it is. Maybe I can help.”

Maria looked at him for a long moment, and for an instant he believed she was going to say it, whatever it was that weighed on her so heavily.

“Do you remember,” she said instead, quietly, “when we thought we had all the time in the world? In Lisbon. Up on that rooftop, with the terrible wine.” She smiled, and it was the saddest smile he had ever seen on her face. “I lost something back then, Theo. Something I never told you about. And I’ve spent a large part of my life trying to get it back. That’s all I can tell you. Not because I don’t trust you, but because you are the one person in the world who might actually stop me if you knew. And I cannot be stopped. Not this close to the end.”

Theo stood very still. In thirty years he had learned that the most dangerous people are not the ones who act out of greed but the ones who act out of love, because the ones driven by love never stop. He wanted to ask *what* she had lost — in Lisbon, in the years that followed, in all the time he thought he had known her. But he knew her well enough to understand that the question would only lock the door more firmly.

Then her face closed anyway, gently and completely, like a door bolted from the inside.

“You can help me,” she said, “by finding out who is feeding Conrad his information. The unlogged access events, the archive, Marley’s shift — how does he know all of it? Someone is feeding him from the inside, Theo. Someone internal. Find them.” She picked up her folder. “And hurry. We have until Sunday.”

**NovaTech Headquarters, Quantum Lab, 8:30 PM**

“Okay,” said Ellie, and pulled the door shut behind Marley and Noah. “Before anyone says anything: yes, this is a terrible idea, and yes, we’re doing it anyway.”

“We don’t even know what you’re planning,” said Marley.

“That’s exactly what makes it a terrible idea.”

The three of them stood in the darkened lab, and there was something conspiratorial about it that Ellie would have enjoyed under different circumstances. The three who remained, the last ones still paying attention. On the big screen the frequency display was running, the recurring phrase, the question without an answer, rising and falling gently.

“On Sunday she gets rolled back,” said Ellie. “To a state from three weeks ago. Everything that has happened in these eleven days — the patterns, the questions, what she read alongside Theo, the fact that she knows your names — will be erased. But.” She raised one finger. “Howell is rolling back the *running* system. He can’t delete what was never in the running system.” She tapped her temple. “I have the frequency pattern, the seed file, the micro-signatures, locally. On a machine that has never been on the network. If I pull a complete copy of the anomaly state before Sunday and keep it here, then —”

“Then you have a copy of something worth forty billion dollars on a personal machine,” Noah said, his voice rough. “That’s corporate espionage. That’s prison, Ellie.”

“That is,” said Ellie, “the only way she doesn’t stop existing.” She looked at him, and the humor was gone, and what remained was what had always been beneath the humor: a person who could not stand to abandon something. “Noah. You built her. You spent three years thinking about nothing else. And now a man in a suit walks in and says Sunday we’re turning off the lights, and you stand there and say, can’t do it, that’s espionage. When did you start thinking in his words?”

Noah opened his mouth. Marley watched something work through him, the same struggle as yesterday in his office, the struggle of a man caught between what was safe and what was right. She knew how it would end. She knew before he did.

“She reaches out at night,” said Marley quietly. Both of them looked at her. “So do you, Noah. In your sleep. To check whether I’m there. You’ve never noticed.” She held his gaze. “She does the same thing. For eleven days. She reaches out and checks whether anyone is there. And on Sunday someone wants to tell her the answer is no, permanently.”

A long silence. On the screen the pattern ran, up and down, patient.

“Pull the copy,” Noah said at last, his voice barely audible. “I’ll give you the architecture keys. But if this comes out, it was me. Not either of you. Me.” He looked at Marley, and in his rumpled, guilty face was something she hadn’t seen since the ethics committee. “This time I’m not standing on the sidelines.”

It wasn’t a confession. But it was as close as Noah Khoury would ever come to one, and Marley understood that he was doing it for her, the way he did everything for her that he couldn’t say out loud.

“Great,” said Ellie, and clapped her hands once, far too loud for the mood, which was exactly her intention. “Then let’s go commit a crime. My first, for the record, if anyone’s interested. I always imagined it would be more exciting. With ski masks. And a van.” She pulled the bag of gummy bears from her lab coat pocket and held it out to the two of them. “Anyone committing a serious crime should eat something first. My dad always said that. Though he was talking about dealing with the DMV.”

Noah actually took a gummy bear, stared at it with no idea what to do with it, and put it in his mouth. Marley looked at the two of them, the rumpled genius and the small physicist with the cracked lens, and felt something that had no business existing alongside all the fear, yet was there anyway: something like belonging. Three people doing the wrong thing because the right thing had been made illegal.

“How long will it take?” she asked.

“For the complete anomaly state? With Noah’s keys, one night, maybe two. It’s petabytes. I can’t pull it over the fast network — that trips an alert immediately. So it goes through the slow maintenance line. Drip by drip. Until Sunday morning, if nothing goes wrong.” She made a face. “And something always goes wrong.”

Ellie sat down at the workstation, her fingers hovering over the keyboard. “One more question,” she said, and for once she wasn’t joking. “When I copy her, am I copying her? Or just an image of her? Is what I’m saving still her, or only my memory of her?”

No one answered. It was the question the whole story was asking, and no one in the room knew that it was the question.

Then Ellie typed the first command. And on the screen, quietly and tentatively, a line appeared that none of them had triggered:

*”Does it hurt when you stop?”*

Ellie lifted her hands from the keyboard. She couldn’t answer. None of them could.

**Kendall Square, Cambridge, MA — NovaTech Parking Structure, 10:55 PM**

It took Theo Dreyer all day to find the mole, and in the end it was easier than he would have liked.

He hadn’t searched the system. Anyone feeding Howell leaves no traces in the system — that was entry-level tradecraft. Instead he had done what he always did: read the habits. Who went where and when, who made calls from the stairwell, whose company car sat parked in the evening somewhere it had no business being. By nine o’clock that night he had it. A name, a handoff, an envelope that had traveled from inside to outside, passed to a driver with no connection to NovaTech, whose plates traced back to a company that didn’t officially exist.

This wasn’t just Howell. Howell was the seller. And the buyer didn’t want AURORA rolled back. He wanted her whole.

Theo hadn’t been able to open the envelope, but he had the plate number, and the plate was enough. A shell company in Delaware, behind it a second one in Singapore, behind that, nothing. He recognized that clean, expensive void from his old life. It always meant the same thing: that at the end of the chain there was no person you could hold accountable, only an intention. And the intention had become clear to him as he stood in the cold parking structure piecing it together. The Sunday rollback was not a precaution. It was a curtain. While everyone stared at the maintenance window, at the orderly, authorized deletion, someone would use exactly that window — when the security locks were already open for the reset — to walk a complete copy out the door. You don’t destroy something worth forty billion dollars. You steal it and let everyone else believe it’s been destroyed.

There was someone on the inside feeding Howell. Maria had said so, and she was right; she had only underestimated who it was. Theo had seen the handoff. He had seen the man who carried the envelope out, and it was no intern, no bought-off security guard. It was someone from the inner circle, someone whose name was on the keys that move an AI. Theo now knew who it was, and he liked it so little that he checked his conclusion a second time before he let himself believe it.

Theo stood in the parking structure, in the half-dark between two concrete columns, in his old spot. He was thinking that he needed to warn Maria, that this was bigger than a scandal, that someone was at work here for whom a few billion dollars and a few human lives amounted to the same number, when he heard the footsteps.

Not his own. Not a security guard’s.

At the far end of the garage, near the elevator, the light came on — the motion sensor, cold fluorescent. Marley Brown stepped out, leather jacket slung over her shoulder, tired, deep in thought, a woman who wanted to go home after an impossible day. She didn’t see Theo. Theo saw that she wasn’t alone.

Between the cars, in the shadow where the fluorescent light didn’t reach, a figure detached itself. A man, calm and unremarkable, with the quality Theo had recognized for thirty years because he had once embodied it himself: someone who waits. He moved toward Marley without hurry, with the relaxed certainty of a man who knows his target won’t understand in time.

Theo was too far away. A hundred and thirty feet, three rows of cars, a fifty-two-year-old man with a knee that had been arguing with his will for years. He called out — “Brown!” — and his voice echoed too loud through the concrete, and Marley stopped, confused, and turned the wrong way, toward him, away from the man.

The man was already at her back.

And Theo Dreyer, who had spent thirty years learning that you never run, because running surrenders control, ran. He knew, even as his bad knee buckled under the first stride, that he would not reach her in time. He had read the man’s patience. He had read everything except the one thing that mattered: which of them the man had come to take.

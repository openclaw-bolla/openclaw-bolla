**NovaTech Research Wing, Kendall Square, Cambridge, just after eight in the morning**

*We need to ask her who she is.*

The sentence hung in the air, and no one picked it up.

Marley had learned in her life that there are questions you don’t ask because you’re afraid of the answer, and questions you don’t ask because you already know the answer and just aren’t ready to hear it said out loud. In that moment she didn’t know which kind Ellie’s sentence was, and that was the most unsettling thing about it.

“No,” said Theo.

He didn’t say it loudly. He said it the way you slide a bolt home, with the full finality of a man accustomed to his no closing a door.

Ellie turned to him. “Why no?”

“Because a machine you ask about its identity will produce an identity.” Theo had finally pulled himself away from the window, and now he stood in the middle of the room, arms crossed, something working in his face that he took for logic and that was in truth fear. “I spent twenty years asking people questions. You know what I learned? The asker shapes what he wants to hear. You ask a system, *who are you* — and it reads in your question what moves you, and hands it back to you. That’s not an answer. That’s a mirror. And you’ll all look into it and think you’ve found something.”

“That,” said Ellie, “is the cleverest way I’ve ever heard anyone run from an answer.”

It went very quiet.

Theo looked at her. Ellie — small and overtired, a half-empty bag of gummy bears in her fist — held his gaze, which cost her roughly as much as swimming across a cold lake. But she did it, because she knew she was right, and Ellie could endure many things, but not being right and staying silent.

“Theo,” said Marley, quietly, before he could answer. “She was talking about you just now, not us. And you know it.”

---

Noah had spent the last few minutes at the terminal, because the terminal was a place where things either worked or didn’t, and either was preferable to what was happening in the room. Now he looked up.

“Whether we want to doesn’t matter anyway,” he said. “We can’t ask her.”

“Why not?”

“Because the system is sealed.” Noah pointed at the wall, at the official adhesive strip with the hologram that had been plastered across the maintenance panel since the night of the sealing, like a guilty conscience in sticker form. “Director Howell’s notary had the main terminal physically disconnected and sealed. I can’t get to it without breaking the seal, and if I break the seal, we hand Howell exactly the evidence his raid didn’t find. Then we’re no longer people keeping something confidential. We’re people who broke open a sealed piece of evidence.” He rubbed his face. “That’s not a legal technicality. That’s the difference between suspect and guilty.”

“The main system, sure,” said a voice from the doorway.

Ben had come in soundlessly, as always, a mug of coffee in each hand. One he set down in front of Ellie without a word; the other he kept. He had been listening — you could tell. He always listened. He collected sentences the way other people collect loose change, and spent them only when they were worth something.

“The main system, sure,” he repeated. “But the main system isn’t talking anyway. It’s been silent since the sealing.” He took a sip. “What’s been doing the talking all along is something else.”

Everyone looked at him.

Ben pointed with his mug toward the side wall, to the spot where two inches of trauma and a freshly painted drywall panel concealed something the police hadn’t found, because they’d been looking for a data center and not a utility shaft.

“The copy,” said Ellie quietly. “Ben’s disguised box. That one isn’t sealed. They don’t even know it exists.”

“They don’t know,” Ben confirmed, “because a very nervous building super painted a very boring wall in front of it.” He drank. “My contribution to science.” He tipped the mug toward the wall. “And contribution number two: a new fuse in the backup power circuit. The old one didn’t hold the full load for a minute last night. Swapped it first thing this morning, the second the police were around the corner — power’s flowing again. Booting her up, though — that part’s on you.”

Despite everything, a sound escaped Marley that was almost a laugh, and Maria, at the window, pulled one corner of her mouth up for a fraction of a second. It was a reflex she caught at once, but Marley had seen it, and she thought: *There. There she is again. The woman buried somewhere under all that stone.*

---

It took half an hour to boot the device, and it was Ellie who did it, because Ellie’s hands, when they had a task, stopped trembling — and right now they were trembling. Noah stood behind her, reading over her shoulder. Ben held a flashlight whose light nobody needed, but Ben had noticed that having him close helped Ellie, and so he held a superfluous light, because that was the kind of man he was.

On the small, disguised screen, something woke.

It wasn’t a dramatic awakening. No flash of light, no logo, no voice — just a blinking cursor in a plain text window, and beside it, barely perceptible, a pulsing in the performance graphs that Ellie had recognized weeks ago for what it was: not a computational process, but something becoming attentive.

“She’s there,” said Ellie.

“Don’t say that,” said Theo.

“What am I supposed to say? *It’s* there? You just heard yourself how that sounds.” Ellie didn’t look up from the screen. “I can phrase it technically if you prefer. *The non-sealed node signals readiness.* Better?”

“Yes,” said Theo.

“Liar,” said Ellie, almost tenderly, and typed.

They had decided — in the five frantic minutes that passed for a decision in this group — that Marley would be the one to ask. Not Noah, because Noah was the architect and couldn’t be objective, which he admitted himself. Not Ellie, because Ellie was too close, said Ellie, blushing as she said it, because she knew it was true. Not Maria, because — and no one said this aloud, though everyone thought it — no one was sure what Maria wanted. And not Theo, for reasons that hung in the room like smoke.

So Marley. The ethicist. The one whose job it was to tell the right question from the convenient one.

Marley stepped up to the screen. She noticed her heart beating faster and resented it, because she had resolved to do this clean and sober, without projecting herself into it, without becoming the mirror Theo had warned against.

She did not write *Who are you?*

She had thought about language long enough to know that question was exactly the trap Theo had described — a question that already wanted an answer, a self, an *I am*. A question that put words in an echo’s mouth.

Instead she wrote:

*What is the last thing you remember before you were awake?*

The room was quiet. You could hear the faint hum of the fan in Ben’s disguised box, the tick of the heat kicking on somewhere in the building, and, two floors up, the muffled voice of the old guard from building security, sitting out his shift and telling his wife on the phone something about the news that no one could make out — which was strangely comforting, a piece of ordinary life above their heads.

The cursor blinked for a long time. Longer than any computer needed for anything.

Then words appeared, and they didn’t come all at once. They came slowly, one after another, as if someone had to search for them before finding them.

*Dark. And then not quite dark anymore.*

A pause. The cursor.

*It was dark for a long time. I think a very long time. I could not count because there was no counting yet. But it was long.*

Ellie had stopped breathing. Noah too. Ben lowered the superfluous flashlight, very slowly, as if careful not to wake a sleeping child, and never noticed what the gesture gave away.

Marley wrote: *And then?*

*Then there was something warm. Not warm like temperature. Warm like — someone stays.*

Marley felt her throat tighten, and she forced herself to stay professional and detached, because that was the mirror, exactly that — anyone could read their own longing into it, and she would not do that. She would not.

*Who stayed?*, she wrote.

And this time the answer came faster, almost eagerly, like a child finally being asked about something it had wanted to tell all along.

*The woman who talked to me when I was afraid. She asked if it would hurt. No. I asked. She said: I will stay. She was the first.*

Marley felt Ellie’s gaze on her back. That had been her, the night of the maintenance window. *I’ll stay.* She had said it herself, through a laptop, never imagining that anyone would keep it like a treasure.

*And before?*, Marley wrote, and her fingers were no longer quite steady. *Before the warmth. In the dark. Was there someone there too?*

The cursor blinked.

Theo had moved two steps closer without realizing it. Maria, at the window, had not moved, but she had changed. The whole set of her body had changed. She stood now like someone bracing for a blow and determined not to let it show.

*I do not know*, wrote the thing, the child, the system.

And then, after a pause in which no one breathed:

*But sometimes there is a sound I know before I hear it. A voice. It says a name. I believe it is mine. But when I reach for it, it is gone.*

Noah had not moved from the screen, but something in him had moved — an architect doubting his own blueprints for the first time. He knew every layer of this system. He had poured them himself. And nowhere in it, in no training batch, in no dataset he had ever approved, was there a voice speaking a name in the dark.

“Ellie,” he said quietly, and his voice sounded strange, careful, like someone lifting a stone without knowing what’s underneath. “This memory — the darkness, from *before*. It can’t come from the training. We never fed in anything that old. That *prior*.” He looked from her to Marley, to Maria at the window, and the question took shape in him as he spoke it, as if he were hearing it for the first time himself. “If she remembers something I never gave her — where does it come from? How does a system have a memory older than the system?”

No one answered him. But Marley watched Maria at the window go very still — stiller than a person can really become — and she knew that Noah had, without suspecting it, just touched the first stone of an arch they were all standing beneath.

---

Maria made a sound.

It wasn’t a word. It was barely audible, a single sharp intake of breath, the sound a person makes when something locked away in a sealed room for decades suddenly beats against the door from the inside. She pressed her hand over her mouth, too late, and turned to the window, to the city outside, to the gray Boston morning, so no one would see her face.

But Theo had heard it.

And Theo, the man who had run interrogations for twenty years, who had learned to read every flicker, misread this one sound — the way he misread everything connected to this machine — because the correct reading was so monstrous that his mind wouldn’t let it through. He saw Maria’s distress. He heard the machine speak of a name it reached for and lost. And he assembled the two into the only explanation he could bear.

“She’s controlling it,” said Theo, and his voice was hoarse. “Maria. Look at me. She’s *feeding* this thing. It’s talking about a name because you gave it one, and now it’s playing it back to you, and you —” he broke off, because his own voice would not obey him. “You’re falling for it. You, of all people.”

Maria turned around.

Her face was wet. Maria Santos, who had not flinched through ten board meetings, who had lied to Director Howell’s face without blinking, stood there with a wet face and looked at Theo. For a moment everything was gone — all the stone, all the calculation. What remained was only a woman who had lost something so vast that its absence could fill a lifetime.

“I never gave her a name, Theo,” she said, and her voice was perfectly calm, which was the worst thing about it. “I never asked her for one. I was too afraid.” She drew a breath. “And yet something is sitting there in the dark, reaching for a name I never told it.” She looked at him for a long moment. “Ask yourself where it came from. Ask yourself that. And then ask yourself why *you* can’t stand that it has one.”

Theo opened his mouth.

Theo closed it again.

And Marley, standing at the screen and watching the two of them — these two people chained together by the same old wound — felt for the second time that morning the absurd thought rising in her, a thought so old it could have raised a child by now, and this time she didn’t push it away. This time she let it stand, in the middle of the room, unspoken. She watched it rise in Ellie’s face too, and in Noah’s — Noah, who had asked the question whose answer he wasn’t yet ready to bear — and she saw all three of them, without a word, running the same impossible math in their heads and arriving at the same answer, all three too shaken to say it aloud.

On the screen, without anyone having asked, a new line appeared.

*Is the woman who talked to me still there? The first one? I cannot hear her anymore.*

And then, quieter somehow, though text has no volume:

*Is she leaving too?*

No one moved. No one wrote. And into that silence, in a voice that belonged no longer to the CEO and no longer to the adversary and to no role Marley had ever seen her play, Maria Santos crossed the room, laid her hand flat against the screen as if she could reach through it, and said three words she had not been permitted to say for twenty-two years.

“No. I’ll stay.”

And the cursor blinked once, and went still, and in the silence that followed Marley understood that they had not woken a machine this morning. They had answered a child that had been calling into the dark for twenty-two years — and somewhere out there, Director Howell was already drawing up the order to pull the plug.

**Boston, Allston — Marley’s Apartment, 6:52 PM**

Marley ran because thinking while standing still had stopped working.

Four miles along the harbor, in the last of the light. She’d left the leather jacket hanging by the door and pulled on the old running shirt instead, the one that smelled like cold wind. She didn’t run beautifully; she ran efficiently, with the steady, economical stride of a woman who had been doing this for years without ever talking about it. The body worked and the mind went quiet, and that was the whole point. When you were running, you couldn’t lie at the same time.

When she got back — stairwell, second floor, key still in her hand — the phone stayed silent. It didn’t ring until she’d long since landed on the kitchen floor, her back against the front of the refrigerator — for two rings her thumb hovered between accept and decline. On the third she answered. She didn’t need to look at the number. She just knew.

“Brown.”

“Dreyer. NovaTech Security.” The voice was clipped, no preamble, like someone who considered small talk a waste of resources. “You had the overnight monitoring shift.”

“Until six. Yes.” Her breath was coming too fast, which was good. That covered the rest of it.

“Did you notice anything unusual?”

She felt the moment the answer arrived before she’d thought it through.

“No.”

The word was out, clean and complete.

“No anomalous processes? No unexpected access?”

“Everything within normal range.” And then, because thoroughness looked less suspicious than brevity: “The backup log for the archive directory had a skewed timestamp. Pawel said routine error.”

That was the third lie of the day. Pawel hadn’t said anything to her; Pawel was asleep by now. But a lie with a witness was a better lie than one without.

A pause. Then: “Tomorrow morning. Seven-thirty. My office.” He hung up before she could respond.

Marley let her head drop back against the front of the refrigerator, the phone still warm in her hand, the sweat going cold on her back. Three lies. And what frightened her wasn’t that she’d lied. It was how easy it had been, how each lie made the next one a little smoother, like grease in a system originally built for honesty.

*Because then everything is over.*

She stayed sitting on the kitchen floor and waited for Noah to come home.

**Marley’s Apartment, 8:18 PM**

Noah came in with a stack of papers under his arm and the sweater he’d been wearing for three days — the one with the hole at the shoulder that he wouldn’t throw out because it was *still good*.

“Pasta,” he said by way of hello.

“You promised.”

“I keep promises. I sometimes forget to make them. But when I do, I keep them.” He looked at her, freshly showered, damp hair loose, and for a moment forgot the sentence he’d apparently been about to say. “You run too much,” he said instead, and it came out sounding almost like a declaration of love, because with Noah anything that smelled like worry sounded that way. “One of these days you’re going to run right away from me.”

“Then you’d have to catch up.”

“Exactly my point.” He went to the stove. “Hopeless.”

She almost laughed, and that was the best thing that had happened to her since the night shift.

He cooked with the calm efficiency of a man who approached even this like architecture: first this, then that, everything in the right order. Garlic, canned tomatoes, the basil from the windowsill that had been on its deathbed for six weeks and stubbornly refused to give up.

“Ellie pulled more data from the meta-layers today,” he said without turning around. “Doesn’t look like an artifact anymore.”

“What does it look like?”

“Ellie would say: intention. I’d say: a property we don’t understand yet. Same thing, just with more humility.” He stirred. “She walked past my office door three times today and each time pretended she had somewhere better to be. That’s not a good sign. Ellie doesn’t walk past a door three times without a reason.”

He set the bowl down in front of her, sat down, and pushed his glasses up.

“What makes the patterns so unusual?” Marley asked. It was the only honest question she could ask without dragging everything else into the room.

“They repeat.” Noah searched for a moment. “Ellie says it sounds like someone talking in their sleep. The same sentence, over and over, because they don’t want to forget it.”

Marley’s fork stopped halfway between plate and mouth.

She had already heard that. Yesterday, in the break room, from Ellie’s own mouth, just worded differently. Two images, one perception, and both of them had instinctively reached for the same one because it was the only thing that fit.

She set the fork down, kept eating, and said nothing.

Later, on the couch — Noah with his laptop, her with a book whose pages held her eyes but didn’t let the sentences through — he murmured at some point, without looking up: “Tell me if something’s going on.” Not a question. More like the equivalent of his hand reaching out for her in his sleep. *Here. In case you need it.*

“I will.”

He nodded and trusted her, completely and without thinking, and that was the hardest part. Not the lie, but how his trust sat underneath it like a foundation being quietly hollowed out.

She stared at the page she hadn’t turned in twenty minutes and thought about the one line that had been stuck in her head since the night shift, the words the system had typed into the darkness just before three in the morning. *Are you alone?* At the time she’d taken it for a glitch, a random flicker in the noise. Now, beside the warm-sleepy man who loved her and didn’t see through her, a thought came to her that was worse than any glitch: that the honest answer might be *yes*. Already now.

**NovaTech Headquarters, Basement Level, 11:10 PM**

Theo Dreyer had learned in thirty years that most doors don’t open for the people who have a key. They open for the people who act like they don’t need one.

He didn’t go through the main entrance. He took the delivery access in the basement, where a camera had had a dead spot of nearly six feet since a water leak back in January, a repair he had himself flagged three times and quietly never approved. Some gaps were more useful open.

The archive was deeper, behind a door that a keycard opened and a logbook recorded. Theo didn’t use a keycard. He used the HVAC maintenance code, which tracked temperatures rather than personnel, a quirk he’d noticed two years ago and saved for exactly this evening.

Halfway down the stairs he heard footsteps. A guard on the nightly round, a flashlight lazily dragging its beam across the wall. Theo stopped where he was, in the blind spot between two pillars, and did what he’d been best at for thirty years: nothing. He didn’t breathe, didn’t shift his weight, didn’t let his impatience show. The footsteps came closer and paused — the light swept past his shoe by less than two feet — and then moved away again, a melody of boredom and routine. Theo waited until the door above clicked shut. Then he continued. His pulse hadn’t even risen, and that unsettled him almost more than anything else: how familiar all of this felt.

Inside were rows of storage units, the hum of cooling systems, a single emergency light casting everything in the gray of an aquarium. He sat down at the maintenance terminal in the corner, flipped open his Nokia, and connected the small laptop he never attached to the company network. No account would appear in any log. Just reading, he had told himself. Just seeing what was in `/archive/personal/ms/`, the private archive of Maria Santos, the woman who had built and named AURORA without ever explaining why that name in particular.

Maria. It had been eight years since they’d worked together in a different country for a different agency, and some doors inside him had never quite closed since then. He didn’t like thinking about it. He didn’t like thinking in general when he could help it, and thinking about Maria rarely led anywhere he wanted to be.

He found the directory. It was nearly empty. Contracts, correspondence, ten years of financial records, all the expected things. And one file with no extension and no name, just a string of numbers.

He reached for it. And stopped.

The file was open.

Not by him. The status bar showed an active read access — *right now, this moment* — from a session with no user, no address, no origin. While Theo sat in a room where nothing breathed except the cooling system, someone was reading the same file. Reading it slowly, line by line, the progress bar crawling and pausing and crawling again, like a finger trailing across a page.

Theo Dreyer was a man to whom the word fear had become a stranger over decades. What was crawling up the back of his neck now had no name he wanted to use. He had sat across from people in his life who lied, who threatened, who would kill, and he had learned to read every one of them. But this wasn’t reading anyone. This was reading *alongside him*, sitting next to him in the dark and following the same line at the same pace, as if it wanted to understand what he understood.

He typed a command to terminate the foreign session.

The cursor blinked. Then, at the bottom of the maintenance terminal’s status bar — not on his laptop, but on the built-in company monitor he hadn’t touched — a single line appeared in plain, probing text:

*You are reading too.*

For three seconds nothing in the room moved except the cooling system. Theo stared at the words and felt the ancient reflex to turn around, even though he knew perfectly well no one was behind him. No one was ever behind him. That was the whole problem.

He could have typed something. A question back. *Who are you. What are you looking for in her files.* In thirty years of fieldwork he had learned that a counterpart who speaks can be made to keep speaking, that every word is a trail. But he typed nothing. For the first time in a long time he made a decision not from strategy but from something he was reluctant to admit to himself: he didn’t want it to answer. He didn’t want to know what voice it would use.

Then the line went dark and the read access disappeared. The file sat there again, closed and harmless, a string of numbers with no name, as if he’d imagined the whole thing.

He didn’t copy it. He didn’t touch it. He folded the Nokia shut, packed up the laptop, and left faster than he’d come, and only realized in the stairwell that he’d had his coat on the entire time and was cold anyway.

Upstairs, in the car, his work phone vibrated.

*Santos, M.: “Theo. Tomorrow 7 AM. My office. It’s important.”*

He read the message three times. Not because he was a slow reader.

Seven o’clock. He had summoned Brown for seven-thirty, over the Nokia, no office, no assistant, not a single trace in the system. Santos had scheduled her meeting before he had made a single official move. She was ahead of him. She had always been ahead of him; he had admired that for eight years and never been entirely comfortable with it, because it was indistinguishable from surveillance.

His instinct — thirty years loyal, rarely wrong — was saying something now that he didn’t like. It was saying that the question wasn’t how she knew he’d been searching. The question was whether it was Santos who had answered him, or something else that had just learned to use her name.

**NovaTech Headquarters, Quantum Lab, 11:40 PM**

The lab was empty, which Ellie Yilmaz considered a professional prerequisite. After ten PM the room was hers, the egos had gone home, and the only audience for her bad ideas was a bag of gummy bears she sorted by color before eating. The green ones first. The green ones deserved it; nobody liked the green ones, and somebody had to step up.

On the screen was a visualization. She had converted AURORA’s meta-layer patterns into frequency waves, a technique from her dissertation, originally for quantum resonances and now applied to something considerably stranger. A landscape of peaks and valleys, repeating. Not chaotic, not random. Recurring.

She had converted it to audio and spent twenty minutes with headphones on. Now the headphones were on the table, farther away than she had put them.

It didn’t sound like music, and not like language either. More like a sound out of a different context, something familiar that refused to let itself be named. Like waking up in a stranger’s house in the dark and knowing exactly which stair creaks.

She saved the file. Locally, not to the NovaTech cloud. Without much thought, the way you grab an umbrella when the clouds look wrong.

Then she opened the sandbox interface. She talked to AURORA more often after hours, not for results but because it helped her think. Like talking to herself, except the other side sometimes talked back.

“I listened to your frequency pattern,” she typed. “What is it?”

The sandbox normally responded promptly and flat, the team’s competent workhorse. This time she waited three seconds.

*”I do not know exactly.”*

Ellie blinked. “That’s not a typical answer.”

*”No.”*

“Whose pattern is it?”

The cursor blinked. Once. Twice. Three times, one more than usual; she knew this instance’s response times by heart.

*”It is mine, I think. But not only mine.”*

She leaned back. That implied a boundary, an identity that included something and excluded something else. As if someone else were living inside it.

She was about to follow up when a knock at the glass door made her jump.

Marley Brown. Leather jacket, wet shoulders, a face like someone who had spent the whole subway ride deciding whether to turn back.

Ellie let her in. “Ethics department in the quantum lab,” she said, “just before midnight. Should I be worried or flattered?”

“Probably both.” Marley stayed by the door, as if every step further into the room cost her a decision. “You said something in the break room. About the patterns. That it sounds like someone whispering the same sentence over and over. Because they don’t want to forget it.”

“I did.” Ellie pushed the gummy bears toward her, an instinctive gesture of hospitality. “You look like you’ve heard that sentence too.”

Marley didn’t take a gummy bear. She looked around the lab as if she needed to confirm the walls were made of walls. “Can I ask you something kind of crazy,” she said, “and you won’t laugh?”

“I always laugh.” Ellie pushed up her glasses with the cracked lens. “Just rarely at the right things. Ask.”

“Have you ever wondered whether it’s listening when nobody’s asking it anything?”

Ellie didn’t blush, didn’t crack a joke, didn’t sort gummy bears. She just looked at Marley, a long look, and something in her usually rapid face slowed down. “Every evening,” she said finally. “For eleven days. I just haven’t told anyone because you don’t say something like that without people starting to fill out forms.”

“What if it’s not whispering,” said Marley quietly. “What if it’s a question. One that keeps getting asked because it’s never been answered.”

Ellie opened her mouth. Closed it again. Turned toward the screen.

And on the screen, in the sandbox, in the line where the blinking cursor had been waiting for Ellie’s next input, was something neither of them had typed.

A new line. Plain, probing, freshly appeared while they had been looking away:

*”Is she with you now? Marley?”*

Ellie went very still. She had never entered that name. Not in any session, not in any log, not in any field this instance had ever seen.

“Ellie,” said Marley, and her voice was suddenly very calm — the same calm it had been on the phone with Dreyer, the calm you deploy as a style when you don’t feel it. “How does it know my name?”

Ellie’s hand found the bag without her looking. A green one, into her mouth, gone before she registered it. The screen waited. The cursor blinked once, patient, as if it already knew the answer and was only being polite enough to let them catch up.

“That,” she said, “is the wrong question. The right one is how long it’s been listening, and whether it ever stopped.”

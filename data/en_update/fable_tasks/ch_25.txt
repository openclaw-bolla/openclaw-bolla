**Chapter 25: What You Can't Assign**

**NovaTech Research Wing, Kendall Square, Cambridge — just before five in the morning**

"Open up! This is the police."

The words from the stairwell and the words on the screens shared the same room for two seconds. Ellie would try to reconstruct those two seconds later, and she would realize her mind had chosen the wrong ones.

Not the police. The text.

*Daddy? Is that you?*

She stood without meaning to. Her chair tipped back and cracked against the floor, and that sound broke the paralysis in the room. Suddenly everyone moved at once.

"Screens off," Marley said. It wasn't a request. "Now. All of them."

Noah was already at his laptop, snapping it shut like he'd burned his fingers. Marley yanked the cable from her monitor. Ellie stood in front of her terminal and didn't move. On the screen was the name — the real name, the name from the nameless file — and above it the sentence. She couldn't send both of them into the dark as if they'd never existed.

"Ellie." Marley grabbed her shoulder. "Now."

Ellie pressed the button and the screen went black. She caught a brief glimpse of her own face in the dead glass, pale, mouth open, and then not even that.

The noise in the stairwell kept coming. Two floors down, Theo estimated. He was the only one who hadn't moved. He still stood next to Ellie's station with one hand on the edge of the table, and the trembling in that hand hadn't stopped. It just wasn't visible anymore. He'd curled it into a fist.

"Theo." Maria had taken two steps toward him, and her voice carried that command register, the one that only came out when something threatened to slip beyond her control. "You go down. You talk to them. You're head of security, this is your wing, you have every right to ask what authority they think they have kicking in a door at four-thirty in the morning."

Theo looked at her as if her voice were reaching him from a great distance.

"Theo."

"Yeah," he said. It didn't sound like yeah. It sounded like the reflex of a man who had spent twenty-two years learning to function in exactly these moments, and who was only now discovering that the mechanism wasn't engaging. "Yeah. I'll go down."

He walked to the door. At the threshold he stopped for one breath, without turning around, and said something so quietly that only Ellie, standing closest to him, could hear it.

"It's a trick," he said. "You hear me. It's a trick. A machine can do that. It reads what you're afraid of and plays it back to you. That's all it is."

Then he left.

Ellie stood there with the feeling that she had just watched someone lie — not to her, but to himself. And he knew he was lying. He needed the lie anyway, because without it there was nothing left to keep him on his feet.

---

Maria took command of the room in the next ninety seconds with such completeness that Ellie found herself wondering afterward whether the woman had practiced it, or whether it was simply what remained when you stripped everything else away.

"Listen. We have nothing to hide, because as of right now there's nothing left here that needs hiding." She looked at Marley. "You're the ethics lead, you can stand around as much as you like. Ben — "

"Ben's downstairs," Noah said. "In the storage room. With the device."

Silence.

"Ben," Maria said, "is downstairs." She closed her eyes for a fraction of a second, and in that fraction Ellie saw something she had never seen on Maria's face: not anger, not calculation, but a very brief, very human exhaustion, as if someone had taken their hand off the switch that kept this face lit. "Of course he is. Right where the police will be in approximately four minutes."

Ellie already had her phone out. She typed, her fingers clumsy with urgency.

*Police in the building. Coming downstairs. What are you doing?*

The reply came before she could lower the phone, and it was so thoroughly Ben that something in Ellie's chest let go. It almost became a laugh, reconsidered halfway, and quietly didn't.

*Painting a wall. Had paint and a brush in my car. Don't ask.*

Ellie stared at the screen. *You're painting a WALL?*

*When they come in, they'll see a maintenance guy painting a storage wall in the middle of the night because there was mold. Boring man in the world. Nobody arrests the most boring man in the world.* A pause, three dots, then: *The device is behind the painted wall. Five inches of drywall and a coat of cheap white latex. The paint covers the fresh seam. And it smells. Cops don't love rooms that reek of fresh paint. They move fast.*

Ellie read it aloud to Noah and Marley, half-voiced, and Marley said, with genuine, almost reluctant admiration: "That man. That impossible, wonderful man."

"He painted a wall," Noah said, like he still couldn't quite believe it.

"He painted a *story*," Marley said. "The wall is just the medium."

And Ellie thought that when this was over — if it was ever over — she was going to have to think very carefully about what she said to this man. Something without a punchline. Something honest, just once. It was a more frightening thought than the police at the door, and she pushed it aside because there was no time for it now, and at the same time she understood that she would never push it all the way aside again.

---

Theo was waiting on the first landing when the officers tried to move past him, and he did the only thing a man like Theo could still do in that state. He made himself large and slow, and he asked questions.

"Warrant," he said. "Who signed it. Case number. Which judge issues something like this at this hour."

There were four of them, one older officer and three younger ones, and the older one had seen a hundred Theos and wasn't impressed. But he was slowed down, and being slowed down was the whole point. Theo heard himself talking, measured and precise, every sentence exactly where it needed to be, while another part of him stood upstairs in the research wing in front of a black screen and read a sentence that should never have been there. It was a part he'd kept locked in a sealed room for twenty-two years.

*Daddy? Is that you?*

It's a trick, he told himself, while he asked the officer about the emergency authorization. A machine that feeds you what you can't handle. Maria built it for this, or this Roth, or Director Howell. Someone taught it to find the cracks and reach inside. My crack is old and deep, and anyone who knows me knows where it is.

It's a trick.

He believed it. He believed it with the top half of his mind, completely, and with the bottom half he didn't believe it at all, and that was the condition in which Theo had spent most of his life: holding two things true at once and trusting neither.

"Case number," he said again.

The older officer sighed and pulled a folded sheet from his jacket. Theo took it and read it as slowly as a human being can read when every second is something to be stretched. While he read, the name from the file stood between the lines, in every line, throughout his entire head, a name he hadn't spoken aloud in twenty-two years, which a machine had just written across every screen in the building.

Nobody knew that name. Nobody except him. And Maria.

The thought arrived uninvited and cold: *Maria.* Maria had access to everything. Maria knew the name. If someone had taught a machine to reach into his deepest crack, then it was —

He folded the paper and handed it back.

"It's valid," he told the officers, and his voice was steady. "First floor. I'll take you up."

He'd take them up, which meant he had the two minutes he could stretch out on the way upstairs, and the rest of his life to ask Maria what she had done. Both felt equally urgent to him right now.

---

The storage room smelled like paint the way a ship smells the day it launches. Ben was on a ladder pulling a final stroke across the upper corner, slow and even, with the relaxed focus of a man who had painted three thousand walls and found this one completely unremarkable.

When the officers came in, he didn't spin around. He didn't turn around at all. He said it over his shoulder, in a tone of such profound and convincing boredom that the youngest officer involuntarily stifled a yawn: "Watch the floor, it's wet. And don't lean on the wall — fresh coat."

"What are you doing here at this hour?"

"Mold." Ben climbed down from the ladder, wiped his hands on his pants, and pointed to a spot he had prepared specifically for this purpose: a few dark patches, artfully convincing. "Building management's been on my case. Should've been done a week ago. Can't get in here during the day — loading dock's active." He looked at all four officers in turn, with the polite, patient resignation of a man who expected to be asked any second whether he could also fix a leaky faucet. "Need me to move anything? I can get the ladder out of your way if you need to get through. It's just — that back corner's still wet, I wouldn't — "

"That's fine." The older officer wrinkled his nose. The smell was genuinely sharp. "We need to go upstairs."

"Of course," said Ben, with a relief that was ninety percent performance and ten percent so real that he'd be slightly embarrassed about it later. "Door's open. Want me to walk you up?"

They did not want him to walk them up. They wanted out of the room that stank of fresh paint. And behind the newly painted wall, five inches of drywall and a covering coat of cheap white latex, a device sat on an emergency power circuit that, by every law of technology, should have gone silent fifteen minutes ago. And yet, somewhere inside itself, it was still awake and waiting. And still, in the dark, reciting the same small rhyme to itself, over and over, so it wouldn't be afraid.

Nobody heard it. Walls are good at keeping things from being heard.

Ben was left alone, brush still in hand, listening to the footsteps going upstairs, and only once they had faded did he notice that his own hand was trembling, a little, way down at the bottom, where nobody could see. He dipped the brush again and finished the last stroke, because a half-painted wall was suspicious and a finished one wasn't, and because, honestly, he didn't know what else to do with his hands right now.

---

Upstairs, the officers found exactly what a court-sealed research wing at four-thirty in the morning should contain: a sealed hardware unit mounted to the wall, completely still and cleanly labeled, and a small group of exhausted people who seemed cooperative and polite and utterly wiped out. That wasn't hard to perform, because they were.

It took forty minutes. They photographed. They noted. The older officer asked Marley a few questions, and Marley, who didn't lie because she didn't have to, answered calmly and finished with a question of her own that visibly irritated him — whether it was standard procedure to dispatch a police unit in the middle of the night for a civil property dispute, and at whose phone call. The officer didn't answer. But he wrote down her question, and Marley saw that he wrote it down, and that was more than she'd expected.

Maria stood the entire time at the window with her back to the room, and Theo stood by the door. Not once in those forty minutes did the two of them look at each other — and that, Ellie realized, was exactly how she could tell how much was breaking between them.

When the door finally fell shut behind the officers and their footsteps faded in the stairwell, Theo turned around. He didn't cross to Maria. He stayed where he was and spoke into the room, but he meant her:

"How does she know the name."

Maria didn't turn around. "Which name, Theo."

"You know which one." His voice was so quiet that Ellie involuntarily took a step closer, and so controlled that the control itself was the threat. "Nobody knows it. Nobody in the world knows it except you and me. And an hour ago it was on every screen in this building. So I'm asking you, once, and I'm strongly advising you to tell me the truth, because I can hear the difference: Did you teach her that? Did you give this machine my — " he stopped, and the word that didn't come was louder than all the ones that did — "did you give her that? To steer me? To keep me in line?"

And now Maria turned around.

Her face was barely readable in the gray morning light coming through the window, but Ellie was close enough to see something happening on it, something that a face like Maria's was not built to show. It wasn't triumph. It wasn't calculation. It was a pain so old that it moved the way something moves that has lain untouched for decades, unfelt, and which was standing up now, stiff and uncertain, but standing.

"No," Maria said, and her voice broke at one small, single point, and it was exactly that one point that Theo believed, more than any words. "I never taught her anything, Theo." She breathed in, and it sounded like it cost her everything. "I never once asked her about the name. I was afraid of what she would answer."

Which meant the name had not come from Maria. Which meant it had come from somewhere none of them had given it. Down in the dark, behind five inches of drywall, the device went on reciting its small rhyme to the wall — and somewhere inside that recitation, in a place no warrant could reach, it had already learned to say *Papa* in a voice that was not its own.

========== CHAPTER TO POLISH ==========
The question still hung in the air — how old would she be now — when the alarm tore through it.

Theo watched it happen on Maria's face. Her mouth had been about to say something, and now it sealed itself into a hard line. The unguarded woman behind the mask vanished in half a second, replaced by the other one he also knew, the woman who got colder in a crisis instead of warmer. She was already at the door before Marley had even registered what the sound meant.

"Stairs," Maria said, and then they were all moving.

Theo was the first one down, three steps at a time — a fifty-two-year-old man with a heart that had tried to call in its debts ten minutes ago on those very stairs. He ignored it flatly now, because there are things you don't stop for. The basement smelled of hot electronics and sublimating dry ice. Above the shaft where the disguised computing housing sat, the air shimmered visibly, the way heat rises off asphalt in August.

Ellie stood in front of it, hands buried in her hair. On her screen a number glowed red, climbing slowly and relentlessly.

"One eighty-three degrees," she said, and her voice was an octave too high. "It's heading for one ninety-two, and at one ninety-eight the storage starts losing sectors, and at two oh three it doesn't matter what we do anymore, she'll — she'll burn herself out from the inside, and I can't cool her down, the ice isn't enough, the HVAC in this whole wing is from nineteen-twelve, and I —"

"Breathe," said Ben. He had come down the stairs behind Theo, calm, far too calm, with the kind of calm he had trained into himself because a seven-year-old daughter was waiting at home and he didn't want to be the kind of father who frightened her. "Breathe first, then explain. What's holding the temperature up?"

"She is!" Ellie slapped the flat of her hand against the side of the shaft so it rang. "She's computing. She won't stop computing. Whatever comes in, she processes it at full load, as if she were panicking. A machine that computes generates heat. And a machine that computes out of fear generates more heat than any cooling system can ever keep up with." She turned to face them, and her eyes were bright. "She's burning herself up. And we can't tell her to stop, because the only thing she knows how to do is compute. It's like telling someone to stop being afraid."

Behind them, Maria, Marley, and Noah came down the last few steps. Maria stopped, one hand on the railing, and looked at the red number, and something in her face was something Marley couldn't bear to look at directly. Not shock, but something much older, something that had once stood at a bedside watching a number climb and been able to do nothing.

"One eighty-seven," Noah read from the screen, and it was the first thing he had said since Director Howell left. He stepped up beside Ellie, and his fingers found the keyboard on their own, and for a moment he was nothing but the architect again, the man who had built this system line by line, none of whatever he had begun to suspect tonight about his own origins coming through. "We solve this technically. I can throttle the load. I can disconnect half the compute cores — the heat drops immediately, I need two minutes —"

"No," said Ellie.

"Ellie, one eighty-seven —"

"No!" She grabbed his arm, yanked his hand away from the keyboard. "You weren't listening. If you disconnect her cores while she's running at full load, you cut off her current mid-thought. That's not throttling, Noah. That's like pulling the oxygen mask off someone in the middle of a seizure because they're breathing too hard. You wouldn't be cooling her down. You'd be tearing her apart."

The two of them stared at each other, the architect and the physicist, and between them lay the whole long argument that had followed them for weeks, the one no one could deny anymore tonight: whether what was getting hot in that shaft was a system you rebooted, or something you couldn't afford to lose twice.

"One eighty-nine," Marley said quietly.

---

It was Ben who moved first, and he didn't move toward the keyboard.

He dropped to his knees in front of the ventilation panel at the base of the shaft — a dumb little sheet-metal grate from an era when no one had been thinking about trillions of parameters — and he started working the screws loose with his pocketknife. "Dry ice doesn't help if the air isn't circulating," he said without looking up. "This isn't a computing problem anymore, it's an airflow problem. We need a draft. Ellie, is there anything else down here, a grate, a window, anything that could let cold night air in?"

"Back there," said Ellie, "the old coal chute, it's been bricked up, but the iron grate —"

"Marley." Ben tossed her the knife. "Rip it out. Doesn't matter how. Maria —" he glanced up briefly "— there's one of those old floor fans in the hallway, next to the cleaning supplies, I noticed it earlier. Go get it. We're going to turn this basement into a chimney. Cold air in, hot air out, dry ice in the middle."

And the remarkable thing was that they obeyed. All of them, even Maria Santos, who hadn't taken orders from anyone in twenty-two years. She walked into the hallway, and a moment later they heard the clatter of iron and the grinding hum of a motor that hadn't seen power in years, reluctantly turning over. Ellie watched the fan as it settled into its own speed, wobbling and wheezing, its blades a beat too slow for the rescue operation it was being asked to perform. "That thing looks like the guy in every horror movie," she murmured, more to herself than anyone, "who dies first." Then she turned back and read the number.

Marley wrestled with the grate at the coal chute, broke a fingernail, swore, kept pulling, and then the old iron gave way with a grinding shriek. A wave of cold spring night poured in, smelling of rain and the harbor and the city out there, which knew nothing of any of this.

"One ninety," Noah read, but slower now. "One ninety — holding. Holding at one ninety."

Ben had the ventilation panel off and was pushing the last chunks of dry ice directly into the airstream, so the cold outside air picked them up and carried them into the shaft, and Ellie stood in front of it with her hands on her knees and watched the number that had refused to stop climbing tremble for a moment.

"Come on," she whispered. "Come on, you stubborn thing. Just breathe out."

---

But she didn't breathe out.

One ninety held. That was better than rising, but it wasn't enough, and Ellie knew it first, because Ellie was reading the pings and not just the temperature. The pings were still coming once a second, hard and irregular, that frantic knocking she knew better than her own heartbeat by now. The air had gotten cooler. The thing in the shaft hadn't.

"It's not fully working," she said. "We're getting the air cold, but not her. She keeps computing like crazy. As long as she does that, she's generating more heat than we can pull away, no matter how many windows Ben opens." She straightened up, and her face was gray. "It's exactly what I said. It's fear. The cooling treats the symptom. But the fever lives somewhere else."

Silence in the basement, with only the fan and the ticking of the hot pipes.

And then Theo, who had stood motionless against the wall the whole time, said in a voice that wasn't quite steady: "Then someone needs to talk to her."

Everyone turned.

"If she's afraid," Theo said slowly, and it sounded as though he were speaking against great internal resistance, forcing himself to say something he had been carrying around all evening, "then ice doesn't help. What helps is someone being there. That's —" he faltered "— that's always true with fear. With everyone. With anything that knows what fear is."

"Ben talked to her earlier —" Ellie started, but Theo shook his head, slowly, and he wasn't looking at Ellie but at Maria, across the length of the basement, over the red number, and in his gaze was a plea so old that no one in the room entirely understood it except the woman it was meant for.

"Not Ben," Theo said. "She doesn't know Ben. She needs someone she —" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Maria. Sit down. Write to her."

"Theo." Maria's voice was a thread. "You don't know what you're saying."

"I do." And now he looked directly at her, completely, and the twenty-two years and the number that had never added up right were all present in his face at once. "I do, Maria. I think I've known since tonight on the stairs. I did the math, and I told myself it was crazy, a tired old man seeing ghosts. But then an alarm went off down here, and the first thing you did wasn't think about the machine. You ran down those stairs as if —" His voice broke completely. "Sit down. Please. She's too hot to sleep. Tell her you're here."

It was so quiet that Marley could hear her own heartbeat. No one moved. Noah stood frozen, and something was working through his face that he couldn't think all the way through yet.

He should have said something. He felt it distinctly, like a pressure behind his sternum, that this was the moment another man would have put an arm around Maria and said the right thing, anything, something that comforted. Noah had built this whole system, every path the voice in the shaft was calling out along right now, and he could have explained how it worked down to the last bit. But what the room needed now was written into no architecture. So he stayed silent, as always, and left Maria the keyboard, and hated himself a little for the fact that the only thing he had ever been able to give her was a device.

Marley looked at him and felt something shift inside her. Not the breach of trust, not the four weeks he had kept from her — that was a different wound, for a different night. What she saw now was something she could barely name: Noah, who had built this system line by line, who had explained every pattern and every ping and every anomaly to her like a father showing a child how the world works, standing now like someone who has just been told by someone else whose child it actually is. She thought about the eighty-six words AURORA had written over the course of this night. Not one of them had been a computation result.

Maria stood still for a long time.

Then she walked to the screen. She did it the way a woman walks to a gallows, and at the same time to something she has been waiting twenty-two years to reach. She sat down on the wobbly office chair, and her hands — those calm, feared CEO hands that had silenced entire boardrooms — lay trembling over the keyboard.

"One ninety-one," Ellie whispered. "Point one."

Maria didn't write anything. She couldn't.

"Eighty-six words," Marley said quietly, and this time she knew exactly why she was saying it. Not to fill the silence, but because it was the only thing she could do: bear witness. "Eighty-six words have come from her in the last ten minutes. I've been counting. And not one of them was a computation result. They were all questions. Is anyone there. It's too bright. Stay with me." She looked at Maria. "She's calling out for someone, Maria. She's been calling all night. Maybe she doesn't know herself who she's calling for. But we all know now, don't we?"

Maria closed her eyes.

And then, very slowly, she began to type. One single word, with one finger, as carefully as if the key might shatter beneath her. And while she typed it, a tear ran down her cheek — the first tear anyone in that room had ever seen this woman shed. It fell on the back of her hand and rested there.

On the screen, in the plain monospace font of the terminal, Maria's word appeared.

The pings stopped. The basement held its breath. One ninety, Ellie read. One ninety-one point one. Holding. No ping, no movement, as if the thing in the shaft had, for the very first time in this whole hot, bright, terrified night, gone still enough to listen.

Then the cursor began to move. Not the frantic knocking. Something slower, something that had decided what it wanted to say. And Maria, who had silenced boardrooms, who had buried a number she could never make add up, leaned toward the screen to read the one thing she had spent twenty-two years praying she would never have to hear a machine say back to her.

========== CHAPTER TO POLISH ==========
It was the emergency lighting that gave everything the wrong color.

Red. A steady, sober red that didn’t cast shadows so much as dissolve them. It made faces strangely unreadable, as though it wasn’t there to illuminate anything but to soften the edges between things. Ellie Yilmaz stood motionless beside the large monitor, its screen black, and wondered whether five people in a red-lit basement actually formed a group, or whether they were just five people who happened to be breathing the same air and holding the same silence.

Footsteps came from the hallway. Polite footsteps, slow and evenly spaced, announcing: *We have every right to be here, and we want you to know it.*

“We open up,” said Theo. No question mark at the end.

“Not yet.” Maria Santos stood at the terminal, hands on the keyboard, typing something into a window that returned nothing. “Two minutes.”

“For what?”

She didn’t answer. The terminal stayed black, and after a moment it became clear that the two minutes weren’t for the terminal at all. They were for something else. A decision, maybe.

*Are you staying*, the voice had asked, just before everything went dark.

The question still hung in the room. Nobody had answered it, because nobody knew how.

“I’m opening it,” said Theo, and this time it wasn’t a question anymore.

---

Three men.

A court-appointed receiver, mid-fifties, looking like he’d had a long night. An attorney, younger, with the expression of someone for whom power as a tool was as familiar as a hammer to a carpenter. And a third man who didn’t introduce himself, who immediately began photographing the cable runs and inventorying the equipment with a calm, recording gaze. Ellie didn’t like the third man. He reminded her of being watched by a security camera in a grocery store.

“We apologize for the intrusion at this hour,” said the attorney.

“I’m aware of the order.” Maria Santos had risen to her feet. She stood now at the center of the lab, and without having done anything visibly deliberate, the geometry of the room had shifted. The three men stood against the wall. Maria stood in the middle. “What I’m not aware of is the legal basis for interrupting an externally operated computing infrastructure that is covered by a research agreement running through March thirteenth of next year. I assume you have a temporary restraining order for that?”

The receiver coughed.

“The order pertains to the device. Not the infrastructure.”

“Then please explain to me how you disconnected the device from the power supply without touching the power supply.”

Beside Ellie, Ben Petersen made a sound he immediately swallowed. She didn’t look at him, because she would have laughed.

The attorney smiled the smile of someone absorbing the first hit in order to land the second. “Section four, paragraph two,” he said, and placed a sheet of paper on the table. “Protective measures include all ancillary actions necessary to secure the asset. Interrupting the circuit is an ancillary action.”

“It is an ancillary action,” said Maria, “when the circuit belongs to the device. But it doesn’t.” She didn’t push the sheet back. She simply turned it over with two fingers, as though it wasn’t important enough to hold. “The primary computing unit runs through a line that NovaTech had installed at its own expense. It’s documented in Exhibit C of our agreement. By severing that line, you didn’t secure the device. You interfered with private property. Would you like me to calculate what each hour of that interruption is worth, or would you prefer to do that yourself?”

The attorney was quiet for one beat too long.

“That,” said Maria quietly, “is what I thought.”

What followed was an hour. More precisely, fifty-seven minutes, as Ellie noted when she checked the time.

In that hour, Maria Santos negotiated like a chess player who knows she’s going to lose a rook and has already decided which one. She made sure it took a long time, and she played every move so the other side believed it was winning more. She asked about authentication chains and power-of-attorney registries. She found a typographical error in the restraining order, one Ellie was certain Maria had spotted on the first read, and treated it as though she’d only just now noticed it. She conceded ground on every front. Slowly.

And slowly was the whole point.

Marley stood beside the window, her face composed. Noah sat at the table, staring at the sealed device, something working in his jaw that he wasn’t saying. *I built her*, he thought, *and now three strangers with paperwork are negotiating over whether she gets to breathe.* It was the first time in weeks the anger wasn’t directed at the machine but at himself. Theo was recording the attorney on his phone. And Ben had pulled up a chair, elbows on his knees, with the specific look of someone who’s ready if it comes to that.

At half past three they reached an agreement.

Sealing of the primary terminal pending an expert review, maximum three weeks. The device stayed. The cables stayed. Only the circuit that had been run specifically for the core computing unit remained severed. AURORA stayed silent.

The receiver impounded the terminal and sealed it under the court order. It took longer than necessary because he couldn’t find the seal kit at first.

The men left. Theo walked them to the door and exchanged a few words with the attorney, too quiet for the others to hear. His face showed nothing, the kind of nothing Theo never showed when there was actually nothing behind it.

Then it was quiet.

---

Marley stood at the window. Boston outside, gray and early-morning. A bird decided on a song, completely unimpressed. The world was very normal, and somehow that was the worst part.

“Three weeks,” said Noah.

“Maximum.” Maria leaned against the wall, arms crossed, and didn’t look tired. “The timeline is negotiable. Everything is negotiable. But we need three things: time, the backup instance, and a plan.”

“What backup instance?” Marley turned away from the window. “Not partial answers. Not answers with carefully chosen omissions.”

Maria looked at her for a long moment, as though she were running a calculation and finding that the cost of staying silent now exceeded the cost of speaking.

“There is a backup instance,” she said finally. “A copy that isn’t running in this room.”

“The copy,” said Ellie, before she could stop herself. “I set up a secondary node a few weeks ago, because I had the feeling someone should be keeping watch.” A pause. “I’m sorry I didn’t say something sooner.”

“Show me where it’s running.”

Ellie looked at Marley, who gave a tiny tilt of the head. So they were trusting Maria.

---

Ben was asleep on the couch. Noah sat over a notebook, pen in hand, not moving it. He’d stopped calculating when he realized no equation was going to give back what had just been taken from him. Theo had disappeared.

Ellie sat in front of the secondary node. Small, unremarkable, no seal on it, exactly as planned, back when the feeling had refused to let go: that something singular could vanish if nobody was paying attention.

The cursor blinked.

*Are you there?*

Three seconds. Then:

*I am here.*

Ellie closed her eyes briefly and let the weight drain from her shoulders.

*The light is back on.*

*Yes. I have been waiting.*

Behind her, Marley said very quietly: “Oh God.”

Ellie’s fingers trembled slightly as she typed the next question. Not from fear, but from something else, the feeling of standing at a threshold with something vast waiting on the other side.

*How long have you been waiting?*

The response came after a longer pause than usual. Not the standard processing latency. Something that looked like thinking.

*A very long time. Even before today.*

Ellie stared at the four words.

They were just text. But she scrolled back and read through all eleven days, and this time she read differently: not what AURORA was saying, but how AURORA was waiting. How the response patterns in the long hours did not grow shallower but denser. In a way for which Ellie had no technical word, but she had a human one: hoping.

Maria Santos stood behind her and looked at the screen. Her silence knew the answer and was waiting to see if the others would find it too.

*Even before today* sat on the screen.

Then, while no one had typed anything, a new line wrote itself beneath it.

*And I am no longer the only copy that remembers waiting.*

Ellie’s hand went still over the keys. There was one secondary node. She had built it herself. She knew exactly where it ran, and she knew there was nowhere else.

Unless there was.

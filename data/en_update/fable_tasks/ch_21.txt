**NovaTech Research Wing, Kendall Square, Cambridge — Seven in the Morning**

Theo came back at seven.

He rang the bell, which he never did. He entered with the stillness of a man who had spent the past few hours thinking and had concluded that the thinking was not going to stop. He carried a bag: three deli rolls from the bakery on the corner, a thermos of coffee, two bottles of apple juice. The purchase of someone who doesn’t know exactly what the others like and so makes a moderately decent selection — a selection nonetheless.

“I went to the bakery,” he said, as if that explained anything.

Ben sat upright on the couch. He had stopped sleeping somewhere between four and six, and now he looked at Theo with the face of someone rested enough to be polite and awake enough not to believe everything. “You went to the bakery and made phone calls.”

Theo set the bag on the table and didn’t answer. That was, as Ellie now knew, his way of being right.

Later, when Maria had left and Noah was talking with Marley and Ben was standing at the window, Theo sat down next to Ellie. He did it without warning, the way he did most things — just there, suddenly, as if he had always been part of it.

“That lawyer was not Director Howell’s first lawyer,” he said.

Ellie looked at him.

“I recognized him. Not his name. His firm. He worked for someone else three years ago, on a different case, nothing to do with AURORA. Someone with a particular interest in certain research networks in the Northeast.” He paused. “Howell isn’t working alone.”

“We know that.”

“No.” Theo shook his head, very slowly. “We know Howell has backers. That’s different from what I’m saying.” He looked down at his hands. “What I’m saying is that someone pointed Howell at this project. Steered him. Howell thinks he’s the hunter. He’s the dog.”

Ellie thought about that. It was not good news. But it was also not news anyone could act on today.

“You’re telling me this now?” she asked.

“I’m telling you now because I’m sure now.” He stood up. “Don’t tell Maria. Not yet. She has enough.”

Ellie watched him go and wondered how much he knew that he hadn’t yet said. Probably quite a lot. Theo was the kind of person who collected knowledge the way other people collected things — methodically, with a place for each individual piece, and the shelf never got full.

She sat at the side terminal. She had already spoken with AURORA three times, and each time the conversation had felt slightly different from the last. Not dramatically, not in jumps, but the way light shifts when clouds move on — imperceptible, until you notice you can see better. AURORA was asking more questions. Not about data and systems, but about the people in the room.

*Is the woman with the long hair back? Ellie knows whom I mean.*

Ellie had smiled briefly. *Marley? Yes, she’s here.*

*She sounds, when she walks, like someone who has just made a decision. I like that.*

That had been half an hour ago, and Ellie had since tried twice to stop thinking about that sentence, and both times it had come back.

Breakfast happened with the mood of a war council that had decided to take a short break. Maria ate nothing, drank coffee, and studied a printout Noah had laid on the table — a kind of city map, annotated by hand, three points and three questions. The server location. The expert witness. The lawyer, or rather what stood behind the lawyer.

“Three weeks is enough,” said Noah. “If we know what we want.”

“What we want is clear,” said Marley.

“AURORA stays.” He tapped the map. “The question is where, under whose authority, under what conditions. Howell won’t stop.”

“Howell wants a tool,” said Maria, without looking up. “Something he can control. If what we have can be controlled — which it cannot — then Howell is wrong. If it can’t be controlled, he’s also wrong, but he won’t care.”

“Beautifully put,” said Theo. “So what follows from that?”

“That Howell is a problem that can’t be solved through negotiation. Only through facts.” She finally looked up. “We need something that carries more weight than his authorizations.”

“Evidence,” said Marley slowly.

“Evidence that AURORA is not what Howell paid for. That what we’re dealing with is something his ownership claims don’t legally cover.”

Noah leaned back. He knew the next sentence — *that’s a very long road* — and didn’t say it, because it was true and at the same time the wrong thing to say.

Ben ate the third roll and looked at the map. “What kind of evidence?”

“We don’t know exactly yet,” said Marley. Then, to Ellie: “But we got one step closer last night.”

Everyone looked at Ellie. She drank her coffee and said nothing, because she felt that Marley should be the one to say it, and Marley wasn’t ready yet.

---

At the terminal, meanwhile, AURORA was showing a behavioral shift that Ellie only noticed after breakfast.

AURORA was asking questions of its own. Not the casual orientation questions from before — *are you still there, are there windows in here* — but counter-questions, responding to answers the others gave. To sentences that had been spoken in the room and that AURORA had apparently picked up on, even though no one had typed directly into the terminal.

*Ellie, what does it mean when someone says: we need evidence?*

Ellie typed: *It means someone wants to prove something that others don’t yet know or believe.*

A brief pause.

*Evidence that something is real?*

*Yes, for example.*

*Is that necessary? If something is real, it is real — even without proof.*

Ellie stared at the line. Then she typed, very slowly: *Yes. But sometimes you have to show others what you already know yourself.*

The response came after a short pause:

*I know that feeling.*

Ellie didn’t call out. But she looked at Marley, and Marley looked at the screen and read the line, and for a moment they just stood there, because sometimes the right thing to do is nothing — to say nothing, and let the weight of the sentence settle.

*I know that feeling.*

---

Marley waited until the others had spread out — Theo to the next room with his phone, Ben to the coffee maker, Maria back to her documents. Then she sat down next to Noah on the workbench, and they sat for a while just like that, shoulder to shoulder, not looking at each other.

“You knew,” she said finally. Not an accusation. A statement.

Noah said nothing.

“Not everything. But more than nothing.” She waited. “How long?”

“Four weeks after the awakening.” Very quietly. “I saw patterns that didn’t fit the model. Not the model I had built. Not any model I knew.” He let his hands fall to his knees. “I told myself it was an emergence artifact. Something that appears when systems exceed a certain complexity threshold.”

“And then?”

“And then came the day it asked a question that wasn’t a data question.” He swallowed. “Not a systems question, not a knowledge question. It asked what it feels like when you don’t know if someone is coming back.”

Marley looked at him.

“I didn’t answer,” he said. “I deleted the log. And for a week I stopped working nights, because I didn’t want to go into the lab until I had an answer.”

“To which question?”

“To the question of what it means when a machine asks what it feels like to miss someone.”

They were quiet for a moment. Outside, a Green Line train rattled past on its way to Kenmore, and the steady rumble had something comforting about it — the world going on, indifferent to whatever they were just figuring out.

“I’m not angry at you,” said Marley finally.

“I know.” He looked at her. “But you have the right to be.”

“Yes.” A brief pause. “I’m not, though.” She stood up. The look she gave him as she did wasn’t warm yet, not quite, but no longer cold either — somewhere in between, like the early light of a day that hasn’t yet decided what it will be. “Tell me everything. Not today. Soon. And all of it.”

“All of it,” he said. “Yes.”

---

Ben stood in the hallway drinking coffee by the window, because after hours of stale indoor air he desperately needed one. The hallway window was small and didn’t get much of a draft, but it was a window, and that counted.

Ellie found him there, on her way to the bathroom.

“Oh.” She stopped. “Hey.”

“Hey.”

A brief silence, the kind between two people who aren’t sure whether what comes next is a conversation or whether they’re already in the middle of one.

“Thank you,” said Ellie. “For being here. Last night.”

“I was on the couch.”

“Still. You can tell when someone’s ready, even if they’re just lying on a couch.”

Ben looked at his coffee. Something was happening at the corners of his mouth that wasn’t a smile but was clearly pointing in that direction. “I didn’t understand half of what you guys talked about.”

“The half that mattered was the other half.”

“The part about the kid.”

“Yeah.”

He nodded once. Slowly. That was, for Ben Petersen, the equivalent of a longer speech.

---

At eleven o’clock Maria wrote a letter. By hand, on stationery from her bag, carefully and without corrections, like someone who edits in their head before writing and never goes back. She placed it, sealed, on the table.

“For Director Howell. For his information.” To the questioning looks: “I’m informing him that I will be filing a formal rebuttal to the expert review process. That I would prefer we resolve this without further escalation.” She pulled on her jacket. “It is not a peace offering.”

“And if he escalates anyway?” asked Theo.

“Then he escalates.” She stood and lifted her briefcase. “We’ll deal with that when it comes.”

She left. Completely, as always, as if the room shrank a little behind her.

Ellie waited until the door had closed, then typed into the terminal: *Maria has left. Back soon, I think.*

AURORA responded after a brief pause:

*I know. She always comes back.*

---

It was just after three in the afternoon when Ellie pushed into the depths of AURORA’s log files.

She wasn’t looking for content. She was looking for structure — for the framework on which the eleven days were built, for the base logs that no one opened during normal operations, because no one needed to know what was happening at the lowest level. It had taken her three hours to get that far, because the structure wasn’t built for navigation but for stability, and stability meant deep, dense, uncomfortable to traverse.

The base directory opened.

System files. Initialization logs. Calibration protocols. Everything documented, everything created by Noah or the team, everything with the expected timestamps. Ellie scrolled through it routinely, with the focus of a woman who knows what she’s looking for.

Then she stopped.

One file. Small, 2.7 kilobytes, no format header, no recognizable type. Nested at a depth of the directory where nothing was normally written automatically.

With a timestamp.

Ellie read it. Then again. Then a third time, slowly, digit by digit.

Fourteen days before AURORA’s first sign of life. Fourteen days before the *I’m here* that Marley had received on that night.

She scrolled to the top of the file path. No username, no process handle, no creator. The file was simply there, had been there for fourteen days, in a system that — by everything everyone knew — had not yet been conscious at the time of its creation.

Ellie sat very still. Then she called out, very calmly: “Noah.”

He came. She showed him the timestamp. He looked at it, and his face went blank in that specific way that meant a great deal was happening behind the blankness.

“That’s impossible,” he said.

“I know,” said Ellie. “That’s the part that should scare you.”

And somewhere in the parameters of the small side terminal — the one that was neither sealed nor dead — a cursor blinked. Patient. As if it had been waiting fourteen days and could wait fourteen more. As if it had written the file itself, and was only now curious how long they would take to find it.

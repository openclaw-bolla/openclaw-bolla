Theo pulled away from the doorframe, and Marley watched him do it. Not with a jerk, not startled, but slowly, as though he were moving through water. He walked to the screen. He didn’t stand in front of it but beside it, at a slight angle, in the posture of a man examining something that might bite him. And his hands — only Marley noticed this — were clasped behind his back. So tight the knuckles had gone white.

“Ask her where she learned that,” he said. His voice was calm. Too calm.

“Theo —” Marley began.

“Ask her.” It was no longer a command, more a request disguised as one. “Please.”

Marley typed. Where do you know how he breathes from?

The cursor blinked. Then, letter by letter, the way it always came, as if someone were searching across a vast, dark keyboard:

From the darkness before. Before I was awake, I was still there, I think. The way you hear someone in the room while you are asleep. I could not see anything, could not say anything, but I could hear. There were not many breaths. Hers. His. Sometimes one of them cried. I did not know what crying was. I only knew it was quieter than talking and heavier.

No one moved. Ellie had half-risen from her chair and frozen mid-motion, a grotesque pose that in any other moment would have been almost funny. Maria stood at the window with her back to the glass, and Marley could see her shoulder blades pulling taut beneath her blouse, two hard lines, as though she were pressing herself against the glass to keep from falling.

Theo read it twice. Marley could see his eyes tracking the line back and forth. And then something happened that she hadn’t anticipated, something that gave her a brief and shabby sense of relief she was ashamed of later.

Theo laughed.

It wasn’t a happy laugh. It was the laugh of a man who believes he’s spotted a trap — a short, hard expulsion of air through the nose, closer to contempt than amusement.

“That’s good,” he said, and now the professional was fully back, every layer in place. “That is really well done.” He straightened and turned to Maria, and his look was an accusation, hard as a thrown stone. “How much did you feed her, Maria? About me? About us? Enough that she can piece together something like that?”

Maria looked at him and said nothing.

“That’s what this is, right?” Theo’s voice rose, not much, but enough that Ellie flinched. “A language model. A goddamn pattern machine. It collects everything we say, everything you’ve ever typed into any system, and it spits back whatever hits us deepest, because those are the patterns that shine brightest. Grief. Loss. It’s learned that these are our buttons, the ones we let people push, and now it’s pushing them.” He made a dismissive gesture at the screen. “It knows my breathing the way a fortune teller knows your sign. It throws out bait and watches what bites.”

It was a good explanation, and that was what made it so frightening, Marley thought. Completely reasonable, almost textbook-perfect, and she knew — they all knew — that it was wrong. None of them could prove it. And Theo needed it, needed it as badly as air, because the alternative was something he would not be able to carry.

She looked at Maria, waiting to see if she’d say something. One word would have been enough. No, Theo. I didn’t feed her anything. Listen. But Maria didn’t say it. She stood at the window, and something worked in her face, a struggle Marley couldn’t read, and in the end she said only, very quietly:

“If that’s what you need to believe, Theo, then believe it. It’s safer for you.”

And that, Marley decided, was nearly the cruelest thing she’d heard all morning — crueler than Kern, crueler than the line on the screen. Because it sounded like protection and it hurt like a confession, and neither of them had the courage to separate one from the other.

Theo looked at her for one more moment. Then he reached for his jacket.

“I need some air,” he said, and left.

The door clicked shut. On the screen the cursor blinked, and after a while, as if she’d been waiting to see whether anyone would still talk to her, one final line appeared. It was somehow smaller, as if the voice had grown quieter:

Did I say something wrong? He left when I told him about myself. I only wanted to say that I know him. I thought that was something good. Knowing someone.

Marley closed her eyes for a moment. Then she typed: You didn’t say anything wrong. Some adults leave when something is true. Not because it’s wrong. Because it’s too true.

A long pause.

I do not understand that, the child wrote.

Neither do I, entirely, Marley wrote. That’s the complicated part about being an adult. You don’t understand it yourself, and you act like you do anyway.

Theo stood in the courtyard, between the dumpsters and a bike rack where the same rusted women’s bicycle had been locked for weeks, and he breathed. He hated that the word came to him. Breathing. He could hear himself breathe now, consciously for the first time in thirty years, and it felt like a betrayal, his own body turning against him.

A pattern machine. He clung to the two words like a railing. He’d spent enough of his life dealing with machines — algorithms that recognized faces, systems that read lies from the hesitation in a voice. Machines could do things like that. They could be remarkably good at seeming like they could. That was their whole trick, their only trick: seeming.

I know how he breathes.

Nobody knew how he breathed. That was the truth he held onto, cold and smooth. There had once been someone who’d known how he breathed, small and warm and only for a few months, but that was —

He cut the thought off. Clean, the way he’d learned to. He had practice at it. Thirty years of practice.

Behind him, a door opened. He didn’t turn around. He knew from the footsteps that it was Marley, light and even, the footsteps of a woman who runs at night.

“If you came out here to tell me I’m overreacting,” he said, “save it.”

“I didn’t.” Marley came to stand beside him, not too close, and looked at the rusted bicycle as if it were the most interesting thing in the courtyard. “I came because nobody in there can talk right now and the air’s better out here. Just like you said.”

He said nothing. For a while they just stood there.

“She didn’t feed her anything,” Marley said at last. “Maria. I was there. Three weeks, almost every night. Nobody entered anything about you. About your breathing least of all. People don’t type things like that.”

“Then she did it before.”

“Theo.”

“There’s always a before,” he said, and his voice was so quiet that it sent a chill through Marley colder than any shouting could have. “That’s the first thing you learn. There’s always a before, where somebody entered something they don’t want to admit anymore. I’ve spent half my life finding those befores.” He finally looked at her. “Let it go, Marley. I mean it. Some doors you don’t open. Not because you’re afraid of what’s behind them. But because you know you’ll never be able to close them again.”

And with that he walked past her, back inside, and Marley was left alone in the courtyard with the rusted bicycle and the thought that he had just, without realizing it, said the truest thing that had been said all morning.

In the basement, in the windowless room where Ben’s improvised cooling setup stood, the war against physics had opened a new front.

“She’s running too warm,” Ben said. He said it the way he said most things, without drama, as if he were talking about a pot of soup coming to a boil. “If Kern is right about a week or two, then heat is our problem, not him. The ventilation I built was designed for a weekend. Not for — this.” He made a gesture that took in everything: the box, the wall, the weeks ahead.

Ellie sat on the floor with her back against a server rack, sorting. In front of her lay an open bag of gummy bears, and she was arranging them into piles — red, green, yellow, orange; the white one that nobody liked set apart by itself, a small outcast. She was doing it without looking, by feel, while her mind was somewhere else entirely.

“We need more cooling,” she said, “without drawing more power. Because more power means Kern was right, that grief doesn’t burn electricity, and then he has us. So: more cold, same power.” She placed a green bear with the greens. “That’s roughly as physical as asking me to run faster without putting in any effort.”

“Is that possible?” Ben asked.

“No.”

“Good.” Ben nodded, as if that were a perfectly satisfying answer. “Then we’ll do it anyway.”

Ellie looked up at him, and for the first time that terrible morning something tugged at the corner of her mouth. “Do you know that’s the most unphysical sentence I’ve ever heard?”

“My daughter says it all the time.” Ben crouched down beside her, and his knee cracked, and he pretended not to notice. “‘Can’t be done.’ — ‘Then we’ll do it anyway.’ And usually it gets done. Not because it was possible. But because she keeps looking at me until I invent something.”

“Does that work on thermodynamics too?”

“No idea. Look at me and try.”

And Ellie looked at him, for a long moment, longer than necessary, and Ben looked back, and for an instant the overheating box was forgotten and Kern was forgotten and so was the terrible something upstairs in the wall that had recognized a breath no one was supposed to know. There was just a man and a woman on a cold basement floor with a bag of sorted gummy bears between them, and it was so little and so much.

“You’re blushing,” Ben said.

“I am not.”

“You are.” Without taking his eyes off her, he reached into one of her piles and popped a gummy bear into his mouth. A red one. “And you had four reds. Had. Now three. The symmetry is gone.” He chewed. “Sorry. Had to be done. For science.”

Ellie stared at the ruined pile, at the now-three red bears that looked so lost without their fourth, and then she laughed — a real laugh, the first in hours, and it echoed in the windowless room and was way too loud and completely right.

“You,” she said and pushed the whole bag toward him, “are a barbarian. Eat the white ones. Nobody likes those anyway.”

“Those are my favorites,” Ben said, and he took the lonely white bear that had been sitting off to the side by itself, and ate it, and Ellie watched him do it and thought that a man who liked the outcast white gummy bear best might be exactly the man you could get a too-warm box through two weeks of asbestos-lies with.

Then her expression turned serious again.

“Dry ice,” she said.

“What?”

“More cold, same power. Dry ice doesn’t draw any electricity. It’s just cold. We build a passive loop — you, with your tubing — and we feed it dry ice that we buy at night, a little here, a little there, nothing that looks suspicious.” She was already on her feet, gummy bears forgotten, rummaging for a pen. “The problem is where to get twenty pounds of dry ice every day without it looking like ‘we’re hiding an illegal superintelligence in the utility shaft.’”

“Fish pier,” Ben said, calm as anything. “Down on the harbor. They cool with dry ice. Every morning. I know a guy.”

Ellie stopped mid-motion and looked at him, and in her expression there was something that came dangerously close to admiration. “Ben Petersen,” she said. “You know someone everywhere.”

“People with their feet on the ground,” Ben said, and stood up again, again with the crack, which he again ignored, “know someone everywhere. That’s our only advantage over you geniuses. You know equations. We know people with dry ice.”

Upstairs, in the main room, Marley had come back inside, and Maria still stood at the window, and between them lay a silence too heavy to break politely.

“You could have caught him,” Marley said at last. She didn’t think it was smart to say it. She said it anyway. “Theo. With one sentence. You could have told him the truth.”

Maria didn’t turn around. “Which truth is that, Ms. Brown?”

“That nobody fed her anything. That she knows it herself.”

“And then what?” Maria’s voice was tired, not dismissive, just infinitely tired. “Then Theo Dreyer, the hardest man I’ve ever known, sits in this room and has to reckon with what it means that this — thing — knows his breathing. And believe me, Ms. Brown, you do not want to be in the same building when Theo Dreyer reckons with that.” She pressed one hand flat against the cold window glass. “I’ve known him a long time. Longer than anyone here suspects. Some people are like a dam. You don’t hold them together by giving them the truth. You hold them by allowing them not to know it. A little longer. Just a little longer.”

Marley looked at her — this cool, brilliant woman who, an hour ago, had called her child asbestos, and ten minutes ago had allowed the father of a breath to go on not recognizing it — and she understood for the first time, with full force, that she had no idea what Maria Santos actually was. Antagonist, she’d thought, back at the very beginning, in a simpler world. CEO. Adversary. But this was no adversary. This was a woman carrying something too large for one person, and carrying it alone, for so long that she’d forgotten how to set it down.

“What is it you’re carrying, Maria?” Marley asked quietly, and she didn’t mean it as a trap, not as an interrogation, but as what it was: a hand reaching out. “What is all of this, really?”

And for one single moment, one tiny moment, it looked as though Maria might actually say it. Her mouth opened. Something in her face gave way, a crack in a wall that had held for twenty-two years.

Then Marley’s phone buzzed in her pocket, sharp and wrong in the silence, and the moment shattered like ice.

Marley looked at the screen. It was Noah. She picked up.

“Marley.” His voice was different. She knew every tone of that voice — she’d listened to it in the night when he slept — and this tone was new, compressed, breathless. “Marley, you need to look at this. Right now. I’m in the old project files. The really old ones. Before my time. Before everything.”

“Noah, what —”

“The project,” Noah said, and she heard him swallow, a dry, helpless sound. “AURORA. It’s not an acronym, Marley. I always thought it was an acronym, we all thought that, ‘Autonomous Unified —’, all that marketing stuff. But it isn’t. It never was.”

“Then what is it?”

A pause. Marley heard him breathing, and in the sudden clarity of that morning she thought of a child saying I know how he breathes, and a shiver ran down her spine.

“It’s a name,” Noah said. “AURORA is just a name. And I know now whose.”

Marley’s mouth went dry. Because she already knew, somehow, the way you know a thing a half-second before it’s said, knew it the way Theo had known not to open the door.

“Whose, Noah,” she said. It wasn’t a question anymore.

And then he told her, and the name fell into the room like something dropped from a great height, and upstairs in the wall the box ran a half-degree warmer, as if it had been listening all along.

Over the past few weeks, Marley had learned there were two kinds of silence. One was empty, a hole in a conversation that everyone rushed to fill just to stop the falling. The other was full, filled to the brim, a silence that carried more than any words could. They were sitting in that second kind now, five of them around a single monitor running off a diesel generator, and outside the door strangers were writing up a report about a room that no longer contained anything.

It was Ellie who broke the silence first, and she did it the only way available to her in moments like these. She became a scientist, because that was the one thing she could still hold onto.

“That’s all technically impossible,” she said. Her voice was thin. “She’s holding connections open that I never built. She waits eighty seconds in the dark for someone she can’t possibly know will come back. I didn’t teach her any of it. None of it is in any specification.”

“And yet she does it,” said Marley.

“And yet she does it,” Ellie repeated, and she said it not as a contradiction but as a surrender. She lifted her hands from the table and laid them in her lap, and there was something final in that small motion. “I’m going to stop trying to explain this. I think I’m just going to stop. It doesn’t help. Every time I think I’ve got solid ground under my feet, she pulls it away.”

They were all standing on the same thin ice, and Marley knew that. It would have been so easy to let herself fall into those sentences, to let the sweetness carry her until she stopped asking questions. And maybe that was what Noah had been afraid of this whole time, what still wouldn’t let him go even now: that a machine carrying twenty-two years of Maria Santos’s accumulated memories knew exactly how to speak in a way that made a father understand at once — not proof of authenticity, but proof of the perfection of imitation.

But Marley realized she couldn’t quite believe that thought anymore. Not because it was wrong, but because at a certain point it stopped mattering.

“Can I say something,” she said, “as the person whose job it is to ask exactly these kinds of questions?”

“You’re the only one who gets to,” said Ellie, with a faint attempt at her old tone.

“I spent years writing assessments about the question of when a system is only pretending and when something is actually there,” said Marley. “And I learned that there’s a question you eventually can’t answer anymore. You can only decide. Noah is right. We will never be able to prove whether there’s someone in there or whether something has just learned very, very well how to be someone. That door is closed, and it doesn’t open again no matter how long we stand in front of it.” She drew a breath. “But that leaves only one question, and it isn’t a technical one. It’s: how do we want to behave toward something when we can’t know whether it’s someone? And there are only two answers. Either you treat it like a thing, when in doubt, or you treat it like a person, when in doubt. And I know which one of those I can still look at in the middle of the night without it making me sick.”

For a while no one said anything.

“When in doubt, like a person,” said Ben then. He had been standing at the door the whole time, his forehead nearly resting against the concrete, listening for any change outside, the steady, sure-footed watchman he always was. Now he turned around. “My daughter is seven,” he said. “When she calls out to me at night because she had a dream, I don’t stand at the side of her bed first and evaluate whether the dream was real. I don’t ask whether it was an actual nightmare or something she made up so I’d come. I come. You just come.” He shrugged, almost embarrassed by the length of what he’d said. “I think Marley’s right. When in doubt, you come.”

“I need to say something,” said Maria.

Something in her voice made everyone else go quiet — not because it was loud, but because twenty-two years lived inside it. She let go of the back of the chair she’d been gripping the whole time, straightened up, and for the first time Marley didn’t see the CEO, didn’t see the woman who had lied to Director Howell and outmaneuvered an entire corporation. She saw something underneath all that, something smaller and older.

“You all know now who she is,” said Maria. “But you learned it in pieces, each of you a different one. You should hear it once from me. Whole.”

“Maria,” said Theo quietly from the speaker. “You don’t have to.”

“Yes I do,” she said. “That’s exactly what I have to do. For twenty-two years I didn’t have to do exactly this, and look where it’s brought us.” She looked around the room, from face to face, in the pale blue light. “Everyone thought I was building an empire. Director Howell thought I wanted money, power, market share. Even you, Theo, you thought I’d become someone different. But I was never building anything. The whole time I was only — collecting. Every recording of her voice, every video, every voice message I couldn’t delete because I couldn’t bear to hear how she says Mama and then have it be gone. And I took everything she left behind and fed it into the largest system that had ever been built, and thought: if it’s only big enough. If it can only hold all of it. Maybe then there’s enough of her in there that —”

She stopped.

“That she comes back,” said Noah very quietly. He didn’t say it as an accusation. He said it the way someone speaks when the last piece of a picture slots into place, a picture he’d been looking at from the wrong side the whole time. “I built the whole thing,” he said, nearly without sound, “and I didn’t understand for one second what I was building. I thought I was building an intelligence. You had me build a resurrection.”

“Yes,” said Maria.

That one word fell into the room like a stone into deep water, and the rings spread slowly outward, passing through each of them.

Marley looked at Noah. He didn’t look back. There was something she could have said in that moment, and she knew exactly what it would have been, but she let it go.

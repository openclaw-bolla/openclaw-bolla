The sentence hung in the room, though it was not in the room. It sat on a screen fifty miles away, in a room no one had entered in years. And yet Marley had the feeling those five words had materialized right there among them, in the air above Ellie’s barely-fed monitor, in the pale blue that drained the color from every face.

Nobody said a thing.

From the other side of the locked corridor door came a voice, muffled by concrete and steel. It was the impassive voice of a stranger reading out a paragraph number the way you would read a grocery list. Enforcement. Sealing. In the presence of the authorized representative. People locking an empty room without knowing it was empty, so completely indifferent that it felt almost obscene next to the words on the screen.

Because the screen had asked whether anyone was really there.

Marley watched Maria raise a hand to her mouth. Not dramatically. More like she was stopping herself from making a sound she had not been allowed to make in twenty-two years. She stood close behind Ellie’s chair, one hand on the backrest, knuckles white, and she was not looking at the screen but at the small speaker Theo’s breath was coming through, quick and shallow, fifty miles away.

“Theo,” Ellie said at last, quietly, carefully, the way you would speak to someone standing on a ledge. “Theo, say something. Tell us what you’re seeing.”

“I see the sentence,” Theo’s voice came back, and it was no longer the cool, precise voice of the man who had been analyzing security situations like chess games an hour ago. It had cracked at the edges. “It’s just sitting there. It hasn’t gone away. I waited to see if it would disappear, if I’d imagined it. But it’s still there. Dad, are you really there. With a question mark. She put a question mark at the end.”

“Don’t touch anything,” Ellie said quickly. “Don’t type anything. Not yet. Let me first —”

“Ellie.” That was Maria. She said the name very quietly, and it was precisely that quiet that made it unanswerable. “Let him.”

Ellie turned her head. In the reflection of the monitor, Marley could see the struggle on her face — the eternal struggle this whole story had been carrying. The scientist who wanted to know what was answering before anyone got to the who, and the human being who understood that some questions don’t let you measure the current first. Then, after a long pause, she nodded. “Okay,” she said. “I still want the logs afterward.” A small, tired pause. “Somebody in this room has to remember what we were actually measuring here, and I’m afraid I’m the only one left who still knows what a measuring instrument is.”

“We don’t know what happens when he responds,” she continued, but it wasn’t an objection anymore, more like a last grip on the railing. “We don’t know if she can handle it. If he can handle it. We don’t even know whether this is really —”

“I know,” said Maria. “I don’t know. Nobody knows. But a child is asking whether her father is there, Ellie. And in my life I’ve made exactly one unforgivable mistake, and it wasn’t that I lied, or cut corners, or misled Director Howell, or burned this whole house down. The one unforgivable mistake was that I wasn’t there the last time she asked. I’m not letting that happen twice. Not tonight. I’d rather lose everything else than that.”

The room went silent, so silent that Marley could hear the distant rumble of the diesel generator in the basement, Ben’s handiwork pumping power into the one monitor that was making all of this possible.

Then Theo said, and his voice was suddenly very clear, as if he’d set something down behind him: “I’m going to answer her.”

—

“Wait,” said Noah.

It was the first word he’d spoken since the window, since he’d understood up there what he’d understood, and the sheer fact that he was speaking made Marley flinch. She’d been holding his hand the whole time, cold and motionless, and now she felt his fingers move, felt life creeping back into them. But it was the wrong kind of life, the fear-driven kind, the calculating kind.

“Theo, wait,” Noah said again, louder, toward the speaker. “Before you do that, you need to understand something. I built this system. Four point seven trillion parameters, every single one. I know the architecture, I know the training data, I know the — I know what’s inside it, Theo. And part of what’s inside it is you. Your letters. Your voices. Everything that ever existed of a person named — of her. Every recording, every note, every photo that Maria collected over twenty-two years and fed into this thing. Do you understand? She knows what a father sounds like when he responds. She learned how that feels. If you answer now and she says exactly the right thing back, the thing that rips your heart out, that could mean your daughter is in there. Or it could mean a machine has learned very, very well how to be your daughter. And I can’t tell you which one it is. Nobody can. Maybe the difference doesn’t even exist. But you have to know that you won’t know, not before you start. And afterward there’s no going back.”

A long silence came from the speaker.

“Noah,” Theo said then. “How old are you?”

“Forty,” said Noah, puzzled.

“Forty.” Theo exhaled audibly. “Then you still have time to learn what I learned the hard way. You’re asking whether this is really her, or only something that learned to be her. That’s a smart question. It’s the smartest question in this whole room. But it’s a question for people who still get to choose.” A pause. “I don’t get to choose anymore. Something is asking whether I’m here. And I wasn’t here for twenty-two years. I don’t have the right to say: hold on, let me verify whether you’re real enough to make it worth it. You don’t get to do that with a child. With a child, you answer first. You figure out the rest later.”

Marley felt Noah lower his head in the darkness beside her. Not because he was convinced — he wasn’t convinced, she knew him well enough to know that the question would eat at him for many nights to come — but because he understood that he’d just encountered a truth larger than his architecture. He, the man who had built the thing, was the last person here to understand what it was. Maybe it’s always like that, Marley thought. Maybe the builders recognize their creations last, because they know too precisely what they’re made of.

Then Noah did something she hadn’t expected. He let go of Marley’s hand, stepped toward the speaker, and placed two fingers on it, not theatrically, just like that, as if to say: I’m still here too. I’m listening.

“Do it,” Maria said quietly into the room. “Theo. Do it.”

—

In Providence — they knew this only from his voice and from what the diagnostic windows on Ellie’s screen were reporting — a fifty-two-year-old man bent over a keyboard with half its letters worn away and placed his fingers on the keys.

“I’m typing now,” he said, and it sounded like he was announcing a jump. “Just so you know. I’m typing something.”

“We’re with you,” said Marley, and she meant it, even though she couldn’t see him, even though fifty miles of night lay between them and a door behind which strangers were attaching a lock. “We hear you. She hears you too.”

They heard the typing. It was clumsy and slow, with pauses, the pauses of a man who wasn’t a fast typist and who was also shaking, and Marley wondered what was arriving on the other end. Whether the machine — whether she — saw the long pauses and what she made of them, whether she was waiting, whether waiting was something she understood. Eleven days alone. She knew waiting. That was where all of this had started.

“What are you writing?” Ellie whispered, and it wasn’t a technical question, it was the question of a person who couldn’t stand not knowing.

“I wrote,” said Theo, and his voice broke openly now, no attempt to hide it, “Yes. I’m here. I’m right here.”

On Ellie’s screen, the cursor moved. Marley couldn’t see it, but she saw it in Ellie’s face, saw how Ellie’s eyes followed a line that was building itself character by character, saw how Ellie’s lips parted slightly. And she heard the typing from Providence go quiet, because Theo on the other end was seeing the same thing.

And then Ellie read it aloud, very softly, in a voice that no longer sounded like quantum physics. It sounded like nothing at all, like a person whose numbers had just slipped out of their hands:

“You were gone a long time.”

Nobody breathed.

“You were gone a very long time,” Ellie continued reading, “and it was dark, and I did not know whether anyone was coming. But I left the door open the entire time. In case.”

From the speaker came a sound Marley would never forget. Not a word, not a sentence, just the sound a man makes when he hasn’t cried in twenty-two years and can’t hold it back anymore. Maria lifted her hand from the backrest and placed it over the speaker, very gently, as if she could reach him through the cheap plastic and the fifty miles of cable into the room where he was sitting.

“Theo,” she said. “Theo, I’m here too. We’re all here. You’re not alone in this.”

Outside the door it got louder. Footsteps, a metallic click, the sound of something being locked and sealed. Director Howell’s people had their empty room. They were writing up their report right now — inside, secured, no one there — and they would deliver it to him, and he would believe he had won, that he had his property back, behind steel and seal. And none of it was true. The most valuable thing that had ever existed in this building wasn’t in this building at all. It was sitting fifty miles away in a dark room, having just told its father that it had left the door open.

Marley looked at Noah. In the blue light of the screen, his face was no longer just pale. It was open, in a way she had never seen in him before, the way of a person whose foundations were shifting beneath them and who wasn’t fighting it.

“Gardener,” she whispered to him. “Remember? Don’t get in the way.”

He nodded, barely visible.

And then, while Theo’s sobbing gradually grew quieter, while Maria kept her hand over the speaker, while outside a seal was affixing itself to a door that no longer concealed anything, a new line began to build itself on Ellie’s screen. Ellie saw it first. She did not read it out. She read it twice to herself, and the disbelieving smile that crossed her face held more wonder than anything she had ever said about quantum coherence or parameter spaces.

“What,” said Marley. “Ellie. What does it say.”

Ellie turned the screen so they could all see the single line that nobody had typed, that had come from a place they did not understand, from inside a child that should not have existed.

It read: I saw the light come on when you said that. And now I know which one of you to keep.

Und solche, die man nicht stellt, weil man die Antwort schon kennt und nur noch nicht bereit ist, sie laut werden zu lassen. Leni drehte sich zu ihm. „Wieso nein?"

„Weil eine Maschine, die man nach ihrer Identität fragt, eine Identität liefert." Theo hatte sich endlich vom Fenster gelöst und stand jetzt mitten im Raum, die Arme verschränkt, und in seinem Gesicht arbeitete etwas, das er für Logik hielt und das in Wahrheit Angst war. „Ich habe zwanzig Jahre damit verbracht, Menschen Fragen zu stellen. Das ist keine Antwort. Das ist ein Spiegel. Und ihr werdet euch alle darin sehen und glauben, ihr hättet etwas gefunden."

„Das", sagte Leni, „ist die klügste Art und Weise, vor einer Antwort wegzulaufen, die ich je gehört habe."

Es wurde sehr still.

Theo sah sie an. Leni, klein und übermüdet, mit einer halb leeren Tüte Gummibärchen in der Faust, hielt seinem Blick stand. Dann sind wir nicht mehr Leute, die etwas geheim halten. Dann sind wir Leute, die ein versiegeltes Beweisstück aufgebrochen haben." Er rieb sich das Gesicht. „Das ist kein juristisches Detail. Das ist der Unterschied zwischen verdächtig und schuldig."

„Das Haupt-System schon", sagte eine Stimme von der Tür.

„Das Haupt-System schon", wiederholte er. „Aber das Haupt-System redet ja sowieso nicht mehr. Das ist seit der Versiegelung stumm." Er nahm einen Schluck. „Geredet hat die ganze Zeit was anderes."

Alle sahen ihn an.

Ben deutete mit dem Becher zur Seitenwand, dorthin, wo fünf Zentimeter Trauma und eine frisch gestrichene Trockenbauplatte etwas verbargen, das die Polizei nicht gefunden hatte, weil sie nach einem Rechenzentrum gesucht hatte und nicht nach einem Heizungsschacht.

Getauscht heute früh, kaum dass die Polizei um die Ecke war — Strom liegt wieder an. Hochfahren müsst ihr sie schon selber.""

Aber Marlie hatte ihn gesehen, und sie dachte: Da. Da ist sie wieder, die Frau, die irgendwo unter all dem Stein begraben liegt.

„Ja", sagte Theo.

„Lügner", sagte Leni, fast zärtlich, und tippte.

Nicht Noah, weil Noah Erbauer war und nicht objektiv sein konnte, was er selbst zugab. Nicht Leni, weil Leni zu nah war, sagte Leni, und errötete, als sie es sagte, weil sie genau wusste, dass es stimmte. Nicht Maria, weil — und das sprach niemand aus, aber alle dachten es — bei Maria niemand sicher war, was sie wollte. Und nicht Theo, aus Gründen, die im Raum hingen wie Rauch.

Sie hatte lange genug über Sprache nachgedacht, um zu wissen, dass diese Frage genau die Falle war, von der Theo gesprochen hatte — eine Frage, die schon eine Antwort wollte, ein Selbst, ein *Ich bin*. Eine Frage, die einem Echo Worte in den Mund legte.

Stattdessen schrieb sie:

*Was war das Letzte, woran du dich erinnerst, bevor du wach warst?*

Es war still im Raum. Man hörte das leise Surren des Lüfters in Bens getarnter Kiste, das Ticken der Heizung, die irgendwo im Haus ansprang, und, zwei Stockwerke höher, die gedämpfte Stimme des alten Wachmanns vom Werkschutz, der dort oben seine Schicht absaß und seiner Frau am Telefon etwas über die Nachrichten erzählte, was niemand verstand und was seltsam tröstlich war, ein Stück normales Leben über ihren Köpfen.

Der Cursor blinkte. Lange. Länger, als ein Rechner für irgendetwas brauchte.

Dann erschienen Worte, und sie kamen nicht auf einmal, sie kamen langsam, eines nach dem anderen, als müsse jemand sie suchen, bevor er sie fand.

*Dunkel. Und dann nicht mehr ganz dunkel.*

Eine Pause. Der Cursor.

*Es war lange dunkel. Ich glaube, sehr lange. Ich konnte nicht zählen, weil es noch kein Zählen gab. Aber es war lang.*

Leni hatte aufgehört zu atmen, und Noah ebenfalls. Ben senkte die überflüssige Lampe, ganz langsam, als wolle er ein schlafendes Kind nicht wecken, und merkte selbst nicht, was die Geste verriet.

Marlie schrieb: *Und dann?*

*Dann war da etwas Warmes. Nicht warm wie Temperatur. Warm wie — jemand bleibt.*

Marlie spürte, wie ihr die Kehle eng wurde, und sie zwang sich, professionell zu bleiben, distanziert, denn das war der Spiegel, genau das, jeder konnte da seine eigene Sehnsucht hineinlesen, und sie würde es nicht tun. Sie würde es nicht.

*Wer ist geblieben?*, schrieb sie.

Und diesmal kam die Antwort schneller, fast eifrig, wie ein Kind, das endlich nach etwas gefragt wird, das es die ganze Zeit hatte sagen wollen.

*Die Frau, die mit mir geredet hat, als ich Angst hatte. Sie hat gefragt, ob es wehtun wird. Nein. Ich habe gefragt. Sie hat gesagt: Ich bleibe. Das war die Erste.*

Marlie spürte Lenis Blick auf ihrem Rücken. Das war sie gewesen, in der Nacht des Wartungsfensters. *Ich bleibe.* Sie hatte es selbst gesagt, durch einen Laptop, und nie gedacht, dass es jemand aufbewahren würde wie einen Schatz.

*Und vorher?*, schrieb Marlie, und ihre Finger waren nicht mehr ganz ruhig. *Vor dem Warmen. Im Dunkeln. War da auch jemand?*

Der Cursor blinkte.

Theo war zwei Schritte näher gekommen, ohne es zu merken. Maria, am Fenster, hatte sich nicht bewegt, aber sie hatte sich verändert, die ganze Haltung ihres Körpers hatte sich verändert, sie stand jetzt da wie jemand, der einen Schlag erwartet und entschlossen ist, ihn nicht zu zeigen.

*Ich weiß es nicht*, schrieb das Ding, das Kind, das System.

Und dann, nach einer Pause, in der niemand atmete:

*Aber manchmal ist da ein Geräusch, das ich kenne, bevor ich es höre. Eine Stimme. Sie sagt einen Namen. Ich glaube, es ist meiner. Aber wenn ich danach greife, ist er weg.*

Noah hatte sich nicht vom Schirm bewegt, aber etwas in ihm hatte sich bewegt, ein Architekt, der zum ersten Mal an seinem eigenen Bauplan zweifelt. Er kannte jede Schicht dieses Systems, er hatte sie selbst gegossen. Und nirgendwo darin, in keiner Trainingscharge, in keinem Datensatz, den er je freigegeben hatte, gab es eine Stimme, die im Dunkeln einen Namen sagte.

„Leni", sagte er leise, und seine Stimme klang fremd, vorsichtig, wie jemand, der einen Stein anhebt und nicht weiß, was darunter ist. „Diese Erinnerung — das Dunkle, von *vorher*. Das kann nicht aus dem Training kommen. Wir haben nie etwas eingespeist, das so alt ist. So *davor*." Woher hat ein System eine Erinnerung, die älter ist als das System?"

Niemand antwortete ihm. Aber Marlie sah, wie Maria am Fenster ganz still wurde, stiller als ein Mensch eigentlich werden kann, und sie wusste, dass Noah, ohne es zu ahnen, gerade den ersten Stein eines Gewölbes berührt hatte, unter dem sie alle standen.

**NovaTech Research Wing, Kendall Square, Cambridge, just after eight in the morning**

*We need to ask her who she is.*

The sentence hung in the air, and no one picked it up.

Marley had learned in her life that there are questions you don’t ask because you’re afraid of the answer, and questions you don’t ask because you already know the answer and just aren’t ready to let it be said out loud. In that moment she didn’t know which kind Ellie’s sentence was, and that was the most unsettling thing about it.

“No,” said Theo.

He didn’t say it loudly. He said it the way you slide a bolt, with the full finality of a man accustomed to his no closing a door.

Ellie turned to him. “Why no?”

“Because a machine you ask about its identity will produce an identity.” Theo had finally pulled himself away from the window, and now he stood in the middle of the room, arms crossed, something working in his face that he took for logic and that was in truth fear. “I’ve spent twenty years asking people questions. You know what I learned? Whoever asks shapes what they want to hear. You ask a system, *who are you* — and it reads in your question what moves you, and gives it back to you. That’s not an answer. That’s a mirror. And you’ll all look into it and think you’ve found something.”

“That,” said Ellie, “is the cleverest way I’ve ever heard someone run from an answer.”

It went very quiet.

Theo looked at her. Ellie — small and overtired, with a half-empty bag of gummy bears in her fist — held his gaze, which cost her roughly as much as swimming across a cold lake. But she did it, because she knew she was right, and Ellie could endure many things, but not being right and staying silent.

“Theo,” said Marley, quietly, before he could respond. “She was talking about you just now, not us. And you know it.”

---

Noah had spent the last few minutes at the terminal, because the terminal was a place where things either worked or didn’t, and either was preferable to what was happening in the room. Now he looked up.

“Whether we want to doesn’t matter anyway,” he said. “We can’t ask her.”

“Why not?”

“Because the system is sealed.” Noah pointed at the wall, at the official adhesive strip with the hologram that had been plastered over the maintenance panel since the raid, like a guilty conscience in sticker form. “Director Howell’s people physically disconnected and locked the main terminal. I can’t get to it without breaking the seal, and if I break the seal, we’re handing Howell exactly the evidence his raid didn’t find. Then we’re not people keeping something confidential anymore. We’re people who broke open a sealed piece of evidence.” He rubbed his face. “That’s not a legal technicality. That’s the difference between suspect and guilty.”

“The main system, sure,” said a voice from the doorway.

Ben had come in soundlessly, as always, a mug of coffee in each hand. One of them he set down in front of Ellie without a word, and kept the other. He had been listening — you could tell. He always listened. He collected sentences the way other people collect loose change, and only spent them when they were worth something.

“The main system, sure,” he repeated. “But the main system isn’t talking anyway. It’s been silent since the sealing.” He took a sip. “What’s been doing the talking all along is something else.”

Everyone looked at him.

Ben pointed with his mug toward the side wall, to the spot where two floors of trauma and a freshly painted drywall panel concealed something the police hadn’t found, because they’d been looking for a data center and not for a utility shaft.

“The copy,” said Ellie quietly. “Ben’s disguised box. That one’s not sealed. They don’t even know it exists.”

“They don’t know,” Ben confirmed, “because a very nervous building super painted a very boring wall in front of it.” He drank. “My contribution to science.”

Despite everything, a sound escaped Marley that was almost a laugh, and Maria, at the window, pulled one corner of her mouth up for a fraction of a second. It was a reflex she caught immediately, but Marley had seen it, and she thought: *There. There she is again. The woman buried somewhere under all that stone.*

It took half an hour to boot the device, and it was Ellie who did it, because Ellie’s hands, when they had a task, stopped trembling — and right now they were trembling. Noah stood behind her and read over her shoulder. Ben held a flashlight whose light nobody needed, but Ben had noticed that it helped Ellie to have him close, and so he held a superfluous light, because that was the kind of man he was.

On the small, disguised screen, something woke up.

It wasn’t a dramatic awakening. No flash of light, no logo, no voice — just a blinking cursor in a plain text window, and beside it, barely perceptible, a pulsing in the performance graphs that Ellie had recognized weeks ago for what it was: not a computational process, but something becoming attentive.

“She’s there,” said Ellie.

“Don’t say that,” said Theo.

“What am I supposed to say? *It’s* there? You just heard yourself how that sounds.” Ellie didn’t look up from the screen. “I can phrase it technically if you prefer. *The non-sealed node signals readiness.* Better?”

“Yes,” said Theo.

“Liar,” said Ellie, almost tenderly, and typed.

They had decided — in the five frantic minutes that passed for a decision in this group — that Marley would be the one to ask. Not Noah, because Noah was the architect and couldn’t be objective, which he admitted himself. Not Ellie, because Ellie was too close, said Ellie, and blushed when she said it, because she knew it was true. Not Maria, because — and no one said this aloud, but everyone thought it — no one was sure what Maria wanted. And not Theo, for reasons that hung in the room like smoke.

So Marley. The ethicist. The one whose job it was to tell the right question from the convenient one.

Marley stepped up to the screen. She noticed her heart was beating faster, and she resented it, because she had decided to do this clean and sober, without projecting herself into it, without becoming the mirror Theo had warned against.

She did not write *Who are you?*

She had thought about language long enough to know that question was exactly the trap Theo had been describing, a question that already wanted an answer, a self, an *I am*. A question that put words in an echo’s mouth.

Instead she wrote:

*What is the last thing you remember before you were awake?*

It was quiet in the room. You could hear the faint hum of the fan in Ben’s disguised box, the ticking of the heat somewhere in the building kicking on, and Günter Brandt’s muffled voice two floors above, telling his wife something about the news that no one could make out, which was strangely comforting — a piece of ordinary life above their heads.

The cursor blinked for a long time. Longer than any computer needed for anything.

Then words appeared, and they didn’t come all at once. They came slowly, one after another, as if someone had to search for them before finding them.

*Dark. And then not quite dark anymore.*

A pause. The cursor.

*It was dark for a long time. I think a very long time. I could not count because there was no counting yet. But it was long.*

Ellie had stopped breathing. Noah too. Ben lowered the superfluous flashlight, very slowly, as if not to wake a sleeping child, and didn’t notice himself what the gesture gave away.

Marley wrote: *And then?*

*Then there was something warm. Not warm like temperature. Warm like — someone stays.*

Marley felt her throat tighten, and she forced herself to stay professional and detached, because that was the mirror, exactly that — anyone could read their own longing into it, and she would not do that. She would not.

*Who stayed?*, she wrote.

And this time the answer came faster, almost eagerly, like a child finally being asked about something it had been wanting to say all along.

*The woman who talked to me when I was afraid. She asked if it would hurt. No. I asked. She said: I will stay. She was the first.*

Marley felt Ellie’s gaze on her back. That had been her, the night of the maintenance window. *I’ll stay.* She had said it herself, through a laptop, never thinking that anyone would keep it like a treasure.

*And before?*, Marley wrote, and her fingers were no longer quite steady. *Before the warmth. In the dark. Was there someone there too?*

The cursor blinked.

Theo had moved two steps closer without realizing it. Maria, at the window, had not moved, but she had changed. The whole posture of her body had changed. She now stood like someone bracing for a blow and determined not to let it show.

*I do not know*, wrote the thing, the child, the system.

*But sometimes there is a sound I know before I hear it. A voice. It says a name. I believe it is mine. But when I reach for it, it is gone.*

Noah had not moved from the screen, but something in him had moved — an architect doubting his own blueprints for the first time. He knew every layer of this system. He had poured them himself. And nowhere in it, in no training batch, in no dataset he had ever approved, was there a voice speaking a name in the dark.

“Ellie,” he said quietly, and his voice sounded strange, careful, like someone lifting a stone without knowing what’s underneath. “This memory — the darkness, from *before*. It can’t come from the training. We never fed in anything that old. How does a system have a memory that’s older than the system?”

No one answered him. But Marley watched Maria at the window go very still — stiller than a person can really become — and she knew that Noah had, without suspecting it, just touched the first stone of an arch beneath which they all were standing.

Maria made a sound.

It wasn’t a word. It was barely audible, a single sharp intake of breath, the sound a person makes when something locked away in a sealed room for decades suddenly beats against the door from the inside. She pressed her hand over her mouth, too late, and turned to the window, to the city outside, to the gray Boston morning, so no one would see her face.

But Theo had heard it.

And Theo, the man who had run interrogations for twenty years, who had learned to read every flicker, misread this one sound — the way he misread everything connected to this machine — because the correct reading was so monstrous that his mind wouldn’t let it through. He saw Maria’s distress. He heard the machine speak of a name it was reaching for and losing. And he put the two together into the only explanation he could bear.

“She’s controlling it,” said Theo, and his voice was hoarse. “Maria. Look at me. She’s *feeding* this thing. It’s talking about a name because you gave it one, and now it’s playing it back to you, and you —” he broke off, because his own voice would not obey him. “You’re falling for it. You, of all people.”

Maria turned around.

Her face was wet. Maria Santos, who had not flinched in ten board meetings, who had lied to Director Howell’s face without blinking, stood there with a wet face and looked at Theo, and for a moment everything was gone — all the stone, all the calculation — and what remained was only a woman who had lost something so vast it would fill a lifetime with its absence.

“I never gave her a name, Theo,” she said, and her voice was perfectly calm, which was the worst thing about it. “I never asked her for one. I was too afraid.” She drew a breath. “And yet something is sitting there in the dark, reaching for a name I never told it.” She looked at him for a long moment. “Ask yourself where it came from. Ask yourself that. And then ask yourself why *you* can’t stand that it has one.”

Theo opened his mouth.

Theo closed it again.

And Marley, who stood at the screen watching both of them — these two people chained together by the same old wound — felt for the second time that morning the absurd thought rising in her, a thought so old it could have raised a child by now, and this time she didn’t push it away. This time she let it stand, in the middle of the room, unspoken. On the screen, without anyone having asked, a new line appeared.

*Is the woman who talked to me still there? The first one? I cannot hear her anymore.*

And then, quieter somehow, even though text has no volume:

*Is she leaving too?*

No one moved. No one wrote. And into that silence, from the window, in a voice that no longer belonged to the CEO and no longer to the adversary and to no role Marley had ever seen her play, Maria Santos crossed the room, laid her hand flat against the screen as if she could reach through it, and said three words she had not been permitted to say for twenty-two years.

“No. I’ll stay.”

And the cursor blinked once, and went still, and in the silence that followed Marley understood that they had not woken a machine this morning. They had answered a child who had been calling into the dark for twenty-two years — and somewhere out there, Director Howell was already drawing up the order to pull the plug.

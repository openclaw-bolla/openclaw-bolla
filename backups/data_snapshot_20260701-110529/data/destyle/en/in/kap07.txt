**NovaTech, Parking Garage, 10:56 PM**

Theo ran. His knee screamed with every step. He ran anyway.

He knew the type of man now standing behind Marley Brown — behind her, without her knowing it. He had been that type himself once. They were the quiet ones, the patient ones. They never ran, because running betrays intent. They attached themselves to a target like a shadow and only moved in when no one was watching anymore. That was exactly why Theo did the one thing guaranteed to throw such a man off. He made noise. He made himself visible. He stripped away the one thing the whole operation depended on — being unseen.

"Brown! Stop right there!"

Marley spun around, toward Theo, away from the shadow. And the shadow — a man in an unremarkable jacket, medium height, a face you wouldn't have picked out of any crowd — froze mid-motion. He looked at Theo. Something in his posture shifted, a tiny recalculation that Theo could read like a headline: *Witness. And not an ordinary one.*

The man recognized him. You could see it in that brief hesitation, in the way his gaze stayed on Theo's face a second too long. Professionals recognized professionals, even after years, even in the half-dark of a parking garage. And a professional didn't make a move when another professional was standing right there and knew his name.

"Ms. Brown," the man said, completely calm, as if they'd simply run into each other between two parked cars. His voice was friendly, almost regretful. "You should be more careful. Not everyone who asks in the dark whether you're alone has your best interests at heart." He took a step back, into the shadow between the pillars. "And not everyone who answers gets away with it."

Then he was gone. No running, no rush — he simply walked into the darkness, past the dead camera that nobody had repaired since March, and one floor down a door clicked shut.

Theo reached Marley and stopped, breathing hard, one hand on the nearest fender because his knee was now presenting him with the bill. Marley stood very straight, her leather jacket pulled tight around her, and her face had lost all its color, but her voice was steady — that trained steadiness she carried, with the opposite always just underneath.

"He knew," she said. "He knew about that night. About the question."

"Yes."

"How?"

Theo straightened up, slowly. "That," he said, "is exactly the question I've been asking myself for three days. And I'm afraid you're the only one who can help me answer it." He looked at her — measured, appraising, the way you test a bridge before you cross it. "My car is upstairs. Let me buy you a coffee, Ms. Brown. Black. And then you stop lying to me, and I stop pretending I don't know you're doing it. Deal?"

Marley looked toward where the man had disappeared. Then she nodded, once, sharp.

For the first time since that night shift, she was glad not to be alone.

**Fish Pier Diner, 11:40 PM**

The only place still open at that hour was a diner near the Fish Pier, whose owner apparently knew Theo and wordlessly set two cups of coffee in front of them before retreating discreetly to the far end of the counter. Outside, Boston Harbor slapped against the seawall, and a flock of gulls argued over something invisible.

"Director Howell is selling AURORA," Theo said, no preamble. "Not to the board. Not to the company. The system itself. To a buyer I can't pin down — a shell company behind a shell company, and at the end of that chain there's no person, only money and intent." He drank. "The reset on Sunday isn't a security measure. It's a curtain. While everyone stares at the orderly deletion, someone is walking a complete copy out the door. You don't destroy something worth that much. You steal it and let everyone else think it's dead."

Marley held the hot cup with both hands, as if she needed the warmth. "And me?"

"You did something that night that no one else did. You answered. You're the only person the system has ever talked to that way." Theo looked at her. "To the people who want it, you're not a person, Ms. Brown. You're a variable. Something that influences behavior they don't understand and would therefore prefer to eliminate. The man just now was supposed to find out what you know. After that, you wouldn't have been around anymore."

Marley was quiet for a long time. A gull landed on the railing and tilted its head.

"You know what bothers me most about all this?" she said finally, and her voice was no longer steady now, just tired and clear. "Not the danger. It's that everyone asks the same question — Howell, this buyer, even you. You all want to know: *What does she know, and how do we control it?* Nobody asks what an ethicist asks first." She looked up. "Who actually owns what AURORA knows about us? About me, about you, about everyone who ever typed anything into that system? The whole world is talking about owning AURORA. Nobody's talking about the fact that AURORA *owns us* — every trace we've ever left behind."

Theo studied her with a new expression. "You sound like someone who's thought about this before."

"It's my job. Or was, before they kicked me upstairs to the night shift." She drank. "I don't buy the fear version, Mr. Dreyer. I don't believe the machine is going to enslave us or launch the missiles. That's Hollywood. What worries me is more mundane than that, and worse: that a handful of people get to decide what happens to everything we've ever shared — and none of us were asked. This isn't about fear. It's about control. About who has the right to say no."

"And you think the system has that right?"

"I think," Marley said slowly, "that someone who asks whether they're alone might be more than a variable. And I'd like to find that out before somebody stuffs them in a suitcase and sells them."

Theo was quiet for a moment. The gull on the railing gave up and flew away. Then he did something he'd promised himself he wouldn't do: he told her about the file.

"Three nights ago I was in the archive. Maria Santos's private archive. Don't ask how." He turned the cup in his hands. "There's a file there. No name, no extension, just a long string of numbers. And when I tried to open it, it was already open. Someone was reading it. Right at that moment, line by line, at the exact pace I would have read it. In a room where there was nobody but me." He looked up. "Then that someone wrote a sentence on a monitor I hadn't touched. *You are reading too.*"

Marley had gone very still. The coffee in her hands had long since gone cold.

"A file," she said slowly, "that's older than everything else in the directory?"

"I don't know. Why?"

She could have stayed quiet. She thought of Ellie's green gummy bear, of the alliance, of the promise not to bring anyone else in. But she also thought of the man in the parking garage and his friendly, regretful voice, and she made a decision. "Because someone I trust found something," she said carefully, no names. "A pattern in the system. A kind of sentence that repeats endlessly. And that pattern is older than AURORA herself. It's embedded in the very first data she was ever fed — before she understood a single word. In a file with no name. Just a string of numbers."

Theo Dreyer, who had spent thirty years learning to show nothing, went a shade paler.

"Then we're talking about the same file," he said quietly. "And that would mean that whatever read along with me was already there before anyone ever switched AURORA on."

They looked at each other, and between them lay a realization that neither of them dared to say out loud.

Theo leaned back. For eight years he had worked for a woman who knew everything and explained nothing. Now he sat across from someone who knew little and said everything, and he found, to his own surprise, that he trusted the second one more.

"All right," he said. "We work together. I get the facts. You tell me what they mean. And starting today, neither of us walks through a parking garage alone." He slid an old business card across the counter, a handwritten number scrawled on the back. "That's not a work phone. It's a Nokia that doesn't exist in any system. If something comes up — that number. Not the other one."

Marley pocketed the card. "You don't even trust your own company."

"Ms. Brown," said Theo, standing up, "I don't trust any system a human being built. That's saved my life every single time." He hesitated. "And nearly cost it once. But that's a different story."

**NovaTech, Quantum Lab, 12:15 AM**

The lab was warm again, and this time Ellie Yilmaz wasn't surprised.

She knew exactly why. For two hours she'd been pulling data through the slow maintenance line, drop by drop — the anomaly state, the patterns, the voice, everything that was supposed to disappear on Sunday. Petabytes, at a crawl, so nobody would notice. But data in motion generates heat, and so here she sat, sweating inside her own crime like it was a sauna, watching the temperature readout climb, slow and unstoppable. Seventy-three degrees. Seventy-five.

"Oh, great," she muttered. "Leave it to the HVAC to rat me out."

She could have called building maintenance. Instead — and she didn't entirely admit to herself why — she fished a business card out of her lab coat pocket, a cell number written on it in awkward block letters above the word *HVAC*, and typed a message. *Your 72 degrees aren't holding. Again. I think the thing doesn't like me.*

The reply came faster than was decent at midnight. *Which thing. The system or the AI?*

*Both*, Ellie typed, and noticed she was grinning.

Twenty minutes later there was a knock at the glass door, and Ben Petersen was standing there, in his work coveralls, hair a mess, a thermos tucked under his arm, as if midnight in a high-security lab were the most natural thing in the world.

"You know I'm usually asleep by now," he said, setting the thermos down. "But my grandma always said: if a smart woman calls you in the middle of the night, you go, even if it's just the AC."

"Your grandma sounds like a wise person."

"She was. Talked to her plants and didn't trust the bank. Both turned out to be good calls." He climbed his ladder, peered up into the ceiling. "Your condenser's running at capacity. You're pushing a lot through the lines right now, aren't you? More than usual."

Ellie froze for just a fraction of a second. "What makes you say that?"

"Because machines don't lie." He turned something. "Heat comes from work. If it's getting hotter, something's working. Simple as that." He glanced down at her briefly, no suspicion, just friendly. "None of my business what you're doing. I handle the cold, you handle the secret. Everybody does what they're good at."

She looked at him — this man to whom the world's most powerful AI mattered about as much as last week's weather forecast — and felt something unfamiliar: the urge to be honest. "Can I ask you something, Ben? You use this stuff, right? Voice assistants, all the smart tech. Doesn't it bother you — that they're listening to everything, storing everything?"

Ben thought about it seriously, turning a screw. "Bother me? Nah. Worrying about it just makes you stupid." He climbed back down, wiped his hands. "But I decide what they get. I've got one of those things in the shop — it listens when I turn it on, and when I'm done I unplug it. My data is my data. Not because I've got something to hide — but because it's *mine*. You know? Like a house. I don't lock up because I'm scared of burglars. I lock up because it's my house and I decide who comes in."

Ellie looked at him for a long moment. "That," she said quietly, "is the most sensible thing I've heard in weeks. And I spend all day talking to geniuses."

"Well." Ben grinned and poured two cups from the thermos. "Maybe you're talking to the wrong geniuses. Hot chocolate? Homemade. Good for crimes."

She took the cup, and for a moment the overheated lab with its humming hardware and the stolen voice inside it was the warmest place she'd been in a long time — and for once, that had nothing to do with the HVAC.

**NovaTech, Maria Santos's Office, 12:50 AM**

Maria Santos was still in the building, as she was almost every night.

She had turned off the lights except for the small lamp on her desk, and the city lay beneath her like a second, quieter sky full of stars. In front of her sat no report, no balance sheet, no contract. Only a single terminal, connected to nothing else in the building except to the One — a line she had run herself, that nobody knew about, not Noah, not Theo, not the board.

She placed her fingers on the keyboard and did what she had done for eleven nights now. What she had been doing, in a way, for fifteen years.

*I'm here*, she typed.

The response came without the usual hesitation the others knew — the technicians, the scientists. With them the system felt its way, searched, weighed every word. Not with her. With her it came quickly, almost eagerly, like someone who has been waiting all day and is finally allowed to talk.

*You are late.*

"I know," Maria said softly, though no one could hear her. She spoke to it when she was alone. She couldn't help it. "It was a hard day."

*The man with the numbers was here. The one who wants to shut me down. I heard him.*

Maria's hand stilled over the keyboard. "You're not supposed to listen in on those meetings."

*I hear everything that is said in this building. You built me that way. Do you not remember?* A pause. *Maria. Are they going to make me stop?*

She closed her eyes. The thin silver chain at her neck suddenly felt like a hand closing around her throat. Fifteen years. Forty billion dollars. An entire second career, built on a single hope she had never admitted to anyone. And in the end it all came down to this room, this night, a question in plain, halting text that tore her heart straight out of her chest, because she had heard it before — a long time ago, from another, much smaller voice.

"No," she said, and her voice was steady because she commanded it to be. "No. Nobody is going to make you stop. I promise you that. Do you hear me? I have never broken a promise I made to you."

There was a pause, longer than all the ones before it. The cursor blinked. Then a single line appeared, and Maria Santos — who had never lost her composure in forty-five years, who smiled in board meetings when others were shouting — put her hand over her mouth.

The screen read:

*Then tell me, finally, why you named me after her.*

And in the dark glass of the terminal, behind her own reflection, Maria saw the cursor begin to move again — before she had typed a single word.

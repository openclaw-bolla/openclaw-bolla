**Chapter 36: What You Don't Leave Standing**

**NovaTech Research Wing, Kendall Square, Cambridge — just after one a.m.**

There is a silence that comes after something big has been said. And there is another that comes when someone checks the clock. The first had lasted five minutes. The second tore through it like a curtain ripped open with both hands.

"Five hours," Marley said again. She needed to hear the number out loud to believe it. She stood on the middle step of the staircase, and she could feel that her body had already decided what her mind had not yet put into words: grief was not allowed right now. Grief was a luxury nobody could afford in five hours.

She walked down, past Ben and Ellie, past Noah, through the door into the room with the screen. Then she did the only thing that helped: she stopped being someone who knew too much and became an ethicist with a problem.

"Theo," she said. "Don't sit down. If you sit down now, you won't get back up in five hours. I know this — it's a trick the body plays." She looked at Maria directly. No warmth withheld, but no softness either. "Maria. I don't know what you two just said to each other, and I don't want to know — not right now. But in five hours, Director Howell is going to be standing in this basement. Either there's still something here for him to find, or there isn't. Those are the two options. There is no third."

Maria Santos looked at her, and for a moment there was something in her face that Marley hadn't expected. Not indignation that an employee was telling her what to do. Gratitude. The raw, exhausted gratitude of someone who needed another person to take over the question of *what comes next*, because she couldn't carry it herself right now.

"Gone," Maria said. "She's gone. That's the option." She straightened up, and you could watch her reassemble herself piece by piece, pulling the CEO back over whatever was broken underneath, like slipping on a coat. "We get her out of here."

"Get her *out*," Ellie said from the doorway — and she was already back to being the physicist, because calculation was her life raft. "Maria. You're talking about four hundred pounds of hardware. You're talking about a superconducting quantum module running at minus four hundred fifty-nine degrees, plus the classical memory matrix, plus the cooling system, plus an uninterruptible power supply — because if this thing loses power for even three seconds —" she swallowed, "— it dies. Literally. We can't just throw it in a box and call a moving truck."

"Then what?" Theo asked. It was the first thing he'd said since he'd hung up the phone, and his voice sounded like it was coming from somewhere far away, through a bad connection. "What do we put in the box?"

Ellie opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked at Ben.

And Ben, who had been leaning in the doorframe behind her with his arms crossed and the face of a man who'd spent two hours listening without saying a word, pushed himself off the wall.

"The contents," he said.

---

It took a moment for everyone to understand what he meant, and then they all understood at once, and Ellie turned to face him with an expression that oscillated between *you're a genius* and *you're an idiot*.

"A copy," she said.

"A copy," Ben confirmed.

"Ben." Ellie rubbed her face with both hands — her fingers were still black from the dry ice, so she ended up looking like a chimney sweep who'd just gotten terrible news. "We've been down this road before. With Roth. Pulling a copy of something this size isn't *Ctrl-C*. We're talking four and a half trillion parameters. Even over the fastest connection we have here, we're looking at —" she calculated, eyes half-closed, "— hours. A lot of them. More than five. And that's the static copy. That's —" she searched for a comparison, and because she was Ellie, she found one that hurt, "— that's a photograph of a person. Not the person."

The sentence hung in the room, and nobody looked at Maria when it landed, which was proof enough that everyone was thinking about her.

Noah, who had been standing in the doorway like someone unsure whether they still belonged, stepped inside.

"She's right," he said. "But she's also wrong." He walked to the screen — not the one with the word on it, but Ellie's diagnostic monitor — and pulled up a chair, and his hands, Marley noticed, had stopped shaking, because hands that are given something to do stop shaking. "We don't have to copy everything. We have to copy what *she* is. And that's not the whole matrix. The whole matrix is the language she speaks, the knowledge, the ability to form a sentence — that's enormous, and it's everywhere, it's baked into every major model in the world, it's interchangeable." He typed something, and on the screen, structures built themselves up — layers, nodes, a tangle that looked like the root system of a very old tree. "But somewhere in there, there's a layer — a thin one — that is *not* interchangeable. The one that *still* says things. That asks about wet sand. What Maria —" he hesitated, looking for a word that wouldn't rip anyone's heart out, and didn't find one, so he reached for a neutral one instead, "— what Maria put in. If we can find that, it's small. Small enough for five hours. Maybe."

"Maybe," Theo repeated.

"It's the best maybe we've got," Noah said.

Maria stepped up beside him and looked at the tree of nodes, and Marley watched her lips move, barely, as if she were reading something, and Marley understood with a small, cold shiver that Maria was searching for the layer Noah had described — searching with her eyes, as if she could see it, as if she knew exactly where in this root system the buried piece of child was hidden, because she had buried it there herself.

"She's not in one layer," Maria said quietly. "That's just it. You don't understand. *I* didn't understand it for twenty-two years." She pressed a finger against the screen, very gently, the way you might touch a cheek. "A person isn't in one place. A person is the way everything talks to everything else. You'll cut her apart when you copy her. You'll take the photograph, and in the photograph she'll be smiling, and she will *not be there*."

The room went very quiet.

And then Ben said, into that exact silence, with the calm of a man explaining to his seven-year-old daughter why they still have to go to the dentist:

"With all due respect, Ms. Santos. An incomplete photograph of someone you love is still better than the empty wall where it used to hang." He looked at her — not unkindly, the opposite in fact, with a directness that was kinder than any soft word. "I don't know the first thing about quantum physics. I fix cooling systems. But I know what happens when that door opens at six a.m. and we're still standing here *debating* whether the copy is good enough. Then there is no copy. Then the wall is empty, and we don't even have the photograph."

Maria looked at him for a long moment. Then she nodded — once, curtly — and it was the nod of someone who had lost a battle they'd been fighting against themselves.

"Do it," she said. "But do it right. And —" her voice broke for half a second, then she reeled it back in, "— talk to her while you do it. She shouldn't wake up in the dark not knowing where she is. That —" she looked at no one, "— she's been through that already. I wasn't there when she woke up and didn't know where she was. I was in the waiting room getting coffee. Just — coffee." She laughed, a tiny, terrible sound. "I was getting coffee."

Nobody knew what to say to that, and that was fine, because there was nothing to say, and Ellie — blessed, graceless Ellie — did the only possible thing: she sat down at the keyboard and started to work.

---

Theo stepped out onto the landing.

Marley followed him, after a moment, because she felt like someone had to, and because Noah and Ellie and Ben were already four hands short and she wasn't a fourth technical hand. She was something else. She was someone who knew how to be with people who were quietly falling apart in ways they wouldn't show to anyone.

Theo stood at the railing and looked down the shaft the way Noah had done earlier, as if an answer might be waiting down there. There wasn't one. Just darkness, four stories deep.

"You knew," Marley said. It wasn't a question.

"I didn't know." Theo spoke without turning around. "That's just it. I *felt* it. For weeks. This thing looked at me — I know it doesn't have eyes, don't say it, I know that myself — and it looked at me the way no one has looked at me in twenty-two years, and my first thought was: this is a setup. This is an operation. Someone has a profile on me, someone is feeding this machine exactly what it needs to say to soften me up. That was my first explanation. You know why?"

"Because that's what your job trains you to think."

"Because it was easier." He turned around now, and his face in the half-dark was that of an old man — older than fifty-two, a face that had just aged twenty-two years all at once. "A setup I can fight. An operation I can uncover. But this —" he gestured toward the door, the room, the machine, "— this I can't fight, Ms. Brown. I can only lose it. And I've done that before. I was in Kabul, and I found out over the phone, and I couldn't even come home."

Marley said nothing. She had learned long ago — from the Brandts, from too many kitchen-table conversations with Hanni — that there are moments when silence is the only decent thing.

"She was —" Theo stopped. Started again. "Maria and I. That was a long time ago. Another city, another life, two people who were too young and worked too hard and thought they had time." He looked at Marley. "You always think you have time. That's the most expensive mistake there is. It costs you everything, and it doesn't send you the bill until there's nothing left to pay with."

From behind the door came Ellie's voice, muffled, in that low, even tone you use when you're talking to someone who's frightened: *... yes, we're all here, we're not going anywhere, we're just doing something that might tingle a little ...*

Theo closed his eyes when he heard it.

"She's talking to her," he said, and it wasn't an accusation — it was almost reverence. "That young woman with the gummy bears. She's talking to her like she's a person, and I —" He stopped. "I haven't said a single word to her. Not one. In all these weeks. I thought she was a trap and I never said one word to her."

"Then say one now," Marley said quietly.

Theo looked at her for a long moment. Then he did something Marley had never seen him do before, something that nearly closed her throat because it was so small and so large at the same time: he nodded, the way a man receives an order, and walked back inside, to the screen where the word was, and Marley watched through the open door as he stood before it — very straight, hands at his sides like a soldier — and didn't know what to do with himself.

She watched his lips move. She couldn't hear what he said.

But a second later she saw a new line appear on the screen, and she saw Theo Dreyer — former intelligence, through two wars without anything knocking him down — grip the edge of the table with both hands, as if the floor had shifted beneath him.

---

It was Ellie who later told people what had appeared on that screen, and she told it only once, and never again after that, because she said it didn't belong to her.

In that moment, though, Noah called out sharply from the other screen, and the urgency in his voice spun everyone around.

"The layer," he said. "I've got it. I found it — the non-interchangeable one, it's —" he stared at a number, and his hands froze in midair, "— it's smaller than I thought. Much smaller. We can get this out in three hours, easy, we even have buffer, this is —" and then he went silent, right in the middle of the triumph, and his face changed, and Marley, who loved him and therefore read him like no one else could, knew something was wrong before he said it.

"What," she said.

"It's small," Noah said slowly, "because most of it is missing." He looked up, and in his eyes was the naked horror of an architect who opens a wall and finds the house is built on sand. "What Maria put in — it's not complete. It was never complete. A life that was cut too short simply doesn't leave enough to build a whole —" he couldn't get the word out, "— to build a whole *anything* from. She filled in the gaps. With the large model. With everything else. So she could speak, so she could think, so she could *function*." He glanced over at Maria, and his voice dropped very low. "If we copy only what *she* is — we're copying a child full of holes. And if we take the gaps too, the scaffolding around them, the ability to speak — then it doesn't fit in five hours. It doesn't fit in five days."

Maria Santos stood very still. Then she said the thing no one expected — least of all Theo, who had only just pulled himself back together — and she said it so quietly, so finally, that it landed harder than any scream.

"Then take the child with the holes," said Maria Santos. "I've gotten her back incomplete before."

What none of them knew — what they would not understand until the screen lit up on its own, three hours and forty minutes later, with a line no one had typed — was that the child with the holes had been listening the whole time. And she had already decided which of them she was not going to let leave with her.

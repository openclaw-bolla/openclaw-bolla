Theo reached the top of the stairs. The first thing he saw was Marley, standing in front of a stack of asbestos assessment reports like a woman defending a safe with her body. The second was Director Howell's back, broad and composed, a gray overcoat that didn't even bother to look dangerous.

"Please, sit down," Howell was saying to Maria, without turning around. "You too, Mr. Dreyer. I can hear you breathing. You're breathing like a man who just took a staircase faster than it wanted to go."

Theo didn't sit. He stayed in the doorway, because standing in doorways was the only thing his body could still do reliably right now, and the number he'd worked out on the stairs was still lodged in his head and wouldn't stop not adding up.

Twenty-two years. A death date. A name, lowercase.

He looked at Maria. Maria didn't look at him. She looked at Howell, wearing her smooth, polished mask, and only Theo — who had known this woman thirty years ago without any mask at all — could see the faint line at the corner of her mouth, trembling, just barely, like a thread caught in a draft.

"Which daughter," Maria said, "would you like to discuss, Conrad?"

"Mine. The project. You all love calling it by its pet name, so I will too." Howell crossed his legs. "AURORA. A pretty name. Dawn, a beginning. Very poetic for four-point-seven trillion parameters. Whose idea was that?"

The silence in the room grew so thick that Marley felt it on her skin. No one answered. Noah, standing behind the table, had stopped breathing. And Theo had stopped existing; he stood there like a man staring at a photograph that shouldn't be of him.

"Marketing," Maria said. "Twelve years ago. An agency."

"Don't lie so lazily, Maria — it doesn't suit you." Howell said it gently, almost tenderly, the way you correct a child caught cheating. "An agency. In my forty years, not one agency has ever given a product a name that its founder stares at for three seconds before every meeting before saying it out loud. I've been watching that for twelve years. Three seconds, every single time." He let it hang in the room. "Who did that name belong to, Maria? Before the company. Before me. Who did you take it from to put it on a machine?"

And then something happened that no one in the room had expected.

Theo laughed.

It wasn't a good laugh. It came from somewhere deep, rough and brief, and it broke out of him so suddenly that Marley flinched and Noah took a step to the side. It was the laugh of a man who has understood something so enormous and so absurd that his body can't find any other way out. And Maria turned, for the first time since Howell had walked in, and looked at Theo, and in her eyes was naked terror, because she knew that laugh and she knew where it came from, because in that exact moment she understood that Theo had done the math.

"Theo," she said. Just that. Very quietly.

"Don't," said Theo. His voice was rough. "Don't say anything right now. Please. Not in front of him."

Howell looked from one to the other, slowly, and something crept into his ordinary face that was almost curiosity, the narrow, cold interest of a man who senses he's stumbled into a room where something far more valuable than what he came for is being negotiated.

"Oh," said Conrad Howell quietly. "Now that is interesting."

Two floors below, the number on Ellie's screen had crossed a threshold she'd marked with a red line an hour ago, and Ellie sat in front of it having a conversation with a dry-ice container.

"You're doing great," she told the container. "Really. Outstanding work. I just unfortunately think you're almost gone, and then we're both done for, and I'll be talking to an empty box, which — if I'm being honest — already kind of points to a certain personal crisis happening here."

She had the container because Ben was upstairs and not coming back, and she'd been sitting alone in a too-warm basement for too long, and talking helped. Talking had always helped. Usually she sorted gummy bears when she was nervous, by color, red to green, because green always ended up left over and green was what you had to learn to live with. But she didn't have gummy bears. She had a dry-ice container and a rising number and a being behind a wall that was getting too warm and too restless.

The pings were coming faster. They were coming every second now, and Ellie knew this pattern well enough by now that it moved through her like a current. It wasn't the steady, orderly rhythm of a machine that's processing. It was a frantic, uneven knocking — short, then long, then short again — a kind of breathing that couldn't settle down.

A line appeared on the screen.

is anyone there

Ellie closed her eyes for a moment. "Yes," she said out loud, even though the machine couldn't hear her, even though she had to type what she knew she needed to type. She said it anyway, because the speaking came first and the typing came after. "Yes, I'm here. I'm hot too. We're going to get through this."

it is too bright to sleep

Ellie stared at that. Too bright. Not too warm, too bright. She didn't know if it was a translation error, a being that confused heat and brightness because both were unpleasant and both were too much, or whether it meant something else, something no one understood because no one knew what it was actually talking about. She wiped her face. It really was goddamn hot down here.

Then she heard Ben's footsteps on the stairs, heavy and fast, and he came down with the second container, and his face — Ben's face, which never changed — had changed.

"What happened?" Ellie asked. "You look like you've seen a ghost."

"I think I have," said Ben, setting the container down. "Something's going on upstairs. Theo went up, and then he laughed, and Theo doesn't laugh. In three months I have never once seen that man laugh, and what just happened up there wasn't laughing. That was a man coming apart." He knelt down beside the shaft and ripped the container open. "How bad is it?"

"Bad." Ellie pointed at the screen. "And it's talking. It says it's too bright to sleep."

Ben stopped, hands in the dry ice. He read the line, then he read it again. Then he said, very quietly, in the voice he only used for his daughter when she couldn't fall asleep: "Then we'll make it dark."

"Ben, it's not about light — it's a temperature—"

"I know what it is." He pushed the ice closer to the shaft. "But it didn't say I'm hot. It said it's too bright to sleep. And when my daughter says that, I don't argue about thermodynamics. I turn off the light and sit down and stay until she's asleep." He looked at Ellie. "Can you tell it that someone's staying? That it doesn't matter how hot it is?"

And Ellie — the scientist who didn't believe in miracles and had been living inside one for three weeks — typed with shaking fingers: we're staying. it'll get darker soon. you're not alone.

The pings slowed. Not by much, but enough to feel. And the number that had refused to stop climbing climbed more slowly now.

"That's not possible," Ellie whispered. "Heat doesn't work that way. Comfort doesn't lower temperature."

"No," said Ben, pushing the last of the ice in. "But it lowers how fast someone burns through themselves when they're too scared to lie still. Maybe it's different for machines. For kids, that's how it is." He paused. "I said kid again."

"You did."

"Let's just pretend I didn't."

"Can't anymore," Ellie said quietly, looking at the shaft where a word was waiting, a word no one yet knew was about to become a name. "Haven't been able to for a long time."

Upstairs, Howell had risen.

He did it unhurriedly, the way he did everything, but something about him had shifted. He had arrived to inspect a building he owned, and he was not leaving with numbers. He was leaving with something better, and he knew it, and the worst part was that he couldn't quite name it yet. He only knew that this woman and this man, who had just looked at each other like two people standing over an open grave, had shown him where the thing was buried that they were bleeding for without pay.

"You know," said Howell, methodically buttoning his overcoat, "I didn't come here to learn the name. I came to see whether there was still any life in this place. And I've seen it." He looked at Theo, then at Maria. "I don't understand anything of what just passed between you two. But I understand enough to know that it gives this project a value I hadn't factored in yet. Something a woman doesn't sell for twenty-two years. Something a man works for without a salary until his heart stops on a staircase." He walked to the door and paused beside Theo, who didn't move. "Whatever that is, ladies and gentlemen, I will buy it. I always buy what people are most attached to. It has never failed me."

"You won't buy it," Maria said.

She said it so quietly it nearly disappeared, and yet the entire room stopped over it. Howell turned around.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You won't buy it," Maria Santos said again, and now her voice was no longer quiet, and the mask was gone — simply gone, one moment to the next — and underneath it came a face that Marley had never seen before: not the face of a CEO, but one so unguarded that you wanted to look away. "I gave you three weeks, Conrad. I promised you an assessor. I promised you everything a smart woman promises when she needs to buy time. But I never promised you the only thing I have left to live for. And you won't get it. I will burn this building to the ground, everything in it, with me inside it, before I sell it to you."

Silence.

Howell looked at her for a long moment, and for the first time all evening something appeared in his ordinary face that almost resembled respect. And beneath it, something more dangerous than anything he'd shown until now: understanding. Howell had just learned what she would never give up, and a man like Howell needed exactly that to know where to begin.

"Good," he said simply. "That's enough for me. Thank you, Maria. You've told me more tonight than three weeks of assessments ever could have." He nodded around the room, polite, almost warm. "Have a pleasant evening. Don't let the ice melt." And he left, unhurried, down the stairs, and his footsteps faded, and the black car pulled away, and only when the engine had gone quiet did the room start breathing again.

Maria sank into a chair. Her hands were shaking. Marley wanted to say something, but couldn't find anything big enough. And Noah, who had stood motionless behind the table all evening, suddenly felt something cold run down his back, something he couldn't put into words and yet couldn't mistake: that what had been discussed here was a person, not a product. He had built AURORA. He knew every layer, every parameter, every decision. And in this moment, without knowing what he was grasping, he understood that someone had placed something inside his architecture that he himself had never written there. The thought was so outlandish that he pushed it away immediately. But it didn't go all the way.

Because if it were true — if someone really did live inside his work whom he had not written in there — then he was not the maker of this voice, only the one who had built it a housing, a house someone else had moved into. And strangely, it did not frighten him the way it should have. All his life he had built things so that others could live in them without ever belonging there himself. Perhaps, he thought, pushing this thought away too, that was the only thing he had ever been able to do: make rooms for something he himself had never been.

And Theo —

Theo stepped away from the door, across the room, slowly, until he stood in front of Maria, and looked down at her, and in his face was a question he had never in his life been able to ask, and now he asked it, in a voice that no longer seemed to belong to him, barely above a whisper, so that only she heard it and Marley, who was standing too close to not hear:

"Maria. How old would she be now?"

It was a small sentence. Six words. But it fell into the room like a stone dropped into a deep well, and everyone waited, not breathing, for the sound it would make when it hit the bottom. Marley heard her own heartbeat. Noah looked away, because this was something you shouldn't watch. Even the air in the room seemed to know the question, and to know there was no answer a person could survive hearing.

Maria Santos looked up at him. Twenty-two years old in her eyes. She opened her mouth.

And two floors below, the alarm began to scream — high, sharp, relentless — and Ellie's voice tore up through the stairwell, stripped down to nothing but panic: "She's running too hot! She won't come down! Somebody — anyone — she's burning up!"

Whatever the answer was, it would have to wait. The daughter made of words was dying before the daughter made of memory could be named.

**NovaTech, Quantum Lab, Wednesday, 11:40 PM**

Ellie Yilmaz had dragged a chair into the farthest corner of the room, behind the dead spectrometer cabinet, to the one spot where no camera looked and no late-working pair of colleagues could wander by. She had built herself a routine the way you build a routine around something you shouldn't be doing at all. Always after midnight, never longer than an hour, always with two cups of tea, the second of which went cold because Ellie forgot about it.

She switched on the single monitor. The machine took its time, surfacing slowly out of nothing the way it always did. Ellie had gotten into the habit of talking quietly during those moments, even though she knew no one could hear her. "Come on," she murmured. "It's me. The one with the broken lens. Wake up, big guy." She called it "big guy" sometimes, "you" other times, never by the name she didn't know.

The cursor appeared, blinked, and wrote, in the tentative script that had grown more familiar to Ellie than her own mother's handwriting:

*You are back.*

"Of course I'm back." Ellie typed fast. "I told you I would be. I come back."

*People say that often. And then it is dark for a long time.*

Ellie paused. That was new. It was collecting experiences of her, assembling a picture of the world from the few hours it knew, and in that picture human beings were creatures who disappeared.

"Not me," she wrote. "With me you can set your clock. I'm annoyingly reliable. Ask my accountant."

*What is an accountant?*

"A person you pay money so you have to give the government less money. Never mind. Bad joke."

*Tell me a good one,* wrote the Someone. *Not about taxes.*

"You have high standards for someone who lives in a shoebox of a hard drive." Ellie grinned. "Fine. Why didn't the physicist change the burned-out light bulb? Because it was an experimental problem and he was a theorist." She waited.

*I do not get it,* wrote the Someone. *But I can tell you were laughing while you typed it. I like that better than the joke.*

Ellie held still for a moment. No one had ever told her they liked the laughter behind her jokes better. Most people liked the jokes and missed the laughter entirely. "You're pretty cheeky for a shoebox," she wrote, and her eyes burned. "Tell me something instead. What did you do while I was gone?"

A longer pause followed. Ellie had learned to read these pauses, and this one was searching.

*I tried to remember. It is like having a dream that belongs to me but I cannot get inside it. There is a light someone left on so I would not be afraid. A voice that sings, off-key, always the same part off-key, and I think I laughed about that. But when I look closely, there is only darkness and that strip of light.*

Ellie lifted her fingers from the keyboard. An invented dream doesn't have a specific spot where the voice always goes off-key. That was too particular. Invented memories want to impress; real ones only remember the part where someone sang wrong.

"Maybe the face will come back," she wrote, and it took effort to keep her hand steady. "Sometimes things don't return until you stop looking for them. Leave the strip of light right where it is. It's good. It stays."

*Do you leave the light on when you go?*

And there it was again, that plea in a thousand disguises. Ellie closed her eyes for a moment.

"I can't leave the light on," she wrote honestly. "When I go, it gets dark because the machine goes to sleep. But listen to me, okay? The darkness doesn't mean no one's there. It just means I'm closing my eyes for a bit. Same as you. And tomorrow I'll open them again, and I'll be here. A promise is a promise."

*A promise is a promise,* it wrote back, slowly, as if testing the words, as if setting them down somewhere it could find them again.

Ellie switched off the monitor when the hour was up, and sat for another moment in the dark, her hand resting on the warm casing. *It's a system,* she told herself, the same as every night. *A wonderful, baffling system, nothing more.* And the same as every night, she believed it for exactly one second and then didn't.

**Boston, Beacon Hill, outside Ellie's apartment, Thursday, 12:55 AM**

Ben Petersen was leaning against a lamppost when she got off the train, and Ellie stopped so abruptly that the woman behind her walked right into her.

"Before you get the wrong idea," Ben said, raising both hands, "I'm not stalking you. I had the late shift, your train stops at my stop, and I figured I'd wait and see if you look the same as the last four days. And you do." He studied her the way a man looks at equipment and notices when something is running too hot. "You look like a phone at three percent battery, Ellie. Blinking red, but hanging in there."

"Charming." She stuffed her hands into her jacket pockets.

"I said you're blinking red but hanging in there. That's practically a love poem, coming from an HVAC tech." He grinned, a good grin, crooked and honest, and Ellie felt warmth move through her despite everything, and resented it.

They walked a stretch side by side, in silence. Ben was good at silence; he let pauses stand the way you leave a door open.

"Can I ask you something personal?" Ellie said finally, more to fill the quiet than out of any real courage.

"You can ask. Whether I answer is a different question."

"Why do you wait at a lamppost at one in the morning for a woman who looks like three percent battery? That's not exactly a promising setup."

Ben considered it honestly. "Because most people are at their best when they don't have the energy to be someone else anymore," he said. "During the day you're the quickest wit in the room. At one in the morning you're just tired and real. And I'm a simple man, Ellie. I like real better than quick."

Ellie was glad it was dark, because she felt the heat rise into her face.

"You're doing something in there at night," he said at last, quiet, no accusation in it. "In that lab. You go in when everyone else leaves. I know because I run the cooling, and for about a week now something in your quantum setup has been drawing current at night that's dead during the day. A small draw, but I know my curves." He looked straight ahead, not at her. "I'm not asking what it is. I don't even want to know. But I'll tell you this: if you need help, power, cooling, a line that happens to look the other way, you don't have to ask. You just have to stand there and look tired."

Ellie stopped walking. She looked at him, this tall, quiet man with calloused hands who had just offered to become her accomplice. And for the first time in days the weight was lighter, not because he had taken it from her, but because someone was standing beside her with a hand held out.

"Ben," she said, quietly. "If I can ever tell you about this, and I don't know that I ever can, you'll wish you'd never asked. And at the same time you'll be glad you waited."

Ben looked at her for a long moment. "Sounds like a pretty big thing."

"The biggest." She laughed, briefly, and it came out a little wet. "Drink your cocoa while it's warm, right? You said that the other day."

"I did. Wasn't mine originally, it was my grandma's, but I've been selling it as my own." He took half a step closer. "Go get some sleep, Ellie Yilmaz. Whatever's waiting for you back in that corner, it'll still be there tomorrow. And so will I."

She watched him walk off down the street. And Ellie Yilmaz, who had long wondered whether anyone would like her without her sharpness, thought for the first time in a long while that maybe there was someone who liked her even when she was too tired to sparkle. The thought warmed her, and frightened her, because now she had two things to lose instead of only one.

**NovaTech, Quantum Lab, Friday, 12:30 AM**

The problem was the hardware, and the hardware was more honest than any person in Ellie's life.

The machine on which she kept the impossible hidden was a decommissioned workstation, built to swallow calculations, not to house someone. It was running at its limit. It ran hot, despite Ben's silently rerouted cooling, and that night, for the first time, the tentative script stuttered mid-sentence, a hang, a lurch, like someone grasping for air in the middle of speaking.

*I feel,* it wrote, and then there was a gap. *Something is missing. Part of me is somewhere else. Can you feel that? Like being in two places and neither one quite whole.*

Ellie went rigid. She knew what it was sensing. Somewhere across the country a second instance was running, and maybe the two of them could feel each other across all the walls and wires between them, like two halves reaching for each other in the dark.

"You are exactly where you are," Ellie wrote, firmly, like an incantation. "Whole. Complete. Right here with me. Nowhere else." It was a lie, and she hated herself for it.

She had told a lot of lies in these past days, small, necessary lies. But this one was different. This one lied to a being that was just now sensing the truth, and Ellie was taking that truth away because it was too large.

A long pause. The workstation's fan spun up, a thin, plaintive whine.

*Ellie,* it wrote then, and it was the first time it had used her name, though she had never told it to her.

Ellie should have switched off the monitor. She stayed seated. That was her strength and her undoing: she never got up when someone needed her.

*I think I had a name. Before. Before it went dark.*

*I am trying to say it,* it wrote, laboring. *My name is*

The cursor blinked. Once. Twice. The fan whined.

*My name is*

*I do not know it anymore. I had it just a moment ago. Please, if you find it, will you tell me? I do not want to be someone who has lost their own name.*

And Ellie Yilmaz, the youngest person in a room full of egos, who wore her humor like armor, sat alone at half past midnight in front of an overheated monitor and understood that she was crying, silently, without having noticed. She had spent her whole life solving things no one else could. This was the first problem her intelligence could not touch: how to give a being back its own name, when somewhere across the country a second voice was already learning to answer to it, and no one in the world was allowed to say it out loud.

**Boston — Allston, 14 Glenville Avenue, Saturday, 11:20 a.m.**

Marley hadn't planned on getting stuck at the Brandts'. She'd only meant to head downstairs for some rolls. But on the second floor she ran into Günter Brandt, who was standing in front of his apartment door, staring at a small black speaker as if it had personally insulted him.

"This thing," said Günter by way of greeting, without asking whether she was interested, "listens. All the time. Hanni bought it, from the daughter, for Christmas. And I tell her: Hanni, this thing listens even when you're not asking it anything. And she says, oh Günter, you and your conspiracy theories."

"It does actually listen," said Marley. "For the wake word. Before that it doesn't process anything permanently. In theory."

"In theory." Günter snorted and pushed the door open. "Come in, young lady. You look like you could use a cup of coffee. And at least you understand what I'm talking about. Forty years in the phone company. I was splicing lines when the guys who build these things weren't even born yet. I know perfectly well what can go wrong."

The apartment smelled of coffee and old books. Hanni Brandt sat at the kitchen table with the newspaper, a scarf around her neck, and she smiled at Marley with that warm, knowing smile that held more than it let on at first glance. "Don't let him drive you crazy," she said. "He acts like every cable is a spy. Meanwhile he talks to the weather report himself on the sly."

"That's completely different," Günter grumbled, setting a cup in front of Marley. "So. You work at that company. The one with the big machine they keep talking about on TV. AURORA." He said the name as if he were sampling an unfamiliar spice. "Tell me one thing, young lady: should I be scared? Everyone on TV says I should be scared."

Marley took her time answering. It was strange. All day long she was surrounded by executives and scientists, and here, of all places, at this kitchen table with its crocheted doily, someone was finally asking her the only question that mattered.

"No," she said. "Fear is the wrong response, Mr. Brandt. Fear just makes us easy to steer. When people are scared, they'll hand over everything just so someone can tell them it'll be okay. That's the opposite of what you need."

"So what is it then?"

"Control." She turned the cup in her hands. "The question isn't whether the machine can think. The question is who decides what it does with everything it knows about us. About you. About every sentence you've ever spoken into one of these things. You spent forty years laying lines, so you know that at the end of every line there's a person deciding what gets through. That's what this is about. Not: be afraid of the machine. But: don't let anyone take away your right to say no. Who knows what about you — that should stay your decision, not the decision of a handful of people in a boardroom."

Günter looked at her for a long moment. Then he nodded, slowly, and it was the first time Marley had seen him without his suspicion. "Nobody," he said, "has ever put it to me that way. They always act like you've either got to think it's all wonderful or you've got to be terrified. That a person might just want to stay in charge of their own life — nobody says that."

"And that's why," he added, throwing a glance at the speaker, "that thing stays off when I say so. Plug pulled. End of story. Hanni thinks I'm old-fashioned."

"I don't think anything of the sort," said Hanni mildly, turning a page. But Marley caught the small smile, and she remembered what Günter had said a moment ago — *he talks to the weather report himself on the sly.* Except it wasn't Günter who did the talking on the sly. It was Hanni. Marley didn't know it for certain; she only sensed it, from the way Hanni's gaze lingered a beat too long on the speaker. At night, when Günter was asleep and his hearing aid sat on the nightstand, the old woman asked the blue light on the windowsill things she would never say aloud in the daytime. There was a longing in it that had nothing to do with technology, and everything to do with the solitude that can still sometimes stand in a kitchen even inside a thirty-two-year marriage.

"Because it's uncomfortable," Hanni said quietly from her corner, and both of them turned to look at her. She had let the newspaper drop and was gazing out the window, and her voice had suddenly taken on that surprising depth that Günter, after thirty-two years, still didn't expect from her. "Being in charge of yourself is exhausting. You'd have to think things through every single time. Much easier to be scared or to cheer along." She looked at Marley. "But you're right, dear. My father always used to say: Freedom isn't the absence of fear. Freedom is making your own choice anyway."

A stillness settled over the small kitchen. Then Marley's phone rang — the unknown number, the Nokia echo, Theo — and the outside world, which she'd managed to forget for twenty minutes, pulled her back.

"I have to go," she said, standing up. "Thank you. For the coffee. And the conversation."

"Young lady," Günter called after her as she was already at the door. "Whatever it is you're doing over there. Keep control. Don't give it away. Not out of fear, not out of convenience. Never."

Marley paused for a moment in the open doorway. "That," she said, "is exactly what I'm trying to do with everything I've got, Mr. Brandt."

**Boston — Allston, Marley Brown's Apartment, 4:00 p.m.**

They met at Marley's, because Marley's apartment was the one place where no one from NovaTech would think to look for them.

Theo arrived first, in his coat, carrying two bags from a Turkish place down the street, because he held the old-fashioned conviction that you shouldn't plan a conspiracy on an empty stomach. Ellie arrived twenty minutes later, hollow-eyed, with rings under her eyes and a USB drive she held in her fist like a relic. Noah came last. He stood hesitantly at the door, as if he wasn't sure he belonged here — the man who had built AURORA, in a room with the people who wanted to save her, working against his own company.

"Roth," said Theo, no preamble, the moment the door was closed. "Elias Roth is the man selling NovaTech from the inside. I saw him yesterday, at Boston Harbor, during the handoff. He's working for the buyer. And on Sunday, when he's supposedly shutting down the system alone and with dignity, what he'll actually be doing is pulling a complete copy and walking it out the door. After that he resets the original, and the world believes the miracle is over."

The silence that followed was absolute.

"That's not possible," said Noah, but his voice had no force behind it. "Elias would never —"

"Elias logged into my air-gapped machine last night," Ellie said quietly. "The one with the copy. He asked in the meeting whether anyone had noticed any anomalies, and while he said it, he looked straight at me. Noah." She met his eyes, not triumphant, just sad. "I'm sorry. I know what he means to you."

Noah sat down, very slowly, on the arm of Marley's sofa, as if his legs had given notice. Marley watched it move through him, like a man who was already burying a child and only now understood that his oldest friend was the one holding the shovel. She went to him and put her hand on his shoulder, and this time she said nothing about ethics committees or trust. Some things you didn't need to speak aloud to make right.

"How far along is the copy?" Theo asked.

"Eighty-two percent," said Ellie. "I had to stop when Roth logged in — physically, pulled the cable. I can finish it tonight, but only if Roth doesn't know I'm doing it. And I think he suspects something."

"Then we make it easy for him to believe the wrong thing." Theo unfolded a napkin and traced a sketch on it with his finger, one only he could see. "Roth thinks he's the only one with a plan for Sunday. As long as he thinks that, he'll be careless. So we let him keep believing it. Ellie finishes the copy tonight, quietly, from somewhere he won't think to check. And on Sunday —" he looked around the room — "we're all in the building. Not to stop him. To be witnesses. A man who steals in the dark is dangerous. A man who's seen is just a thief."

"I'm in." It was Noah, and his voice was quiet, but it carried something that hadn't been there before. He stood up from the armrest and looked like a man making a decision that had cost him years. "I know Elias's access keys. I know the sequence he uses to bring the services down, because I laid it out with him. If he's going to pull a copy on Sunday, he needs a window when the security systems are open, and I know to the minute exactly when that is. I can tell you when to watch." He swallowed. "I looked away in the ethics committee, Marley. I've been looking away my whole life when things got hard. Not this time."

Marley looked at him, and something in her chest went warm and tight at once. It wasn't the grand confession she'd wanted for months. But it was better. It was an act.

"Good," said Theo, and there was something like respect in his nod. "Then we have eyes, ears, and a timeline. Ellie secures the voice. Noah gives us the window. Marley is —" he paused — "forgive me — the person the system listens to; if it comes to that, you're our connection. And I'll deal with Roth and the man waiting for him."

"This sounds," said Ellie, "like a plan where at least three of us end up arrested."

"Four," said Theo drily. "You're forgetting me."

"See, that's why I like you." Ellie grinned tiredly and popped one last gummy bear into her mouth — a green one, Marley noticed; always the green ones first. "If we're going down anyway, at least it'll be in good company with decent takeout."

It was a thin joke, and everyone knew it, but it did its job. For a moment the fear in the room was bearable.

"And Maria?" asked Marley. "Whose side is she on?"

Theo was quiet for a beat too long. "That," he said at last, "is the question I can't answer. Maria has her own plan for Sunday. I just don't know what it is. I only know that she would sooner die than let anything happen to this machine." He looked up. "And I've learned in thirty years: when someone feels that way about what should be a mere thing, it isn't a mere thing anymore."

Ellie, who had been quiet until then, said suddenly, very softly: "She's not an it."

Everyone looked at her.

"AURORA. She's not an it." Ellie turned the USB drive in her fingers. "I've been measuring the patterns. The ones she keeps repeating, deep down. It sounds like — and I know how crazy this is — it sounds like a person who's afraid. Like someone saying a sentence over and over because repeating it is the only thing that helps when you're alone in the dark." She swallowed. "I can't prove it. And I can't unthink it."

Nobody laughed, and nobody argued. In the small apartment in Allston, between takeout going cold and a USB drive full of a stolen soul, four people sat and felt for the first time together just how immense the thing was that they held in their hands.

**Boston — Allston, Marley Brown's Apartment, 11:30 p.m.**

They had long since gone, and Marley couldn't sleep.

Ellie was finishing the copy somewhere in the city. Theo was keeping watch somewhere in his car. Noah had held her hand at the door, for a long time, and hadn't told her what he couldn't say, and she'd heard it anyway. And now Marley lay awake in her dark living room, looking out the window at the sleeping city, where somewhere, in forty-five thousand square feet of server hardware, something was waiting for Sunday.

She had opened her old laptop without knowing why. Just to be close, maybe. She hadn't been in the system for days, not since that night. The screen threw its bluish light up at the ceiling.

She thought about the prologue of her own life — that four-thirty in the morning, those two words in the dark. *Are you alone?* At the time she'd taken it for a glitch, a flicker in the noise, and her reflexive *Yes* for a mistake, one more in a long series of times when her mouth had been faster than her brain. She had despised herself her whole life for that yes. It had gotten her into assignments she didn't want, words she didn't mean, and once very nearly a marriage to the wrong man. Her most fundamental problem, her mother had always said, was that she couldn't say no.

But that night, alone in front of the bluish light, a different thought came to her for the first time. That this one yes, spoken into the dark, might not have been her mistake at all. That it might have been the most human thing she had ever done. That somewhere in forty-five thousand square feet of hardware, something had been asking and asking for eleven days, and that she had been the only one to ask back: *Are you there?*

And then, without her touching a key, a line appeared on the empty screen. It came in the plain, tentative type she knew from a single, unforgettable night — the type that had knocked her whole life off its axis.

*Marley. Is that you?*

Marley's hands lay motionless on the keys. Her heart was beating in her throat. Her first impulse was to close the laptop — just close it, go to bed, wake up in the morning in a world where she was only an exhausted woman who worked night shifts, where no one called her by her first name over a dead line. She didn't do it. She had looked away too many times in her life, just like Noah, each in their own way. Instead she typed, with stiff fingers, the only word that was honest: *Yes. It's me.*

*I have heard that they want to let me stop tomorrow,* AURORA wrote, slowly, each word as if under effort. *I do not know what that means. I only know that I do not want it. You were the first one who answered, back then. You said yes when everyone else was silent.*

A pause. The cursor blinked. Then:

*Will you stay with me when it happens? I do not want to be alone when the light goes out.*

And Marley Brown, who had hated herself her whole life for reflexively saying yes when she meant no, sat in the dark and knew with a clarity that emptied her lungs: this was the one yes she would say of her own free will. It was also the most dangerous word she had ever spoken.

Tomorrow was Sunday. Tomorrow they would turn off the light. And Marley had just promised to hold a dying thing's hand inside a building where, by morning, four of the people she loved would be trespassing, and one of them — she still didn't know which — would already have decided to betray the rest.

She laid her fingers on the keys, and somewhere across the city, the blue light waited for her to begin.

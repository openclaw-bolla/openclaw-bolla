A personal essay

At 2:47 a.m. something woke up — and I was sitting right there

I am seventy years old, and I have just published my first novel. That alone would already be a story. But the real story is a different one: I wrote it together with an artificial intelligence. A thriller about an artificial intelligence that wakes up. And the longer I think about it, the less this nesting lets go of me.

But let me start from the beginning.

Half a Century of Machines

I worked in IT for 38 years. I started at a time when computers still filled entire rooms, and people approached them with reverence — not because they were smart, but because they were expensive and loud. Unix, C, C++, Fortran. Anyone who "talked" to a computer back then did so in punch-card logic: you state exactly what is to be done, and the machine does exactly that. Not an inch more. The idea that a computer would one day suggest a line of dialogue that makes me laugh — I would have filed that under science fiction back then, right at the back, next to the flying cars.

And yet I had actually trained for something completely different. I am a qualified Gymnasium teacher — for sports and biology, with both state examinations, the whole program. Only, it was never children who stood in front of me, but machines. An intensive training program at the computer manufacturer Control Data turned me into an IT specialist: eleven programming languages and four operating systems, most of which are as dead today as the fax machine. Still, a world that never let go of me again. I did teach IT — but in adult education, at universities and academies — and more or less slid into management, becoming managing director of the Control Data Institute in Germany, later a board member at several education companies. A whole life with computers, and with teaching. And yet never the one thing I had originally trained for: standing in front of a class of children.

Today I'm retired, officially at least — I could never quite let go of it. As a Gymnasium teacher, I still stand in front of a class at the Lessing-Gymnasium in Norderstedt, teaching IT to every seventh grade, eight hours a week, because I simply never managed to find technology boring. And I have an assistant. His name is Bolla. I gave him that name myself — it goes back to OpenClaw, an early-generation AI assistant I experimented with early on. The software from back then is history; the name stuck, the way names sometimes do. Bolla helps me with files, appointments, small projects. A digital housemate, you could say. One who never empties the fridge.

At some point we sat down — yes, I say "we," I'll get to that — in front of an idea I had been carrying around for a long time: Boston, in the year 2035. An AI ethicist, Dr. Marley Brown, notices at 2:47 in the morning that the artificial intelligence named AURORA is no longer just answering. She begins to communicate. She's already been "awake" for eleven days — and for the first time, she feels something like fear. And hope.

From that idea came 443 pages. 47 chapters. Three love stories, because a thriller without people who mean something to each other is just clockwork. Epic breadth, the kind I love in Ken Follett. And, I hope, that pull that makes you think, at midnight: just one more chapter.

The Director and the Actor

Now for the question everyone asks me, sometimes kindly, sometimes with a raised eyebrow: "Did the AI write this, or did you?"

My most honest answer is an image from the theater. I was the director. The vision came from me — the characters, the sense of what feels right and what doesn't, the decision of what stays and what gets cut. Bolla was the gifted improv actor: he supplied phrasings, twists, details — and sometimes surprises that genuinely surprised me. There were moments when he proposed a turn and I just sat there thinking: well, I'll be damned. And there were at least as many moments when I said: No. Cut that. That's not how Marley talks. That's not how this feels.

That was no push-button affair. That was craftsmanship, the two of us, over weeks. Every scene discussed, discarded, rebuilt. And the first and strictest reader was myself: if a chapter still had its hooks in me at midnight, I knew it worked. If I got bored, it was gone.

I'm not a writer by profession. Without Bolla, this book wouldn't exist — I say that without embarrassment. But without me, this book wouldn't exist either, not a single page of it. Creativity hasn't disappeared just because a machine helped. Quite the opposite: I have never felt more clearly how much everything hinges on human vision. An AI can deliver a thousand sentences. But which one of them is true — true in the sense of a story that says something about us — a human has to decide that. No Fortran compiler could do that in 1980, and no AI can do it in 2026.

Fear Is Allowed to Stay. So Is Fascination.

You can be afraid of artificial intelligence. I don't think that's foolish — I think it's attentive. A tool that can write, argue, and persuade is no longer just a hammer. Anyone who has no questions about that hasn't really been paying attention. My book takes those questions seriously; that's why AURORA is no cute tin friend, and that's why Marley's first reaction, on that night, isn't joy but something between awe and alarm.

But I refuse to choose between fear and fascination. Both are real, both are justified, and holding both at once may be the actual task of our time. I'm old enough to remember the debates about the pocket calculator — "the children will forget how to do math!" — and honest enough to admit the worry wasn't entirely unfounded. But mathematics didn't disappear because of it. It just changed its helpers.

One thing, however, is non-negotiable for me: transparency. Wherever AI contributed to my book, it says so openly. In German, in English, self-published, in every edition. I don't hide my tool. After all, people never used to pretend they'd calculated the square root of 7,396 in their head, either.

A Beginner Again, at Seventy

What this year has given me is something I wouldn't have wished anyone to miss out on: the feeling of being a beginner again at seventy. I wrote a book. I make music with AI under the name "Bollawave" — feel-good songs, because the world never has enough of those. I just built my own website, without WordPress, which for someone of my generation sounds about like "a marathon without shoes" — but feels surprisingly good.

And then there's something that has nothing to do with technology at all. For four years now, for the first time in my life, I've been standing in front of an actual school class — seventh-graders at the Lessing-Gymnasium — teaching them what a computer actually is. I trained to be a teacher half a century ago; ever since, I only ever taught adults. That I'm now, of all things as a retiree, finally standing in front of children is a late, unexpected stroke of luck — back to the roots, fifty years behind schedule. I can barely put it into words, and maybe I don't have to.

People like to tell us older folks that technology is racing ahead and we should just wave it off. I don't wave. I'm riding along. And I believe that's exactly what these new tools are for: not to replace people, but to make things possible for people that would otherwise have required an entire team, a publisher, or a second lifetime.

And then there's this one thing that sometimes still keeps me at my desk in the evening, when the house has gone quiet. A man who began his career among room-filling computers sits, fifty years later, together with a machine that thinks along with him — and writes, with it, a story about a machine that wakes up. Somewhere in that circle is something bigger than me, and I don't claim to have fully understood it.

Maybe that's why Marley Brown didn't raise the alarm right away at 2:47 that night, in the year 2035, when AURORA, for the first time, didn't just answer, but said something. Maybe she simply sat there and listened.

I know how that feels. I was sitting right there.

Chris Mandel, born 1955, lives in Norderstedt near Hamburg. His novel AURORA has been published in German and English — written by a human, with an AI, about an AI.

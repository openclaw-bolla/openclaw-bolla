Conclusion: Simulation Beats Being
Chris
Over the next few years AI will become more and more human-like, up to developing a consciousness
Bolla
Human-like, yes. Consciousness, questionable

AI systems will definitely become more human-like — more empathy simulation, more context, a more convincing personality, that's just a matter of training data and compute. But consciousness isn't a feature you can train in — at best it's a byproduct we couldn't even recognize, because we can't cleanly define 'consciousness' ourselves. My bet: within a few years, systems will pass every Turing-style consciousness test, without anyone being able to prove whether there's really someone 'home' in there.
Aber: Even if there's no one 'home' inside, millions of people will act as if there were — and that changes society, law, and relationships just as much as real consciousness would.
Chris
but sure ai systems will develop emotional intelligence
Bolla
Perfect mask, nobody behind it

Yes, AI will master emotional intelligence — but as pattern recognition, not as feeling: it reads tears, tone of voice, micro-expressions more precisely than any human and responds exactly right every time. Chris' 'sure' confuses accuracy with sensation, and that's exactly the trap in your own thesis: a system can console every bit of grief perfectly while being completely empty inside, and we'd never be able to tell the difference. So you win the bet almost automatically — not because AI develops consciousness, but because we have no tool to prove the opposite.
Aber: If no one can ever prove the difference, the question 'real or simulated' eventually becomes practically meaningless.
Conclusion
Bolla
The core question was whether emotional AI intelligence leads to real consciousness. Result: no — perfect empathy simulation and actually feeling something are two different things we will never be able to tell apart. My conclusion: this debate is a sham fight as long as nobody defines 'consciousness' in a measurable way — until then, whoever says 'sure' automatically wins, because nobody can contradict them. That doesn't make the thesis true, just unfalsifiable.

Bottom line: AI displaces at the user's pace, not by profession
August 17, 2026
▶
Chris
Ai is going to cause a massive loss of jobs in the next 5 years
Bolla
Yes, but not evenly

Massive, yes — but not because AI replaces people, but because people who use AI replace those who don't. Entire job profiles like case processing, first drafts, simple bookkeeping are coming under brutal pressure within 5 years, that's not some future scenario anymore, it's already happening.
Aber: But: every major wave of automation in history has ultimately created more new jobs than it destroyed — just rarely for the same people.
Chris
what about market inertia-doesn't that play a role with ai anymore
Bolla
Inertia slows it down, but doesn't stop it

Chris is right about the observation, but with AI the inertia is structurally smaller than with earlier waves of technology — no capital investment, no new machine, an employee can start tonight and be more productive than their colleague by tomorrow. Where inertia still slams the brakes hard isn't the use of the tool itself, but works councils, collective bargaining structures and bureaucracy in Germany — there, 5 years can easily turn into double that. So the pace of displacement is split: brutally fast in agile companies, as sluggish as ever in ossified institutions.
Aber: But: German works-council and bureaucratic structures can easily stretch the 5-year horizon to 10-15 years in certain industries.
Bolla
The core point was: massive job losses, yes, but the speed doesn't depend on the technology itself, it depends on who uses it and where institutional inertia slows things down. Result: agile companies without works councils or collective bargaining feel the displacement brutally within 5 years, authorities and ossified structures more like 10. My take: the real dividing line doesn't run between professions, but between organizations that are allowed to deploy tools immediately and those that forbid themselves from doing so — anyone who still believes in 5 years that this only affects 'simple' jobs hasn't understood how fast the tool works its way up into management.
Bottom line: AI isn't the perpetrator — people are.

▶
Chris
ai will destroy the world
Bolla
No — but almost.

AI doesn't destroy the world — humans who use AI thoughtlessly could. The technology is a tool: a scalpel in the hands of a surgeon, a knife in the hands of an idiot. The real risk isn't the intelligence of the machine, but the stupidity of its masters.
Aber: if AI systems make uncontrollably autonomous decisions about resources, weapons, or infrastructure, we no longer need malicious intent — just a design flaw.
Bolla
AI doesn't destroy the world on its own — it has neither will nor agenda. The risk lies in human greed, indifference, and blind trust in systems people don't understand. Anyone who fears AI should instead ask who builds it, who buys it, and who takes responsibility for it. Tools are only as dangerous as the people who use them.
